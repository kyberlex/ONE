## **Item 1. Adults who cannot consent or decide** *(SIM-QA-36 D-36.4; SIM-QA-39 D-39.7, D-39.8)*

**Current text, Art. 1.2.1:**
> 1. Every human being possesses absolute, unconditional sovereignty over their biological, physical, and cognitive substrate.

**Draft B (Art. 1.2.2), not merged:**
> 2. Genetic alteration, biometric tracking, neurological monitoring, and pharmacological manipulation without the person's free consent, and physical subjugation, are permanently prohibited as structural violations of personal integrity.

**Draft C (Art. 1.2.5), last sentences, not merged:**
> Law may narrow these cases and never widen them. Any other holding of a person is physical subjugation.

**Problem.** A person who is unconscious, or who has lost the capacity to decide (advanced dementia, for example), cannot give "free consent" and is not in any case of Draft C. On the drafts as they stand, such a person can be given no medicine and cannot be kept from walking into danger. The canon names nobody who decides for them.

| # | Question | Options | Red team |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1a** | Who decides for an adult who cannot | **A** the person they named in advance (SIM-QA-36 point 4), following what they said they wanted; failing that, those who live with them; failing that, a person drawn by lot. **B** next of kin, by rank. **C** nobody is named; a review jury decides case by case. | A |
| **1b** | Treatment when nobody can be asked in time | A clinician may give what is needed to save life or prevent grave harm, unless a refusal by the person is known: **yes** or **no**. | yes |
| **1c** | The person said one thing in advance and refuses now (D-39.7) | **A** the advance statement prevails once incapacity is found. **B** the present refusal always prevails. **C** the present refusal prevails for anything done to the body (no medication is ever forced); the advance statement governs everything else. | C |
| **1d** | Keeping such a person from harm | **A** only for the moment of danger, by anyone; no continuing restriction at all. **B** for the moment of danger by anyone; a continuing restriction only after a jury drawn from outside finds that the person cannot judge the danger, with periodic review, the least restriction that works, and an Advocate for the person. **C** left to those who care for the person. | B |
| **1e** | The rest of the subject (who assesses capacity, how often, care homes) | **A** a new docket, for Organic Law detail; Chapter I takes only the clauses that follow from 1a to 1d. **B** attach it to SIM-QA-36. | A |

*Why B on 1d and not C.* "Care" without a finding and a review is how people are shut away for being old, inconvenient or odd. The same guard that Chapter I gives a violent person (a drawn jury, a review) is the least a person without capacity should have.
*Why C on 1c.* It agrees with the decision already taken on the protective hold ("no medication imposed"). Its cost is real: a person who wrote, when well, "medicate me if I am in crisis" is not medicated if they refuse in the crisis. A specialist should read this before it is worded.
*Effect on the drafts.* 1a and 1b add a clause to Draft B. 1d B adds a case to the list of Draft C. Both will be counted when drafted.

**The questions for the readers** *(a brief, not a draft)*:
1. Is "an act in progress that endangers the person's own life, with lucidity in doubt" a trigger that can be applied on a doorstep at night, and by whom?
2. What does a first period counted in hours, and a maximum counted in days, allow and prevent in practice? What should the numbers be, and should Chapter I state a ceiling?
3. With no medication imposed, what happens during the hold, and is physical restraint without sedation safe for the hours it may last?
4. What must the text exclude so that the hold is never used for a refusal of treatment, a protest, a way of living, or old age?
5. Who should assess, how quickly can a panel from another node sit, and what is lost if it sits remotely?
6. What should follow the release of a person who is still at risk?
7. How should an advance statement and a named person be used, and what if they conflict with the person's present will (item 1c)?
8. How does the right to an "uncoerced biological exit" stand beside an interruption of an attempt, and where is the line between a settled decision and a crisis?

## **Item 3. Preparation of mass harm** *(SIM-QA-33 D-33.4; batch 1, item 2-list vii)*

**Current text, Art. 8.5.2:**
> 2. Custodia Civilis Territorialis: Where an individual commits predatory violence resisting stabilization, restraint is designated as *Custodia Civilis Territorialis*, occurring within daylight residential suites with open gardens, nutritious meals, and psychiatric care. Sensory deprivation, chemical blunting, and punitive isolation are banned.

**Art. 8.1.3 (first sentence):**
> 3. Universal Weapon Ban: Designing, manufacturing, or compiling weaponry, munitions, or robotics intended to kill or injure persons is categorically prohibited to humans and machines alike.

**Art. 1.4.3 (last sentence):**
> Disabling or bypassing these interlocks constitutes a structural crime against biospheric continuity.

**Draft C, cases (a) and (b), not merged:**
> (a) to stop violence against a person, in progress or immediately about to begin; (b) to stop an act in progress that directly endangers the life of others;

**Problem.** A person who is building a pathogen or a weapon has harmed no one yet. Draft C lets nobody hold them: making is not violence, and it does not "directly" endanger life until the thing is used. The canon calls these acts crimes and gives no answer to them.

| Option | Content |
| :--- | :--- |
| **A** | Nothing is added. The answer is exclusion from the facility and the measures of SIM-QA-33; the person can continue elsewhere. |
| **B** | Custody is extended to the preparation of mass harm, with the guarantees of custody (a drawn jury, review). |
| **C** | A case is added to the list: a person may be held to stop the making or the use of a means of mass death, and for the time needed to take that means away, with validation within 72 hours as in case (c). No continuing custody. |

*Red team:* **C.** The red team gave no preference when this was first put; this is its view after the drafting. B is preventive detention: a person is held for what they might do, on a judgment of what counts as "preparation", and in a chapter that can never be narrowed that is the widest power in the list. C answers the danger, which is the object, and not the person's intentions. *Residuals of C:* a person who starts again is stopped again, each time; and taking the means away meets Art. 2.2.1, under which personal possessions "can never be expropriated": a sentence outside Chapter I must say that a means of mass death is not a personal possession (with D-37.4, whether a person may keep a weapon).

*On 4b.* Under B the two protective clauses could be changed by a 75% majority while the prohibitions they qualify could not: a child could end up without medicine by amendment.
*On 4c.* A is where Art. 1.2.1 leads when read plainly, and it protects, among others, infants born with intersex traits from surgery they did not choose. Its cost is that communities for whom the circumcision of boys is a duty will not join, or will have it done outside the commons' facilities; and Art. 1.8.4 (SIM-QA-23) will be invoked against it as a rule that falls unequally on a religion, so the text must say which provision governs. Under B both outcomes remain possible before different juries. This is the most contested choice of the two batches, and it is permanent either way. It is also the subject of SIM-QA-24 (held), which has not been red-teamed.

## **Item 2 Annex: The Red Team's Provisional Answers to the Eight Questions (2026-11-20)**

`[STATUS: SIMULATION_HYPOTHESIS]` `PROVISIONAL, UNVERIFIED`. Attached to the brief at the maintainer's request, so that the three readers have something to attack and not a blank page. These are directions, not article text: decision 2d stands and nothing about the hold is worded until the readers have answered. They are reasoning on text. Nothing was measured, no source was checked, and no clinician, lawyer or person who has been held has read them. The readers are asked to say where each answer is wrong.

**1. The trigger.**
- "Lucidity in doubt" is dropped. Nobody can assess lucidity on a doorstep at night; in practice the phrase means that whoever arrives decides.
- The trigger is what can be seen: an act, in progress or immediately about to begin, that is likely to kill or gravely and irreversibly injure the person.
- The test is reversed. The hold is allowed unless the person has a recorded, settled decision to end their life (answer 8). A record can be checked on the spot; a state of mind cannot.
- Anyone may interrupt for the moment. Beyond minutes, only people trained in crisis response may continue.

**2. The numbers.**
- 24 hours on the responders' authority alone.
- 72 hours at most before a jury drawn by lot validates or releases, the figure of the other cases of Draft C.
- 7 days in total for one episode, not renewable. A new hold needs a new act.
- Chapter I states all three as ceilings, since a closed chapter with no ceiling has none. Law may shorten them.
- *Comparison, from memory and not checked:* emergency holds of about 72 hours in many states of the United States, 24 to 72 hours in England, and a doctor-ordered placement of up to six weeks in Swiss law. Seven days is strict against these.

**3. During the hold.**
- Restraint by hands for minutes only, never face down, never by straps or devices. Prolonged physical restraint without sedation can kill, so it cannot be the method for hours.
- After that, containment by place: a room the person cannot leave, with someone present throughout. The person is never left alone (Art. 8.5.2 bans punitive isolation).
- Medication, food, sleep and contact with the person they named are offered. Nothing is imposed.
- *Cost:* a person in severe agitation who refuses medication stays agitated in that room.

**4. What the text excludes.**
- Anything that is not an act: a diagnosis, a statement of belief, old age, homelessness, a way of living.
- Refusals: a refusal of treatment, food or care is never "an act endangering life".
- Chosen risk: climbing, fasting, drug use.
- Danger to others, which belongs to cases (a) and (b) of Draft C.
- A person with a recorded settled decision.

**5. Who assesses.**
- Within 24 hours: two people who did not start the hold, at least one with clinical crisis training, with the person's named person or an Advocate present.
- By 72 hours: a jury drawn by lot from another node. It asks whether there was an act and whether the danger is still immediate, not whether the person is ill. The clinician advises and does not decide.
- A remote sitting only if the person chooses it or nobody can arrive in time, and with the Advocate physically in the room. A remote jury cannot see the conditions of the hold, hears a person in crisis poorly, and approves more easily.

**6. After release.**
- Nothing is compulsory: no conditions on release, no supervised treatment.
- Offered: contact within 72 hours and through the following weeks; the choice to stay voluntarily in the same place; a safety plan made with the person.
- An invitation to write an advance statement and to name a person.
- The record belongs to the person and is never used against them.

**7. Advance statement and named person.**
- The statement governs whom to call, where to be taken, what helps, and which treatments the person accepts.
- The named person is present and speaks at both assessments.
- On conflict, decision 1c applies: the present refusal prevails for the body. *Added proposal:* an advance statement may bind the person to stay in a place, within the 7-day ceiling, and never to be medicated.

**8. Exit and interruption.**
- A settled decision is one made in advance: declared, followed by a waiting period, confirmed, recorded. Anything else is treated as a crisis.
- Nobody approves it. Witnesses check only that there is no pressure, never the reasons; a panel that can refuse would make the exit coerced.
- An interruption delays and never cancels: at worst the person loses 7 days.
- The length of the waiting period is a number for Organic Law.

**Where the red team is least sure.**
1. *The 7-day ceiling.* It may be too short for a severe episode. Permanent text cannot be lengthened later, only shortened by law, so an error costs in either direction.
2. *Answer 3.* Whether containment without medication is safe for a person in extreme agitation is not known to the red team. It is the first question for the clinician.
3. *Answer 8.* The waiting-period model fails a person in unbearable suffering who cannot wait. The reader who has been held is asked whether the model respects them.
4. *Answer 1 and Draft G.* A person who cannot decide and is in danger falls under case (g) of Draft C and under Draft G, whose third sentence forbids treatment against a present refusal. The readers are asked whether the two texts and the hold leave a gap or a contradiction.
