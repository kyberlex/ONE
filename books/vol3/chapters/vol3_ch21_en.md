The floor did not just shake; it dropped an inch and snapped back, vibrating like a plucked cable. 

Down in Drift Three, four hundred meters below the headframe, the subterranean thump blew the pilot flames out on both paraffin stoves. In the sudden dark, thirty children drew breath at once—a collective, ragged gasp that smelled of fear and dry maize porridge.

Then the air vents began to scream.

It was not the wind. Lucía Mamani knew the mountain gale’s tone; the wind was a clean, continuous whistle that polished the granite till it shone. This was a brutal, rhythmic thudding—the heavy twin-blade chop of an AndesGuard turbine holding hover directly above the vertical ventilation flue. 

The downwash hit the surface intake like a descending piston. A solid cylinder of white alkaline dust dropped down the borehole, slamming into the horizontal drift with the force of an exploded flour sack. 

Instantly, the chamber went blind. The emergency phosphor strips on the timber struts became faint smudges of bruised violet. 

"Mamita!" 

A six-year-old boy, his ribs visible beneath a coarse sheep-wool sweater, threw himself against Lucía’s knees with enough force to bruise her kneecap through her canvas trousers. Behind him, two toddlers curled into the gravel, their throats locking in the dry, whooping cough of acute salt-dust inhalation. The air tasted of potash, caustic lye, and scorched transformer oil.

"Masks down!" Lucía shouted. Her voice had the rough, flat crack of an iron shovel hitting caliche. "Pachas over faces! Do not draw with your mouth! *Nose only!*"

Before the mining consortium bought out the upper basin’s springs, Lucía had worked three years in the triage tent behind the sulfur refinery at San Pedro, where women brought babies whose eyes had swollen shut from brine-leaching pits. She knew what panic did to high-altitude blood. At four thousand meters, when fear sped the heart to one hundred and forty beats, the lungs emptied their carbon dioxide too fast; the blood turned alkaline, the fingers curled into rigid claws, and the chest muscles clamped shut in tetany.

If sixty children hyperventilated in a closed adit filled with airborne salt, half of them would pass out before the dust settled. Some would drown in their own pulmonary froth.

The helicopter shifted pitch directly overhead. The roar deepened, a mechanical hammer striking the mountain's roof. Corrugated iron roofing sheets rattled against the rock face up in the yard. Through the shaft, the downwash drove temperatures down instantly, dragging the sub-zero high-desert air down into the warm, damp crawlway. Condensation sheared off the roof bolts, dropping in icy sheets.

"Manuel, hold the wool," Lucía commanded, catching the boy by the scruff of his collar and pulling him flush against her hip. She did not stroke his hair; comfort was useless when the diaphragm was spasming. She gripped the muscle between his neck and shoulder, digging her blunt thumb hard into the nerve bundle until he gasped from the sharp, clean pain. "Look at my teeth, Manuel. *Look at them.*"

The boy blinked through tears rimed with white crust.

Lucía pressed her left hand flat against her own belly, just below the ribs. With her right hand, she caught the fingers of an older girl—twelve, maybe thirteen, her knuckles white where she gripped an empty plastic water jug—and slammed the girl’s palm onto Manuel’s chest.

"In through the snout. Four counts," Lucía said, her vocal cords dropping into the low, chest-cavity resonance of the *samay*—the rhythmic breath-clamp old pack-mules and llama-drovers used when climbing the frozen passes of the cordillera. It was not a song; it was a rhythmic friction of vocal cords designed to force backpressure into collapsing bronchioles. 

"*Huuu-wah.*" 

She dragged the sound out, vibrating her diaphragm against the boy’s chest. 

"*Huuu-wah.* Fill the gut. Not the throat. Push my hand away with your stomach, Manuel. Push it!"

The boy shuddered. His belly expanded against her thumb. 

"Hold," she snapped. "Two. Three. Out through wet wool. Blow the dust off your chin."

Beside them, an infant began to wail—a shrill, reedy sound that cut through the blade chatter. A mother, barely twenty, was rocking the bundle frantically, shaking the child in a blind tremor that was only accelerating the infant’s oxygen starvation.

"Stop shaking him!" Lucía lunged on her hands and knees across the wet rubble, her boots sliding in the greasy grey shale that paved the drift. She reached into her pocket, bypassed the useless paper masks that had turned to soggy paste against her sweat, and pulled out a small piece of clean chamois leather she kept soaked in dilute citric acid for cleaning battery terminals. She ripped it in two with her teeth.

"Press it over his nose," she ordered the mother, thrusting the sour, damp strip into the woman’s shaking fingers. "The acid cuts the lye. Breathe with me, Juana. Do not look at the ceiling. The rock does not care that you are frightened. Breathe!"

"*Huuu-wah.*"

Lucía drove the cadence into the rock. She caught the eye of an elder—old Don Teodoro, whose silicotic lungs were already whistling like broken bellows from the salt dust. The old man understood. Without a word, he planted his heavy miner's boots into the ballast, threw his poncho over three huddled girls, and took up the drone, his cracked baritone joining hers.

"*Huuu-wah.*"

The sound was physical. It was forty chest cavities vibrating in the low register, pushing back against the hysteria that smelled of cold sweat and vomit. The children caught the tempo. They didn't understand the physiology, but their terror found a rail to run on. In through the nostrils, four seconds of cold, caustic air filtered through salt-caked wool; five seconds of held pressure to force oxygen across bruised lung linings; six seconds of low, guttural humming that emptied the stagnant air from the base of the diaphragm.

The communal pitch rose, a deep hum that droned through the adit, beneath the thumping roar of the helicopter.

Then the downwash stopped.

The sudden silence was deafening, broken only by the thin, metallic whine of a dying turbine spooling down—not retreating, but settling onto the mountain terrace directly above the adit mouth.

A heavy mechanical clonk reverberated through the steel vent ducting.

Lucía froze, her hand still locked on Manuel’s shoulder. The boy had stopped crying; his breath was moving with the slow, mechanical cadence she had beaten into him.

"Lucía," Don Teodoro rasped, his face grey with dust. "They didn't come to shoot."

"No," Lucía whispered. "Bullets are expensive. Salt is free."

From the top of the vent flue came a wet, slithering sound—the unmistakable drag of heavy vinyl coated in industrial grease. 

Above them, on the surface, the mercenary crew had not landed to conduct an assault through the drifts. Through the speaking tube on the timber column, Carlos’s voice cut through a squall of static, choked with coughing.

"Lucía! Clear the drift! They’re dropping mud plugs into the intake flues! They’re sealing the shaft!"

A second later, the air inside the adit reversed. 

The natural chimney effect that pulled geothermal warmth up and out of the deep levels ceased instantly as thousands of pounds of weighted bentonite drilling mud and coarse brine-gravel dumped from underslung helicopter hoppers into the intake shaft. The sudden dead-end sent a hydraulic shockwave back down the vents. The thin tin ducts buckled, tearing away from their rusted wall anchors and dropping onto the trackway in a tangle of twisted metal.

The smell changed instantly. 

The sharp, chemical cold of the alpine downwash was cut off. In its place, rising from the fractured floor below where the deep riser had blown three minutes before, came the thick, stagnant warmth of the unventilated deep earth: eighty-degree steam thick with dissolved arsenic, hydrogen sulfide, and boiling iron salts.

The children felt it first. The air in the crawlway grew instantly heavy, humid, and impossibly hot. 

"The bottom blew," Lucía realized, her heart dropping into her gut. "And they just sealed the top."

A muffled boom rattled the rock under their boots—not an explosion of dynamite, but the first deep bubble of superheated steam from the four-thousand-meter aquifer hitting the collapsed mud seal in the shaft above them, turning the entire drift into a sealed pressure cooker.

Manuel looked up at her, his small, dirt-streaked face pale in the dying green phosphor. "Mamita? Is the mountain angry?"

Lucía stood up. She wiped the salt crust from her upper lip with the back of a bloody knuckle. Through the steam-thickened air, fifty pairs of dark, luminous eyes watched her from the shadows of the timbers, waiting for the cadence to break.

She did not break it.

"In through the nose," she said, her voice dropping an octave, hard as mountain quartz. "Take your water bottles. We are going down to the haulage winches. Move."
