The clinking inside the flume died with a dry, suffocating thud, replaced by the hiss of scorched mineral dust. Beneath the soles of Carlos’s boots, the deep vibration that had sustained the abandoned shaft for three days shuddered and narrowed.

Forty thousand liters. That was all the condensing coils in the lower thermal vent could guarantee every twenty-four hours before the salt crust choked the intake screens, and now half of it was choking on upstream shale dumped by Varas’s retreating pick-up trucks.

"Clear the valve!" Mateo yelled, his voice bouncing off the granite ribs of the ventilation drift. His scarred left hand, missing its index finger, clamped onto an iron spanner, veins bulging against the grimy sleeve of his sheepskin coat. "If the coil drops below line pressure, the misting heads dry out. Forty minutes, Carlos! Forty minutes of dry air and three levels of vertical seed greens scorch into straw!"

"And if you rob the downhill pipe, our terraces turn to white chalk by noon!" A weathered hand slammed against the iron casing of the manifold. Don Evaristo, his poncho smelling of dry sheep dung and scorched kerosene, stepped between Mateo and the brass gate valve. Behind him stood six farmers from the upper arroyo, their faces chiseled by high-altitude sun, holding wooden canal shovels like pikes. "Three generations my family has watered the broad beans from the upper melt. Your machines do not eat. Children eat."

"The aeroponic coils feed three thousand in the drift shelters!" Mateo shouted, stepping forward until his chest brushed the elder’s shoulder. "Your terrace furrow loses half its water to the wind before it touches a root!"

"Step back," Carlos said. His voice was not loud, but the hollow rattle in his chest—the old, creeping dust of twenty years under the caliche benches—gave it a rough, scraping weight that carried through the damp gallery.

The two men did not step back. They breathed hard in the freezing draft, their breath pluming white under the yellow glow of the hanging inspection lanterns. Outside, beyond the timbered portal, dawn was beginning to bleed cold slate-grey across the jagged spine of the Cordillera, dropping the mountain temperature to ten below zero.

Carlos reached into his coat and drew out the small, turned-wood sand timer he kept wrapped in an oiled rag next to Rosa’s folded baptism ribbon. He set it on the cold iron flange of the three-way divider.

"Stage One," Carlos said, looking from Mateo’s bloodshot eyes to Evaristo’s stone-set jaw. "Five minutes. Direct exchange. No hands on the cast iron. Go."

Mateo inverted the cylinder. Fine red quartz sand hissed down the glass neck.

"The greens produce ten times the caloric weight per liter," Mateo barked, tapping a calloused knuckle against the intake pipe. "We recycle ninety percent of the drainage through the lower gravel beds. If you divert twenty thousand liters to the open canals in this wind, the humidity will evaporate before it reaches the third switchback. It is murder by arithmetic."

"Arithmetic is for men who do not have their hands in the dirt," Evaristo spat. His voice was steady, but the knot of his throat jerked with rage. "The ayllu does not drink your mist. The elders in the chapel cannot chew your green pulp. Our soil is living flesh. If the salt dries on the clay today, the earth cracks. Once the clay dies, no rain brings it back. You ask us to starve our ancestral earth so your steel shelves can stay green."

"It’s not just shelves!" Mateo fired back. "It’s sixty infants in the triage gallery—"

"The sand is through," Carlos cut in. The last grain of red quartz dropped into the lower bulb. Neither man had blinked. The air between them was thicker than the sulfur smoke leaking from the blocked intake.

Under the Covenant, the procedure was unbending: Stage Two demanded a twenty-four-hour cooling break, a day of strict communicative silence where both sides were bound to work together clearing rubble from the secondary diversion canals before another word could be spoken.

"We take the cooling break," Evaristo said, stepping back, crossing his thick arms. "Tomorrow morning we talk."

"No," Mateo whispered, his knuckles whitening on the spanner. "Look at the condensation glass. The head is already down ten centimeters. In forty minutes the nozzles seize. Salt crystals will bake inside the brass apertures like cement. If we take twenty-four hours of silence, there will be nothing left down there to water."

A dangerous silence fell over the platform. One of the younger farmers behind Evaristo shifted his grip on a heavy ash shovel. On the gallery catwalk above, two miners leaned over the galvanized railing, their faces shadowed, wrenches loose in their hands. The fragile truce that had held off the mining consortium’s lawyers forty minutes ago was shearing along the grain of old mountain survival.

Carlos turned to Sofia, who sat on an upturned grease drum near the air-lock door. Her face was gray with exhaustion, dark bruises of sleeplessness under her eyes, her temples throbbing with the vicious altitude sickness that had pursued her from the coast. She was wrapping her wool scarf tighter against the draft that whistled through the blast doors.

"Article Four, Section Five," Sofia said, her voice dry and rasping from the alkaline dust. She pulled a dog-eared, thumb-smeared sheaf of water-resistant paper from her canvas satchel. "Under catastrophic resource decay—where mechanical desiccation or freezing presents irreversible loss to life-support arrays within the cooling window—the assembly may formally bypass the twenty-four-hour break."

Evaristo scowled. "A Santiago lawyer finding holes in the mountain."

"Not a hole," Carlos said, his boots crunching on the frozen gravel as he stepped between the manifold and the farmers. "A survival latch. We bypass Stage Two only by mutual invocation of the emergency clause. Otherwise, the valve stays locked where it is, fifty-fifty, until the line freezes and both of you lose everything."

Evaristo looked down at the manifold. The pressure needle was shivering toward the red peg. The faint, sweet smell of damp nutrient roots was already beginning to sour with the dry heat radiating from the geothermal exhaust flue.

"Invoke it," Evaristo muttered through clenched teeth.

"Invoked," Mateo breathed.

Carlos stepped to the wooden shelf recessed into the mine wall and lifted down the small brass sortition drum. It was an old lottery cage, once used in the company barracks to assign holiday weekend shifts, dented and scarred, its handle polished smooth by three generations of miners' thumbs. Inside rattled forty-five stamped copper tokens, each marked with the personal mark of an adult living within the mining compound and the upper irrigation terrace.

"Stage Three," Carlos said, holding the drum out into the cold yellow lantern light. "Three arbiters. Odd parity. Their decision binds the distribution manifold until sunset tomorrow."

He did not wait for an objection. He spun the wire drum. The copper slugs chattered like pebbles in a dry creek bed. *Clack-clack-clack.*

Carlos opened the spring hatch. Three tokens tumbled into his cracked, salt-burned palm.

"Lucía Mamani," Carlos read, his voice steady. The elder nurse from the infirmary. "Gabriel Choque." An irrigation ditch-tender from Evaristo’s own family. "Dr. Morales."

The three stepped forward from the small crowd gathered at the gallery mouth. Carmen Morales pushed her thick spectacles up her nose, her down parka covered in chalk dust. Lucía wiped her chapped hands on her apron, her dark eyes scanning the manifold’s shivering needle. Gabriel, a quiet man of fifty whose knees were bent outward from half a century of humping seed potatoes up stone terraces, took off his knitted cap.

"You have six minutes," Carlos said. "The manifold cannot wait for a seventh."

The three did not argue theory. Lucía spoke first, her voice a low, urgent murmur. "The infirmary needs eight hundred liters of clean water for the rehydration salt solutions and the steam laundry. No more. The rest of the domestic cistern can hold on greywater for twenty-four hours."

"If we cut the terrace volume by a third," Gabriel said, looking directly at Evaristo, "we lose the lower corn plot, but the beans and the seed beds survive if we run the water only through the buried stone conduits at dusk, when the sun won't burn the run-off."

"And the aeroponics?" Carmen Morales pressed, her pencil scribbling furiously on the back of a ration slip. "If we pulse the mist every nine minutes instead of four, Mateo, can the root systems hold without cellular collapse?"

Mateo swallowed hard, staring at the diminishing pool of water in the sight glass. "They’ll stunt. The yield drops twenty percent."

"Will they die?" Carmen asked.

"No," Mateo whispered. "They will not die."

"Then the allocation stands," Lucía said, looking across all three men. "Twenty-two thousand liters to the subterranean aeroponic loop on a nine-minute pulse cycle. Twelve thousand liters down the buried stone conduits to the upper terrace seed beds. Six thousand into the central infirmary and domestic storage cisterns. No open ditch irrigation until the intake flume is cleared."

Gabriel nodded once. Carmen signed the scrap of paper and pressed it flat against the cold cast-iron valve box.

Three signatures. A sixty-six percent practical consensus among the arbiters, ratified without a shot, without an injunction, without a sovereign decree.

Carlos took the heavy spanner from Mateo’s hand. He set the jaw over the rusted square nut of the primary diversion gate. The metal groaned, screeching against twenty years of calcium scale, then yielded with a sharp crack that echoed down the tunnel.

Water surged through the divided channels—a split, hollow roar that branched into three distinct voices. One rushed into the plastic subterranean feeder line leading down to the humid darkness of the vertical farm; one gurgled deep into the ancient hand-cut rock conduits leading out toward the terraced slopes; one filled the galvanized pipe plunging toward the shelter cisterns.

The pressure needle steadied. It hovered two millimeters above the red mark, vibrating with the frantic pulse of an engine running on fumes, but it did not fall.

Evaristo looked at the iron pipe for a long moment, then spat onto the gravel. "We clear the intake flume before midday, Carlos. If Varas dropped shale, there is more coming down the mountain."

"Take Mateo’s second crew," Carlos said, exhaling a long plume of gray steam into the dawn. "Take the shovels from the tool crib."

The old farmer nodded once, a curt tilt of the chin, and turned down the drift, his men trailing behind him.

Carlos leaned against the manifold, his ribs aching from the sudden retreat of adrenaline. The air in his lungs felt thin and cold, scraping like broken glass. He reached into his pocket, his thumb brushing the silk baptism ribbon, grounding himself against the memory of Rosa’s fever, against the ghost of dry wells and dry throats that had haunted this ridge for forty years.

"Carlos," Sofia said from the gallery portal.

Her voice was not relieved. It was sharp, clipped, stripped of all color.

Carlos pushed himself off the iron manifold and walked to the timbered entrance.

Sofia was standing by the optical laser transceiver mounted on the old steel headframe. The infrared link was pulsing red, its tiny diagnostic diode flashing an erratic, stuttering code against the frosted metal mast. High above them, across the jagged cut of the mountain gap, the line-of-sight laser to the Bolivian relay was fluttering like an oil lamp in a hurricane.

"It’s not just shale in the flume," Sofia whispered, pointing up toward the eastern ridge where the salt flats met the vertical rock walls of the upper pass.

Through the clear, freezing desert dawn, Carlos saw them: three heavy caterpillar-tread excavators, painted in the matte-black livery of the provincial road commission, grinding along the rim of the canyon above their primary intake springs. Suspended from the boom of the lead machine was a massive steel cofferdam gate, swaying over the narrow gorge like a guillotine.

They were not coming to evict the mine.

They were coming to bury the entire aquifer under four thousand tons of blasted overburden.
