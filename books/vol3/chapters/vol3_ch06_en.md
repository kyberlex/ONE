The amber foam hit the iron catwalk with the slap of wet lard, spattering across Mateo’s canvas work trousers. The stench of industrial solvent and raw chlorine punched down his throat, caustic enough to make his eyes stream instantly. 

“Bypass!” Mateo yelled, his voice cracking against the shriek of the twisted timbers. 

Carlos didn't hesitate. Instead of dropping the four-ton counterweight into the gallery, the older miner pivoted on his bad leg, swung the eight-pound sledge in a tight, desperate arc, and struck the brass release dog of the emergency diverter gate. The rusted plate slammed home over the aeroponic cistern’s intake with an ear-splitting clang, shunting the yellow slurry away from the clean subterranean water reserves and down toward the dead sump of the dry tailings trench.

The defoliant roared past their feet in a churning, chemical tide. But behind the cascade, through the jagged puncture torn in the caprock, came a sound far worse than falling stone: the rhythmic, metallic scrape of crampons against wet dolomite.

Halogen beams sliced down through the vapor. 

Mateo flattened himself against the cold rock wall of the drift, pulling Sofia down beside him. The stump of his left index finger throbbed with a dull, sickening heat—a phantom nerve firing every time the barometric pressure plunged. Beneath his ribcage, his heart hammered a furious, jagged cadence. Through the chemical haze forty feet above, four figures were descending the ventilation shaft, moving with the measured, predatory economy of professional mountain contractors. AndesGuard.

They wore charcoal-grey drysuits over body armor, full-face positive-pressure respirators sealed tight against the chemical wash they had just dropped down the flue. On their chests were strapped heavy zip-ties, battery-powered hydraulic spreaders, and non-lethal pepper-ball carbines. They weren’t here to shoot their way through an army; they were an eviction extraction team, dispatched to secure the high-pressure manifold, cut the laser relay cables, and seal the adit behind concrete plugs before dawn.

“Point team to command,” a voice crackled through an external respirator speaker, muffled by rubber and stone. “Sump diverter engaged. Agitators are on the lower winch deck. Move to clear the catwalk.”

Sofia was kneeling in the freezing brine, shivering violently, her knuckles white where she gripped the rusted angle-iron of the railing. Her lips were blue from the thin air, her breath coming in ragged, shallow gasps as the high-altitude hypoxia clawed at her lungs. She looked up at Mateo, her dark eyes wide, pupils dilated with terror.

Mateo reached into the cuff of his flannel shirt and pulled out a four-inch length of stiff, unbent copper wire. It was an old habit, something he had done since he was twelve, helping his uncle clear lime scale from the high-pressure brass nozzles in the lower valleys before the foreign concessions bought the river rights and turned the canals to cracked dust. Whenever his hands shook, he rolled the copper wire between his thumb and middle finger until the friction warmed his skin.

“Can you climb back to the seed vault?” Mateo whispered, his lips brushing Sofia’s ear.

“Carlos can’t,” she gasped, her voice barely carrying over the gurgling chemical runoff.

Mateo glanced across the platform. Carlos was leaned against the timber cribbing, both hands clamped over his chest. The old miner’s lungs were whistling—a wet, rattling scrape that signaled an acute silicosis flare brought on by the chlorine fumes. He couldn’t run fifty paces up the wet incline, let alone scale the emergency escape ladder.

Above them, the lead contractor touched down on the upper staging grate. The mercenary’s boots rang out on the iron. He swung a battery-powered reciprocating saw from his tactical harness, its hardened tungsten blade whining to life with a high-pitched snarl. He stepped toward the armored conduit carrying the primary line-of-sight laser feed down to the air-gapped node.

If that line went dark, the sortition assemblies in Geneva, Detroit, and Susa would lose telemetry. The whole continental bridge would collapse into the silence the consortium needed.

*Class Zero,* Mateo thought, his breath fogging in the bitter draft. *No guns. No blood.* 

He looked at the wall beside him. Running parallel to the drift was the three-inch schedule-80 stainless steel pipe that carried the geothermal condensation feed—water drawn from the deepest volcanic updrafts, pressurized to three hundred atmospheres by the automated pump array to feed the aeroponic misting heads in the darkness below. The water inside was barely above freezing, drawn through the mountain’s subterranean chill coils before it reached the crops.

Mateo lunged across the catwalk, staying low beneath the sweeping halogen beams.

“Hey!” a voice barked from above. A blinding tactical spotlight struck Mateo squarely in the chest. “Hands on the grate! Down on your face, right now!”

Mateo didn’t look up. He reached the manual purge manifold—a heavy, four-way distribution hub equipped with an array of six micro-fogging nozzles designed to scrub rock-dust from the shaft during blasting operations. The consortium had abandoned it years ago, but the miners had re-plumbed it with salvage valves to clear the stale air.

The contractor on the upper grate raised his carbine, leveling the heavy orange barrel at Mateo’s back. “Step back from the valve!”

Mateo shoved the copper wire deep into the pilot orifice of the bypass actuator, popping the corrosion seal with a sharp click, and slammed the four-foot iron cheater bar downward with the full weight of his body.

The manifold didn't merely open; it ruptured into violent life.

Three hundred atmospheres of hydraulic head slammed through the atomizing nozzles with a shriek that drowned out the whining saw, the chemical runoff, and the shouts of the contractors. A blinding, deafening wall of microscopic water droplets erupted into the narrow chimney of the ventilation shaft—tens of thousands of liters of pulverized, near-freezing moisture suspended in the howling desert draft.

At four thousand meters above sea level, with the night wind plunging down the mountainside at minus ten degrees, the physics were immediate and absolute.

The fog didn't drift. It expanded explosively, caught by the thermal downdraft of the mine flue, turning the shaft into a horizontal hurricane of supercooled whiteout. The atomized droplets stripped the ambient heat from the air in a matter of seconds.

The lead contractor’s shout turned into a strangled choke. The microscopic mist hit the warm polycarbonate visors of the AndesGuard respirators and froze instantly on contact, flashing into an opaque, white rind of solid rime frost. 

“Can’t see!” someone screamed through the roar of the water. “Visor’s iced! Vent is iced!”

The contractor with the saw dropped the tool. It clattered against the iron grating, sparking furiously before dying in the freezing torrent. The pressurized mist saturated the nylon weaves of their tactical suits, creeping past neck seals and wrist cuffs. In the dry, brutal cold of the Altiplano, wet cloth was a death sentence; the desert wind sucked the calories from their bodies with the speed of an open freezer door.

Mateo held the cheater bar down, his boot wedged against the pipe frame, his bare hands searing with cold as the stainless steel frost-burned his palms through his wool mittens. His missing finger screamed with phantom agony, but he didn't let go. He watched the shadows above flail blindly in the white vortex.

The contractors weren't cowards. They were hardened men trained for high-altitude security, men who had guarded brine evaporation ponds and lithium railheads against desperate crowds for years. But human physiology had no counter for thermodynamic collapse. Their gloved fingers, soaked through and stiffening under a skin of ice, lost all purchase on the rungs. Their radios squealed with static as moisture shorted the external mic contacts.

“Fall back!” the squad leader howled, his voice trembling with the uncontrollable, violent shivers of early hypothermia. “Up the ladder! Climb! *Climb!*”

They scrambled upward, slipping on the rime-coated steel rungs, their knees knocking against the rock walls, desperate to escape the blinding, sub-zero cloud that was rapidly turning their boots to blocks of lead. The heavy clatter of their retreat echoed up the chimney until the iron access hatch at the surface slammed shut with a muffled, hollow boom.

Mateo slowly released the pressure lever.

The roaring mist subsided into a fine, drifting drizzle that settled over the catwalk like a curtain of cold grey silk. The winch deck was silent now, save for the rhythmic drip of condensation falling into the drainage troughs and the harsh, rattling breath of Carlos Quispe slumped against the timbering.

Sofia scrambled across the wet grating, dropping to her knees beside the old miner, her hands checking his neck, feeling for the frantic pulse in his throat. “He’s breathing,” she said, her voice shaking so hard the words slurred. “The chlorine… it’s clearing out the shaft.”

Mateo didn't answer. He stood alone in the center of the platform, his chest heaving, his hands numb, staring up into the dark, wet chimney where the AndesGuard squad had fled. The air was pure again, scrubbed clean of chemical solvent by the fury of the high-pressure mist, smelling only of crushed stone and wet mineral salt.

Then, deep in the bedrock beneath their boots, a dull, resonant shudder shook the platform.

It was not the surface drills. It was something heavier, slower—a deep, tectonic groan that vibrated through the steel pipes and hummed in the iron railings. From the dark mouth of the lower drainage drift, where the high-pressure geothermal steam riser fed the aeroponic condensers, came the sharp, unmistakable sound of a steel flange shearing under immense, runaway pressure.

Mateo looked down at the copper wire still clenched in his stiffened palm. The fog had saved the winch deck, but the sudden back-pressure had backed up into the subterranean boiler line.

Down in the dark, the steam was rising.
