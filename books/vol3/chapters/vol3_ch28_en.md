The vibration came through the soles of Lucía’s boots before it reached her ears—a dull, rhythmic shudder that made the zinc basins on the triage table sing against the iron brackets.

In the sub-level staging bay, forty meters below the headframe, the air was warm and smelled of sulfur and unwashed wool. Lucía caught a chipped porcelain basin before it could rattle off the shelf. Inside, half a liter of boiled snow water trembled, throwing crescent-shaped ripples against the enameled rim. 

Beside her, a seven-month-old girl with fever-scabbed cheeks whimpered in a cradle woven from dried totora reeds. Lucía pressed two fingers to the infant’s carotid artery, feeling the fragile, rapid flutter. The child’s mother, an Atacameño woman whose knuckles were swollen and split from scouring brine pans, watched Lucía’s eyes with the hollow, terrifying vigilance of the starving.

"The mountain is coughing," the mother whispered. Her dialect was thick with the glottal stops of the upper ravines.

"The consortium is drilling the upper ridge," Lucía said. Her own voice sounded dry, like maize husks grinding together. She swabbed the child’s forehead with a damp scrap of cotton. "Stay below the timber shoring. If the dust falls from the roof, pull the blankets over her face."

The field telephone bolted to the timber stanchion gave three jagged clints—the hand-cranked copper line from the surface hoist house. Lucía snatched the heavy Bakelite receiver.

"Lucía." It was Mateo. His breathing came in short, ragged gasps, choked with surface wind. "You need to come up to the collar. Now."

"I have eighteen children in the second drift who can’t hold down rehydration salts," Lucía said, her thumb tracing the scarred knuckle she had broken years ago on a hand-turned corn mill in San Pedro. "And the caprock is shaking down shale into the dressing ward."

"Leave the ward to the assistants," Mateo shouted over the receiver. The wire popped with static from the optical transceiver mast overhead. "It isn't AndesGuard. Look out the hoist shaft. It’s the valleys."

Lucía dropped the horn into its cradle. She did not take the cage; the electric winch was reserved for wounded elders and hydraulic ballast. Instead, she took the timber utility ladder, her canvas shoes slipping on the wet rungs. With every vertical meter, the geothermal heat of the deep drifts bled away, replaced by the knife-edge chill of four thousand meters of altitude. The air thinned until each lungful felt like swallowing powdered glass.

When she hauled herself through the heavy iron hatch onto the headframe’s apron, the wind hit her like a wet sheepskin pelt.

The sun sat low and white over the western salt pan, glaring off the crust with a blinding, flat brilliance. Above them, two miles up the eastern scarp, the two tracked quarry augers were still turning. Their massive lattice gantries stood silhouetted against the indigo sky, pounding hydraulic steel into the caprock with a mechanical regularity that rattled Lucía’s teeth in her jaw. Plumes of fine white lithium dust blew off their auger flights, drifting down the ravines like poison snow.

Yet nobody on the headframe apron was looking up at the drills.

Carlos Quispe stood at the edge of the gravel terrace, an old wool blanket pinned across his chest with a bent copper safety pin. Sofia was three paces behind him, clutching an uninsulated surveyor’s slate to her ribs, her breath catching in quick, shallow hitches as soroche clamped down on her lungs.

Lucía stepped beside Carlos. "What is it?"

Carlos did not look at her. He simply pointed his calloused chin toward the dry bed of the Quebrada del Salado.

Down in the canyon, winding up from the baked mudflats of the lower basin, a gray-and-red ribbon was moving through the shimmering heat haze. 

It was not a military convoy. There were no diesel exhaust plumes, no gun-truck antennas whipping against the sky. It was a mass of people, walking four abreast where the defile narrowed, spilling across the gravel terraces where the dry wash opened out. 

They moved with the relentless, dragging pace of mountain marches: women with infants slung behind their shoulder blades in striped *aguayos*, old men leaning on dried cactus-wood canes, lead llamas carrying bundles of patched clothing and kerosene cans. Clutched in their hands, strapped to their hips, and balanced on their heads were vessels. 

There were plastic five-gallon cooking-oil jugs bleached colorless by the ultraviolet glare, dented aluminum milk pails, and red-clay *cántaros* whose porous bellies had turned dark where the last drops of well water had seeped out and evaporated hours ago.

"How many?" Lucía whispered.

"Three thousand," Sofia said. Her voice trembled, thin and brittle. "Maybe thirty-five hundred. They started coming through the transit monitors at the provincial boundary two hours ago. AndesGuard abandoned the lower checkpost at the highway crossing. They didn't even try to turn them back. They just opened the gates and pulled their trucks up to the ridge."

"Why would they stop them?" Carlos murmured. His dark eyes were fixed on the lead group, now less than half a league from the mine’s perimeter ditch. "The consortium didn't need to cut off our power. They cut the municipal siphon in the valley two days ago. They told the communities that the only water left in the whole basin was being hoarded here, in the union’s shaft."

"It’s not a siege," Lucía said, the horror dawning in her chest with cold, absolute weight. "It’s a flood."

The condensation coils in the deep ventilation drifts were pulling forty thousand liters from the volcanic steam pockets every twenty-four hours. It had been enough to sustain the sortition assembly, the four hundred striking mine families, and the aeroponic troughs that kept the nursery greens alive. 

Three thousand people would drink that reserve dry in a single afternoon.

The lead group reached the outer gate—a barrier made of welded mine-car axles and rusted aggregate screens. A woman stepped through the gap where the gravel road met the concrete sorting floor. She was small, her face mapped with the deep, sun-carved trenches of the high puna, her braided hair threaded with lengths of dirty orange twine. In her arms, held against her chest like an offering or an accusation, was an earthenware jug, its spout plugged with a twist of dry straw.

She did not look at the armed mercenary outriggers drilling the ridge above. She looked straight at Carlos, then at Lucía’s white nursing smock.

"The pumps in Toconao ran dry on Tuesday," the woman said. She did not shout. Her voice had the flat, terrifying clarity of stone striking stone. "The tankers from the city never came. The company guards at the wellhead told us the water had been seized by court decree. They said you had the springs."

Lucía walked toward her, her knees aching from the ladder climb. "The springs are four hundred meters underground, auntie. They are volcanic steam. We condense them liter by liter."

The woman pulled the straw plug from the jug’s neck and inverted it. Nothing came out. Not a drop. Only a single dead beetle rolled out onto the white caliche dust between Lucía’s boots.

"My son’s three children are sitting in the shade of a boulder two miles back," the woman said. Her eyes were bloodshot from the alkaline wind. "They cannot walk the grade. If you have steam, you have water. We are not leaving this yard."

Behind her, the crowd surged through the perimeter breach. They did not shout; they did not raise fists. The sound they made was worse—the dragging of thousands of rubber-tire sandals over shale, the hollow, ceramic clatter of empty jars knocking together, and the collective, dry rasp of three thousand throats breathing the dust of the salt flats.

Suddenly, the mountain beneath them gave a sickening, metallic lurch.

A deafening groan sheared through the bedrock—not the steady drone of the drilling augers, but the splintering crack of an underground fault snapping under artificial hydraulic pressure. 

Half a mile up the slope, where the consortium's tracked crawlers were anchored, a geyser of sulfurous yellow foam erupted from the caprock, jetting fifty feet into the freezing air. The consortium wasn't merely drilling exploratory holes. They had opened the high-pressure manifold, forcing thousands of gallons of toxic chemical tailings down into the fracture zone to crush the subterranean aquifer's ceiling.

The ground pitched. A woman in the front row dropped her clay jar; it shattered on the frozen ballast into three dozen jagged terracotta shards.

"They're cracking the upper seal!" Mateo screamed from the hoist tower catwalk, his arm pointing toward the ridge. "The brine injection is overloading the fissure! If that slurry reaches the steam drift, the headframe will blow like an uncapped boiler!"

The three thousand people on the apron froze. 

High on the ridge, the hydraulic lines on the consortium's tracked crawlers pulsed with violent pressure, their diesel engines roaring as they prepared to drive the second auger home. 

And then, down in the dry wash behind the crowd, the dust rose again—three gray tactical gun-trucks from AndesGuard had just swung off the salt highway, maneuvering to seal the mouth of the canyon, trapping every living soul against the trembling mouth of the mine.
