The low mechanical wail of the lead transport reached the defile before its headlights cleared the switchback. It wasn’t a patrol siren; it was the flat, grinding horn of an open-pit hauler clearing haulage roads, vibrating through the basalt floor beneath Mateo’s steel-toed boots.

Mateo pulled the heavy canvas collar of his jacket up against his ears. The air at four thousand meters was minus twelve, dry enough to crack the corners of his lips every time he drew a breath. Beside him on the suspended iron grating of Valve Gallery Three, Carlos was already leaning into the cold steel of the bypass manifold.

Carlos’s breathing sounded like dry husks dragged over sheet metal. His brine-burned palms, wrapped tight in strips of coarse sailcloth, gripped a three-foot iron cheater pipe slotted over the bypass valve wheel. Whenever his lungs hitched and threatened to seize, he tapped the flat band of his brass wedding ring against the cold iron—three quick strikes, a habit Mateo had never seen in daylight, an involuntary metronome keeping the panic from his throat.

“Slurry trucks are third in line,” Carlos rasped, squinting through the salt-encrusted mesh of the gallery screen. “Behind the armored blades. If they reach the collar platform, they pour fifty tons of quick-set cement down the shaft before sunrise. The aquifer seals. The pump chambers drown.”

“They won’t reach the collar,” Mateo said. His voice was thinner than he wanted. On his left hand, the stump of his index finger throbbed with a dull, rhythmic ache, sensitive to the biting barometric drop. As a boy in the valley orchards, his mother had measured out dry pinto beans with a notched copper cup that made the exact same flat clinking sound as the zinc shear pins dangling from the sluice tripwire by his elbow. He had hated that cup because it meant scarcity. Looking at the pins now, the memory sat like lead in his gut.

Below them, the defile narrowed to a single-lane ledge carved out of the mountain during the copper boom. On the outer lip: an eight-hundred-foot vertical drop into the dry salt basin. On the inner wall: the exposed arterial pipes of the geothermal ventilation system, lagged with brittle asbestos and banded in rusting mild steel.

The lead bulldozer rounded the lower shoulder. It was a stripped-down, armored industrial crawler, its front blade welded with heavy plate iron to deflect rockfalls, its roof cages reinforced against overhead drops. Behind it rumbled a six-wheeled tactical carrier, its high-intensity halogen array turning the basalt cliff face bone-white. The light swept across the gallery, catching the frost on Mateo’s eyelashes.

The radio clipped to Carlos’s harness hissed twice and died. Sofia’s voice didn’t come through. The salt dust rising from the convoy’s tracks was already chewing through the optical carrier beam overhead, scattering the light into useless red haze. They were on their own.

“Engine sound is heavy,” Mateo whispered, leaning out over the rail. “Twin turbochargers. They’re running low gear to push through the scree.”

“Let them hit the narrows,” Carlos said. His ring clicked against the valve stem. *Clink. Clink. Clink.* “Not before. If we blow the seal too early, the wind down the gorge shears the plume into the valley. We get one head of pressure, Mateo. One.”

The bulldozer’s diesel exhaust coughed black soot into the frozen air. Through the halogen glare, Mateo could see the driver inside the reinforced cage: hunched forward, hands steady on the twin steering levers, wearing the yellow high-visibility vest of an ordinary open-pit contractor. The man wasn't an assassin; he was a heavy-equipment operator working double overtime on a corporate infrastructure contract, trusting the three-inch steel plates to keep him safe from thrown stones.

Fifty yards. Forty.

The bulldozer’s steel tracks bit into the shelf road, throwing sparks against the frozen shale. Behind it, the concrete pumper truck swung wide, its hydraulic boom folded like a grasshopper’s leg above a twenty-ton hopper of dry aggregate and slurry mix.

“Now,” Carlos grunted. “Take the cheater!”

Mateo stepped onto the frozen manifold, wedging his good right hand and the scarred palm of his left around the pipe extension. “On three!”

“No count! Pull!”

They threw their combined weight downward.

The mineral scale inside the ninety-millimeter bypass valve didn’t yield. It groaned, a high, metallic shriek of crystallized copper salts and volcanic sulfur refusing the torque. Carlos’s boots slipped on the rime-crusted grating. He hit his knees with an iron thud, his chest hitching in a violent, rattling cough that sprayed dark drops onto the galvanized floor.

“Again!” Carlos spat, hauling himself back up by the valve spoke, his canvas-wrapped hands tearing at the frayed seams. “Mateo, the tracks are on the marker!”

Mateo planted his boot heels against the safety kick-plate. He ignored the fire tearing through the knuckle-stump of his missing finger, locked his elbows, and dropped his hips toward the void.

The valve stem broke free with a crack like a hunting rifle.

Inside the mountain, four thousand feet of pressurized volcanic reservoir found daylight.

The sound wasn't an explosion; it was the continuous, deafening tear of a locomotive boiler coming apart at the seams. High-pressure steam at ninety degrees Celsius erupted from the slotted diffuser pipes mounted along the base of the rock wall.

In the sub-zero night air, the physics were immediate and violent. The superheated volcanic vapor met the minus-twelve mountain draft, creating an instantaneous thermal fog bank so dense and violent it obliterated the headlights in half a second.

The whiteout was absolute.

Down in the defile, the halogen beams didn’t cut through—they scattered into an impenetrable, blinding wall of white glare. The bulldozer operator slammed his tracks into neutral, the iron clatter of the idler wheels echoing off the canyon walls.

“The air intakes!” Mateo yelled over the roar of the venting steam.

The superheated moisture hit the crawler’s massive radiator grilles. Cold diesel engines rely on dense, freezing oxygen; they were suddenly sucking in wet, mineral-choked volcanic steam at near-boiling temperatures. The bulldozer’s twin turbos whined, choked, and began to surge, the fuel-air mixture dying in the cylinders.

Down on the ledge, the driver threw the transmission into reverse. The reverse horn began its frantic, rhythmic beep, but the iron tracks slid sideways on the wet, slumping salt-shale. With zero visibility, the driver couldn’t see the cliff edge two feet from his outer bogie wheels.

“The sluice!” Carlos shouted, pointing a shaking hand toward the overhead chute. His voice was entirely gone, a ragged rasp lost in the hiss of the bypass.

Mateo lunged across the gallery.

The mechanical trip release was an old mine-car brake lever, connected by a greased aircraft cable to the timber retention gate of the upper scree sluice—fifty tons of coarse basalt aggregate stockpiled from the original ventilation excavation. No electronics. No relays to be jammed or fried.

The trip pin had frozen in its collar. Rime ice had glued the raw iron together during the early morning hours.

Mateo grabbed a cast-iron spud wrench from his tool belt. He struck the retaining cotter pin once. The vibration jolted up his forearm, shaking his teeth. The pin didn’t move.

The pumper truck below was revving its secondary diesel, trying to blindly swing its outriggers into the narrow shelf to stabilize the rig before it slid into the canyon. If those hydraulic outriggers locked down, the road was theirs.

Mateo reversed the wrench, swinging with the full rotation of his shoulder, using his hip against the railing for leverage.

The iron struck the frozen pin with a sharp, ringing crack—the sound of his mother’s notched copper cup striking the timber table.

The pin sheared.

The counterweight dropped.

Above the defile, the massive wooden gate swung upward on its grease-packed trunnions.

A roaring cascade of dry, jagged basalt scree poured down the thirty-degree chute, funneled by sheet-metal deflectors directly into the defile. It hit the road not as an avalanche to crush steel, but as a deliberate river of loose stone, cascading across the crawler’s steel tracks and washing under the tires of the tactical transports.

The gravel rose instantly past the idler wheels, packing the drive sprockets, wedging between the track links with brutal mechanical finality. The bulldozer’s tracks spun once, twice, biting deep into the loose stone, and threw their pins. The heavy tracks peeled off the rollers like dead snakes, burying themselves in four feet of locked aggregate.

The engines stalled. The howling turbos spun down into silence.

The steam continued to vent, curling thick and white through the frozen dark, smelling of sulfur and damp deep-earth stone. Below, through the swirling mist, the slam of heavy armored cab doors echoed up the defile, followed by the frantic, breathless shouts of drivers coughing in the vapor, scrambling back toward the lower switchbacks on foot, their heavy machinery immobilized, dead in the shale.

Mateo rested his forehead against the freezing iron railing, his chest heaving, his left hand throbbing in time with his racing pulse.

Beside him, Carlos slowly lowered himself onto the grating. He held his torn canvas wraps against his ribs, his breath whistling through his teeth, his brass ring silent against the floor.

A high-pitched chime broke through the hiss of the dying steam.

Mateo turned. The small battery-powered terminal mounted inside the gallery’s waterproof junction box had flickered back to life.

The optical carrier hadn’t re-established Detroit or Susa.

The screen was blinking amber with an internal telemetry breach from Shaft Nine, deep below the aquifer pump house.

A single red line scrolled across the monochrome display:

*WARNING: SEISMIC ANOMALY DETECTED AT LOWER SUMP. DURATION: CONTINUOUS. DRILLING SIGNATURE CONFIRMED.*

Mateo’s stomach dropped into a void deeper than the gorge.

AndesGuard hadn’t sent the bulldozers to take the intake shaft.

The bulldozers had been the decoy.
