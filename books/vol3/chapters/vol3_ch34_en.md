The radio on the dashboard of Patrol Unit 408 did not buzz; it rattled, its speaker housing vibrating against the cracked green plastic under the assault of dry altiplano static.

*“Unit Four-Zero-Eight, Calama Command to Captain Vargas. Confirm you have eyes on the aqueduct right-of-way.”*

Hector Vargas did not touch the microphone. He pressed the heel of his palm against the ache behind his left knee—the torn meniscus from twelve winters ago when a horse had slipped on the black ice of Temuco—and stared through the salt-bloomed windshield. 

Outside, at four thousand meters above sea level, the pre-dawn air sat at nine degrees below zero. It didn't merely freeze; it stripped moisture from the lips until they split and bled into the wool of his collar. Two hundred yards down the volcanic scree, illuminated by the guttering orange glare of kerosene smudge pots, sixty people were moving in a jagged human chain. 

They were not an insurgent column. They were old women in layered alpaca shawls, teenage drill-runners with dust-gray faces, and mothers carrying punctured four-gallon cooking oil jugs tied together with braided hemp. They were lining up at the flange of the emergency condenser pipe, catching the thin, steaming trickle of water condensing from the deep mine vent.

*“Vargas,”* the radio barked again, the voice belonging to Subprefect Morales down in the valley, seated in a warm office where the tap water didn't taste of sulfuric ash. *“Aguas del Altiplano has secured the executive enforcement order from the provincial tribunal. That bypass constitutes industrial theft of mineral utility brine. AndesGuard tactical teams are advancing on your flank. You are instructed to clear the right-of-way. Disperse the squatters. Authorized for full containment protocols.”*

Beside him in the passenger seat, Corporal Silva—barely twenty-two, his knuckles raw and purple where they gripped the steering wheel—swallowed hard. His eyes darted to the dashboard, then out into the black gravel desert.

“Captain,” Silva whispered, his breath clouding thick inside the unheated cab. “They’re... that’s Mrs. Mamani down there. The one who sells fried dough by the rail depot. Her grandson had the bloody flux on Tuesday.”

“Shut up, Silva,” Vargas said softly. He reached down to his calf, adjusting the stiff elastic brace over his joint. His fingers were stiff, numb at the tips. He looked through his wing mirror.

Two hundred meters back along the switchback, three matte-black, three-axle gun-trucks were idling in low gear. Their auxiliary roof bars threw blue-white halogen beams across the salt flats, blinding and sharp, bleaching the dry scrub into bone. AndesGuard. Private contractors hired by the mining consortium, their grilles fitted with welded heavy-gauge cattle guards, their suspensions lifted for rough mine rubble. They didn't have police plates. They didn't answer to the regional governor. Their pay was backed by sovereign debt guarantees tied to lithium extraction quotas.

The lead truck’s door swung open. A man stepped into the biting wind, ducking his chin against the gale. Major Brand. Former army logistics, now corporate security chief for the consortium. He marched toward Vargas’s cruiser, his boots crunching rhythmically on the frozen crust of salt and borax.

Vargas stepped out to meet him.

The mountain air hit Vargas like a wet slap turned instantly to ice. The wind howled through the overhead power lines that hung dead between the timber poles, whistling a flat, minor third. 

Brand didn't offer a hand. He kept his tactical gloves tucked into the sleeves of his windproof soft-shell jacket. His eyes, pale and bloodshot from altitude, fixed on the smudge pots below.

“Your radio broken, Captain?” Brand asked. His voice was clipped, flat, carrying the mechanical cadence of a man reading from an operational timeline. “Morales said you were in position twenty minutes ago. We’re losing reservoir head-pressure. The bypass line is siphoning potable volume from the evaporation pre-heaters.”

“Those people are carrying water in cooking pots, Brand,” Vargas said, his voice gravel-rough from the sulfur dust. “There are three infants in the shelter with dehydration fever.”

“And there are seventy-four million dollars in commercial paper maturing at the clearinghouse on Friday,” Brand replied without blinking. “Every liter diverted from the processing loop degrades the brine concentration by two parts per thousand. That breaches the export contract purity spec. That triggers a credit default.” 

Brand turned his head, spitting a dark speck of tobacco against the gravel. 

“Your command gave the order. Push your cruisers down the access path. Form a corridor. We’ll sweep the flange and cap the valve.”

Vargas looked past Brand’s shoulder. In the bed of the lead AndesGuard truck, two contractors were unspooling heavy coils of razor concertina wire. Behind them, mounted on the roll bar, sat a pneumatic pepper-ball hopper and two pressurized water cannons fitted with antifreeze tanks. At this temperature, spraying high-pressure liquid on people meant hypothermia within six minutes. It wasn't riot control; on this plateau, it was an execution by frostbite.

“The Water Code guarantees minimum survival allotment under force majeure,” Vargas said, his boots holding his weight firmly against the incline.

“Force majeure hasn't been certified by the Santiago registry,” Brand said, taking half a step forward. His frame was wide, armored under the nylon. “You know how this works, Captain. The registry doesn't open until nine a.m. Monday. Right now, this is criminal trespass on a critical infrastructure corridor. Clear the line, or step your men aside and let us secure our client's property.”

The radio in Vargas’s cruiser sputtered again, louder this time. *“Unit Four-Zero-Eight! Acknowledge! AndesGuard is authorized to proceed. Vargas, confirm compliance!”*

Silva was looking out through the cruiser's windshield, his face pale, his hand hovering over the mic. He was waiting for his captain.

Down on the terrace, Carlos Quispe had noticed the headlights. The miner stepped in front of the leaking flange, his canvas-wrapped hands raised, palm-out, toward the road. Behind him, the women did not run. They worked faster, their shoulders hunched against the dark, passing filled plastic drums into the dark maw of the old ventilation shaft.

Vargas felt the cold iron of his sidearm holster pressing against his hip. In twenty-two years, he had drawn it four times—twice for wounded mules on the southern highway, twice at the sky during the riots in Antofagasta. He had never aimed a barrel at a neighbor.

“Brand,” Vargas said quietly.

“Move your truck, Captain.”

Vargas didn't move toward his cruiser door. Instead, he took two strides toward the edge of the access cut, looking down at the single-lane switchback that linked the high road to the community cistern. It was narrow—barely nine feet of packed volcanic shale bordered on the left by the sheer drop into the dry wash, and on the right by the steel casing of the high-pressure condenser bypass.

He turned back to his patrol vehicle.

“Silva!” Vargas shouted over the wind.

The young corporal scrambled out of the passenger seat, his cap nearly torn away by the gust. “Sir?”

“Get Unit Four-Zero-Nine and Four-Ten on the line,” Vargas ordered, his voice cutting through the scream of the gale with absolute clarity. “Tell them to fire up their engines. Bring them down the shelf right now. Nose-to-tail.”

Brand’s eyes narrowed. “What are you doing, Vargas?”

“Executing my statutory duty under Article Five of the Organic Police Charter,” Vargas said, turning his back on the security chief. He opened his driver's door, climbed inside, and keyed the fleet mic. 

“All Carabinero units on the ridge,” Vargas commanded, his fingers tightening on the hard plastic of the handset until his joints went white. “This is the Sector Commander. We have an uncertified industrial hazard on the service cut. Private heavy equipment is operating without road safety permits on unstable shale. Move your vehicles across the access grade. Interlock your bumpers. Broadside to the road.”

*“Vargas!”* the radio from Calama shrieked, Morales’s voice breaking into static. *“You stand down immediately! That is an unlawful—”*

Vargas reached down with his left hand, found the heavy master power toggle on the central radio unit, and snapped it down. The green LED died. The cab fell silent, save for the rhythmic shudder of the diesel engine.

Through the salt-filmed glass, Brand was already running back toward his gun-truck, waving his arms in furious circles. 

The starter motors of the AndesGuard vehicles screamed. The black trucks lurched forward, their massive off-road tires grinding the shale into dust, their halogen bars blinding the narrow pass.

“Captain!” Silva cried, terror cracking his voice as he gripped the dashboard. “They’re not stopping! They’re going to ram us!”

“Foot on the brake, Silva,” Vargas said evenly. His pulse pounded in his temples, a sharp, cold rhythm. He reached for the dashboard switch marked *AUX LIGHTS*. “High beams on. Fog lamps on. Lock the differential.”

Two other green-and-white police cruisers descended from the upper switchback, their sirens silent, their heavy steel push-bumpers swinging wide across the road. They braked in tandem, their steel chassis grinding together with a harsh, metallic groan, sealing the narrow throat of the road forty yards above the water line.

The lead AndesGuard truck surged up the final rise, its turbo whistling like a jet engine. Brand wasn't slowing down. He leaned on the truck's horn—a dual-tone air blast that tore through the mountain silence like a ripped sheet of tin.

Thirty yards. Twenty.

Vargas didn't flinch. He leaned his shoulder against the cab door, watching the black bull-bar of Brand’s vehicle fill his side window.

Then, from deep inside the mountain, the earth gave a sickening, hollow lurch.

It was not a tremor. It was an internal rupture—a sound like a freight train slamming into a concrete bunker three hundred feet below the salt crust. 

The frozen gravel under Vargas’s front tires vibrated violently. Along the rock wall to their right, the high-pressure geothermal steam bypass bucked in its steel saddles. The expansion joints squealed, metal screaming against metal, as an oily, grey froth began to spit from the vent seals.

Brand’s driver saw it first. The black truck locked its brakes, tires skidding on the loose scree, stopping six feet from Vargas’s reinforced door.

Through the windshield, Vargas saw the road surface between their vehicles begin to bubble.

The slurry from the upper dam had hit the subterranean furnace. And the vent wasn't just whistling anymore—it was beginning to howl like an artillery shell trapped inside a chimney.
