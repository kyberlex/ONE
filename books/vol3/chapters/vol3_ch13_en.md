The frost did not fall from the sky; it grew out of the salt.

By eight o’clock, the skin of the altiplano had hardened into iron. At four thousand meters, when the sun dropped behind the volcanic rim, the atmosphere peeled away like gauze, leaving the earth naked beneath a void that sucked every calorie of heat upward into black space. The corrugated sheet-iron of the municipal supply depot cracked with rifle-shot snaps as it contracted. Inside the town’s four above-ground distribution tanks, twelve thousand liters of water were turning to crystalline stone, groaning against their rivets until the perimeter seams sheared with the wet, tearing screech of uncoiling wire.

Lucía Mamani felt the cold in her molars first. It was an old ache, carried down from childhood winters in the high bogs of Isluga where her grandmother used to test the thickness of morning ice by driving an iron heel into the peat. 

She stood on the concrete lip of Shaft Four’s collar, clutching a canvas manifest board against her chest to keep her fingers from stiffening around the grease pencil. Her boots were lined with folded sheepskin, but her heels were numb.

"Not down the central incline," she shouted, her voice swallowed by the wool scarf pulled over her mouth. "The draft from the headframe will freeze their ears before they reach the second landing. Funnel them through the old timber drift! The air comes up from the south sump at eighteen degrees—it will thaw their lungs before we bed them down!"

Beside her, two young surface hands from the agricultural cooperative were hauling a sledge laden with wrapped bundles. Inside the rags, a three-month-old infant let out a thin, mewling reedy cry, choked off by the dry, salt-choked gale that whipped off the flats.

"Move, Juanito!" Lucía barked, stepping into the drift mouth to shove the heavy timber airlock door wider. "Get them past the second air curtain! Carmen has three heat-exchanger coils venting steam in Bay Six. Go!"

They hurried past, boots skidding on the frosted track ties. Lucía didn't watch them go; she bent to an old man sitting on an overturned cable reel, his knees drawn up to his chin under three layered ponchos. His breathing sounded like dry maize husks crushed in a mortar—silicosis, Stage Three, his bronchial passages scarred from twenty-two years underground at Chuquicamata before the state sold the concession to foreign paper.

She reached out, slipping two fingers beneath the collar of his rough wool tunic to find the carotid pulse. She had learned long ago in the clinic at San Pedro never to take an altiplano elder’s pulse at the wrist; the mountain cold shut down the extremities so fiercely that a living man’s forearm felt as dead as cedar.

"Don Efraín," she said softly, leaning close so he could read her eyes above the scarf. "The drift is dry. We have hot broth from the aeroponic roots. Stand up."

"The cisterns broke, Lucía," the old man wheezed, his lips tinged with the faint, purplish-blue shadow of hypoxia. "I heard the iron split from my kitchen. If the water freezes, the trucks won't come back from Calama. We’re finished."

"The water isn't in the tanks," she lied with practiced, steady calm, levering her shoulder under his armpit and hauling him upright. His joints clicked like dried kindling. "The water is beneath the salt. Two hundred meters down, where the mountain can't freeze it. Come."

She led him through the double canvas air curtains. The transition was immediate, shocking the senses. 

Outside, the air was minus twelve, dry enough to cause nosebleeds with every ragged inhale. Inside the timber drift, forty yards past the ventilation seal, the temperature climbed past fifteen degrees. The air was thick, mineral-laden, smelling of wet basalt, sulfur, and the alkaline tang of warm damp earth. Condensed water dripped rhythmically from the rock bolts overhead, pattering against the rusty corrugated overhead shields.

Bay Six had been transformed into a subterranean ward. 

Sixty-four people—mostly the town's oldest miners and its youngest children—were sprawled along the crushed-stone ballast of the haulage gallery. Salvaged lithium-iron-phosphate battery packs, stripped from the undercarriages of discarded haul trucks, sat wired in parallel along the wall, feeding low-voltage heating mats laid out beneath coarse alpaca blankets. 

In the center of the drift, Dr. Carmen Morales was kneeling beside a copper pipe array. The pipe hissed violently, sweating thick, hot droplets as it carried condenser effluent from the deep aeroponic misting chambers. Carmen’s face was gray, salt dust riming the dark circles beneath her eyes like dried tear-tracks.

"We have thirty more coming down the incline," Lucía said, guiding Don Efraín to a pallet beside the warm pipe. "The municipal pipes through Barrio Alto burst five minutes ago. The main line down from the spring is frozen solid. The whole northern quadrant has no tap."

Carmen didn't look up; she was tightening a split-clamp over a weeping brass fitting with an adjustable wrench. "We can give them four liters per head per day if the heat exchangers keep cycling. If we drop below sixty percent power on the lower circuit, the condensation coils freeze in the updraft shaft. Then we aren't just out of water—we choke on dry air."

"Mateo stopped the convoy," Lucía said, pulling a chipped enamel mug from her coat and filling it with warm condensation drip from the purge cock. She pressed it into Don Efraín’s trembling, gray-veined hands. "The gravel sluice buried their tracks. They won't bring heavy machines through the defile tonight."

"Mateo stopped four trucks," Carmen retorted, her voice dropping to an urgent, raspy whisper as she stood up, wiping greasy brine from her palms onto her apron. "That wasn't their offensive, Lucía. Sofia ran the telemetry three minutes ago. Look at the balance sheet."

Carmen pulled a grease-stained notebook from her pocket and opened it against the warm copper pipe. 

No screens. Down here, the humidity would ruin a delicate tablet in three hours, and every watt was measured in cups of warm broth. Carmen’s pencil had dug furrows into the paper.

"The headframe laser dropped seventy percent of its packet sync when the salt-frost hit the mirrors," Carmen said. "We barely kept the Detroit handshake alive for four minutes before the beam scattered. But the air-gap pressure sensor at the base of Shaft Nine... look."

Lucía squinted at the scribbled columns. She knew the layout of the mine better than the engineers who had drawn the concession maps forty years ago; her father had died in the third-level collapse when she was twelve. Shaft Nine was the blind vertical bore that dropped straight down to the geothermal sump—the deepest point of the network, three hundred meters below the salt crust, where the warm volcanic water collected before being drawn into the aeroponic condensation circuits.

The recorded sump pressure had not dropped. It had flatlined, then begun to pulse in rhythmic, twelve-second cycles.

"That isn't drainage," Lucía said, her chest tightening. "That's a mechanical draw."

"Not ours," Carmen said. Her jaw worked, grinding against the ever-present grit of the dust. "The consortium's extraction well at the edge of the salar—Well Seventeen. It’s barely two kilometers west of Shaft Nine. When the municipal line froze, they didn't wait for the court eviction warrant. They fired up their deep-bore rotary rigs."

The floor beneath their boots gave a tiny, almost imperceptible lurch.

It wasn't an earthquake. It had none of the rolling, subterranean thunder of the Nazca plate slipping beneath the cordillera. It was a high-frequency, metallic chatter—a vibration that traveled through the solid dacite rock, vibrating through the soles of Lucía’s boots, into her shins, settling in the small of her back.

Across the gallery, a baby stopped crying, startled into silence. Don Efraín froze with the enamel cup halfway to his mouth, his blind, milky eyes turning toward the dark recesses of the haulage tunnel.

"They aren't trying to climb into the mine," Lucía whispered. "They’re horizontal drilling into the aquifer cap."

"If they puncture the hydraulic seal from the outside," Carmen said, her knuckles whitening around her wrench, "the negative pressure from their high-volume pumps will pull the entire geothermal reservoir into their brine pits before dawn. The heat exchangers will run dry. The temperature down here will equalize with the surface in six hours."

Outside, the frost was creeping through the iron gates of the town, locking every valve, splintering every drum. Down here, sixty-four lives depended on the invisible thermal heart beating beneath the rock.

A sudden shout echoed from the darkness of the lower transit drift. 

Mateo appeared, his breath billowing in ragged clouds, his face black with soot and road grime. His left hand was bound in greasy canvas, held stiffly against his chest, and his eyes were wild with exhaustion.

"Lucía!" he gasped, stumbling on the ties and catching himself against a timber prop. "Shaft Nine! The inspection hatch on the lower sump... the seal is blowing out! The suction from the west is pulling the steam backward down the vents!"

Lucía looked at the rows of sleeping children, at the elders huddled against the whistling pipes, at Don Efraín, who was watching her with the terrifying, total trust of a man who had nowhere left to run.

"Get the timber dogs and the lead wedges," she said, her voice dropping into the cold, flat cadence of an order. "We're going down."
