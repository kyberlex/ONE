The salt-glaze on the optics housing was three millimeters thick and growing.

Carmen Morales scraped the brass spatula across the quartz window, her knuckles aching inside two layers of sheared-wool liners. Each pass came away fouled with crust: airborne lithium carbonate and volcanic flour, fused into a milky rind by the sub-zero wind howling through the headframe. Forty-five hundred meters up, the atmosphere didn't carry enough air to breathe without a knot behind your ribs, but it carried enough razor-grit to blind a solid-state emitter in six minutes flat.

Below her boots, deep in the timbered throat of the haulage shaft, the signal bell rang three short strokes, paused, then struck two.

*Contamination in the wet intake.* 

Carlos was still down on Level Forty. His voice through the dry-cell acoustic tube was thin, stripped of timbre by the half-mile drop. "Aviation fuel. Amber cut, smells like refined turbine wash. Someone tipped a drum into the surface gravel beds above the upper gallery. It’s sitting on the cold-trap jacket."

Carmen clamped the receiver between her jaw and collarbone, not breaking the rhythm of her scraping. "Is the aeroponic loop sealed?"

"Bypass is seated," Carlos wheezed. His breathing had that wet, rattle-and-drag scrape she recognized from the tailing flumes at El Salvador, twenty years ago—the lung-burn of men who spent their youths inhaling powdered quartz before anyone thought to wet down the drill faces. "We're running on the internal reservoir. But the misting headers won't clear forty hours without the return condensator. If the fuel reaches the aerators, the roots drown in kerosene."

"Hold the drain valves closed with the iron drift pins," she ordered. "Don't let them vent the sump until we've locked the ledger. If the parity drops now, the pump gates drop back to the corporate utility's default lockouts. Detroit goes dark. Susa goes dark. We lose the line."

She didn't wait for his answer. She snapped the brass dog back into its groove, pulled the leather storm-flap over the quartz lens, and ducked into the signal shack.

Inside, the only light came from the blue phosphorescent paint on the mechanical balance scales and the dull, amber glow of the twin cathode sweeps. The room smelled of hot vacuum tubes, boiled tea, and the sour wool of five people who hadn't taken their boots off since Tuesday.

Mateo sat bent over the manual rotary dial, his left hand—short an index finger since an exploratory drill rig snapped its winch line in the late nineties—clamping the copper patch cables against the grounding strip. 

"The beam dropped four degrees off true," Mateo said without looking up. His lips were blue around the gums from soroche. "The wind is twisting the headframe steel. The Bolivian relay on Mount Sajama is dancing like a drunk spider. We’re losing forty packets out of every hundred."

"Shift the gimbal three teeth north," Carmen said, stripping her outer gloves with her teeth. She pulled a thick ledger of mimeographed coordinate tables from beneath a flat pig-iron ballast block. "The cold air coming down from the salar creates a thermal inversion layer over the salt flats. It bends the light downward like a bent straw in a glass of brine."

She didn't need a folk tale to tell her how light warped through an altiplano freeze. In her third year working the leaching ponds at Chuquicamata, she had ruined two optical survey transits before realizing the freezing sulfate mist functioned as an unground concave prism. She had learned it with frostbitten fingers and a docked pay envelope, calculating refractive indices by hand on the back of greasy grease-gun cartons.

"Gimbal shifted," Mateo grated. He worked the brass worm screw with his three good fingers, his palm braced against the timber upright to stop his tremors. "Signal carrier rising. But it’s garbage. Detroit’s telemetry is arriving split."

On the paper-feed spool, the stylus was chattering violently, spitting purple ink across the grid paper in stuttering, broken arcs.

*DET_01 -> SUSA_02 -> ATACAMA_03.*

Three continental basins, each holding its balance by an invisible thread of mutual consumption and return. If Detroit took more water from the lower glacial aquifers than its shared regenerative quota allowed, the hydro-electric interlocks on the Italian alpine flumes would trip their sluices to force a compensatory draw. If Atacama's deep geothermal steam dried out, Detroit’s industrial kilns were flagged to shed fifty megawatts to preserve the planetary thermal envelope. It was not an alliance of trust; it was an alliance of cold, thermodynamic necessity, anchored by numbers that could not be printed twice.

And right now, the numbers were slipping on the ice.

"First checksum failed," Mateo said, ripping the paper tape. "The parity hash doesn't clear. Detroit says they discharged six hundred cubic meters through the north turbine at hour seventy-one. Our paper says seven hundred and forty. The hash is fractured."

"Atmospheric jitter," Carmen said, pulling a brass drafting compass from her breast pocket. "The beam stuttered during the burst. Re-read the parity column from the third pulse group. Read the dots, ignore the tails."

She leaned over his shoulder, her eyes burning from the salt dust. Her thumb dragged across the rough newsprint tape. *One, zero, one, one, zero.* The ink had bled where the paper took on moisture from the cold-drip in the ceiling.

"They didn't discharge seven hundred," she muttered, her nail indenting the pulp. "The optical receiver caught a double-reflection off the cloud bank over the pass. It’s an echo. Strike the last three registers. Reconcile by hand."

Mateo’s three-fingered hand flew across the mechanical punch-key, punching the corrected tally onto the brass return ribbon. "Second checksum sent. Waiting on Susa."

The silence in the shack became complete, save for the rhythmic *clack-clack-whir* of the governor weights on the optical transmitter clockwork. Outside, the wind hit the corrugated zinc siding with the sound of shovel-loads of gravel hurled against a bulkhead.

Thirty seconds. Forty.

Then the return bell chimed—a single, clean strike of lead on bronze.

"Susa accepted," Mateo whispered. "They agree with the strike. But Detroit isn't confirming. The Great Lakes link is lagging. Their ground station is reporting five centimeters of wet sleet on their parabolic array."

"Don't wait for their automated return," Carmen said. "Drop to the low-frequency acoustic channel. Send the clearance sequence via the transatlantic copper cable reserve."

"That line has sixty ohms of resistance per mile," Mateo protested, his bloodshot eyes widening. "The signal will be slurred into soup."

"Then we listen for the cadence, not the tone," she snapped. "Listen to the relays clatter. We built the circuit; we know the weight of the brass."

Down in the yard, two hundred yards below the headframe shack, a horn sounded.

It was not the community's hand-cranked copper siren. It was the dry, flat blast of an air-horn from an AndesGuard eight-wheeled scout truck. The sound bounced off the salt walls of the open pit, jagged and ugly.

Lucía’s voice came up the speaking tube, tight and dry. "Carmen. Two trucks through the lower chain-link. They aren't using rifles. They've chained a steel drag-shoe to the intake manifold of the main geothermal riser. They’re trying to wrench the well-head sideways off its concrete pad. If the casing shears, the steam vents into the open air. The whole gallery loses pressure in four minutes."

"Can the gravel sluice reach them?" Carmen asked, her voice dropping into the flat, dangerous calm that comes when panic has burned all the fat off a mind.

"Carlos has his hand on the trip-lever," Lucía answered. "Ten tons of coarse copper slag above their front axles. But if he drops it, he buries the lower bypass valve permanently. We’ll never get down there to clear the kerosene."

"Tell Carlos to wait for my third bell," Carmen said. "Not before."

"Carmen, the trucks are revving their winches—"

"Tell him to wait!"

She turned back to the punch-tape. "Mateo. Third checksum. What did Detroit send?"

Mateo’s face was gray. The paper tape was twisting in his fingers. "It’s corrupted again. The sleet in Michigan broke the carrier wave mid-stream. We have half of their caloric ledger. The Susa node is timing out. We have eighty seconds before the entire three-node network defaults to isolation mode."

If isolation mode tripped, the regional power grid’s automatic lockouts would engage, turning the mine's emergency pumps into dead cast iron. The private water utility in Antofagasta would reclaim the rights to the deep wells by statutory default under the emergency code, their legal receivers stepping in behind the AndesGuard bumper-bars.

Carmen didn't reach for a radio. She didn't look at the sand-timer. She looked at the last five inches of punched tape, where the purple ink was smeared into three horizontal tracks.

She had spent six months in the machine shops of the northern rail line when she was twenty-four, listening to the telegraph clanking out train weights on worn-out spring steel. You didn't read the dots when the wires were iced; you felt the drag of the solenoid return spring.

"The third register isn't a zero," she said, her voice dropping an octave. "It’s a seven. Detroit’s boiler houses are running their secondary steam dumps to clear their own ice. They didn't lose the packets—they shifted their load to the local foundry loop."

"We can't guess, Carmen," Mateo swallowed, his throat clicking. "If we confirm a false balance, the Class Zero invariant triggers an automatic quarantine. We lock ourselves out of the ledger for thirty days."

"I'm not guessing. Detroit’s standard operating shift changes at midnight local. Their foundries take the baseline steam when the residential heaters back off. It’s a seven."

She seized the heavy brass punch-bar from his hand. Her fingers were stiff with cold, the dry skin split along the knuckles where the alkaline lime had eaten through the creases. She aligned the steel die over the third register, drew her elbow back, and struck the punch with the heel of her bare palm.

The iron bit into the brass tape with a sharp *clink*.

"Feed it," she ordered.

Mateo shoved the ribbon into the optical scanner's drive sprockets. He cranked the heavy iron flywheel by hand, his shoulder muscles knotting against the cold grease in the gears.

The beam fired. Through the small mica inspection port, Carmen saw the infrared flash leap from the headframe—a tight, invisible lance of heat piercing the salt-haze toward the frozen crags of the Sajama pass.

Outside, the winches in the lower yard began to scream—high-tensile steel cables pulling taut against thirty tons of unreinforced geothermal riser. The shack floor shuddered beneath them.

The mechanical receiver clicked once.

Twice.

Then the bronze drum began to turn, not with the erratic chatter of lost sync, but with the slow, massive, deliberate beat of a three-way hydraulic lock.

*DETROIT_01: VERIFIED.*  
*SUSA_02: VERIFIED.*  
*ATACAMA_03: SETTLED.*

The three continental ledgers snapped together, the balance holding across twelve thousand miles of cold rock, dark fiber, and salt-choked air. On the lower battery racks, the green contactors dropped with a sound like sledgehammers hitting hard rubber. The local utility’s remote interlocks died, their radio circuits bypassed by the direct mutual ledger now burning through the headframe’s beam.

The power stayed on. The pumps hummed, their deep, forty-bar vibration rising through the shaft timbers like the purr of a waking beast.

Carmen didn't pause to breathe. She lunged for the speaking tube, caught the brass bell-pull, and struck it three times.

"Carlos!" she screamed into the copper horn. "Drop the slag!"

From deep in the gorge below, even over the howling of the gale, came the dry, thunderous roar of ten thousand pounds of blasted copper ore avalanching down the chute. A metallic crump echoed through the rocks—the sound of an eight-wheeled steel chassis buckling under dead weight—followed immediately by the high-pressure hiss of high-octane fuel spraying across red-hot manifold pipes.

Then, through the floorboards, came the secondary shudder.

Not an avalanche. Not a diesel backfire.

A deep, subterranean hollow thump that Carmen felt not in her ears, but in the marrow of her shins.

Mateo froze, his hand still on the punch-flywheel.

The mechanical balance scales on the desk—the ones measuring the physical head-pressure in the four-thousand-meter deep shaft—didn't swing toward zero. They slammed hard against their upper stops, the brass needles bending double against the retaining pins as the dial glass blew out in a shower of square green crystals.

Carmen looked down at the acoustic tube. 

A blast of superheated air, reeking of ancient marsh gas and boiling sulfur, screamed out of the copper horn, spitting black grease across the wall.

"That wasn't the truck," Mateo whispered, his eyes wide in the phosphor glow. "The riser didn't shear at the surface. It blew from the bottom."
