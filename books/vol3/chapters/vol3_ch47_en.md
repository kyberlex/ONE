The salt on Carmen’s eyelashes had frozen into needle-sharp beads that scraped her corneas every time she blinked. 

Her field terminal was wedged between the dashboard grab-handle and the cracked windshield of the winch-truck, its screen pulsing with a sick, amber cadence. 

*167:52:14 UTC.*

Seven minutes and forty-six seconds. If the line-of-sight laser link died before the cycle struck the one-hundred-and-sixty-eighth hour, the entire seven-day metabolic ledger would drop into null-state. The network would treat the mountain as an abandoned lung, a collapsed pocket of air, and default to quarantine. Seven continuous days of thermodynamic proof—every liter drawn from the deep-shaft geothermal vents, every calorie grown in the aeroponic dark, every watt harvested from the brine flats—erased in the lag between two dropped frames.

"Hold the steering column!" Mateo shouted above the scream of the heavy diesel winch. 

He didn’t wait for her answer. The mechanic lunged across the cab, thrusting his left hand—the one missing its index finger—into the greasy linkage of the mechanical throttle override. His palm was wrapped in stiff electrical tape to protect a fresh chemical burn from the battery electrolyte, the adhesive black and peeling in the sub-zero draft. "The cable's bunching on the drum! If the coils overlap, the winch will sheer the axle!"

Outside the cab, the high-tension steel tow-cable shrieked like an unholy cello string. Fifty yards down the slag ramp, shrouded in the alkaline dust churned up by twenty thousand pairs of boots, the battered twin-engine turboprop shuddered as it was dragged upward. Its wings, scabbed with patched sheet-aluminum and secured with pop-rivets, flexed sickeningly under the gradient. 

Carmen didn’t look down at the plane. Her world had narrowed to the three-millimeter crosshair on her display.

*Packet drop: 71%.*

The headframe tower on the saddle above them was tilting. Through the binoculars hung around her neck, she could see the eastern guy-wire whipping loosely in the gale like a severed tendon. Whoever had sabotaged the mountain anchor had done it with a pneumatic cut-off wheel; the clean, sheared face of the four-inch turnbuckle dangled five hundred feet up the iron lattice. Without that tension, the tower was leaning two degrees northward every minute, its infrared beam drifting off the optical relay perched on the distant Bolivian ridge.

"Sofia!" Carmen keyed the dash radio, her voice rasping against the chalky dryness in her throat. She unconsciously reached into her breast pocket, thumbing the smooth, rounded bead of an unpolished amber rosary she kept stripped of its cross—an old habit from her graduate seasons in the Atacama, using the resin to test for static charges in the hyper-arid air. "I need three degrees of down-tilt on the primary emitter gimbal. The software limiters are blocking the servo command!"

Sofia’s voice came back through the speaker, chopped into jagged syllables by the radio jammer the tactical convoy was running from the valley floor. "*—an’t... override... the local board is locked in... freeze-protection mode... heat-pipe is frozen at the base!*"

"Then bypass the logic gate with the manual trim wheel on the gantry!" Carmen yelled.

"*There’s no railing on the north platform, Carmen! The wind—*"

"If that beam drops before 168:00:00, we don't need a railing!" Carmen shouted back, her fingers flying across the cold rubber keys of her console. "We won't have an airfield, we won't have a charter, and the sortition credentials will evaporate before Carlos hits the clouds!"

Through the windshield, she saw Carlos Quispe emerge from the swirling white salt-fog beside the winch drum. He had discarded his parka to move faster, wearing only his grease-blackened woolen vest over an indigo work shirt. He moved with a strange, heavy economy, his boots crunching rhythmically into the crust of compacted iron tailings. He didn't shout. He carried an iron spud-bar, using the five-foot lever to manually guide the groaning steel cable across the drum tracks, inches from where the revolving drum would have ground a man’s ribs to flour. 

Every heave of his shoulders was followed by a wet, rattling gasp—the sound of lungs that had spent thirty years breathing crystalline quartz dust. He caught Carmen’s eye through the glass, his face gray beneath the red sun-scald, and gave a single, downward nod of his chin. 

*Keep the light alive.* That was all the nod meant.

Carmen looked down at the ledger clock.

*167:55:02 UTC.*

The optical link flickered. A single crimson warning flag bloomed across the terminal: *DETROIT: PING LOST. BUFFER COMPACTION RUNNING AT 88%.*

Her stomach dropped, a cold hollow opening beneath her ribs. "No, no, no..."

In her mind's eye, she saw the winter-locked riverfront four thousand miles to the north: the steam vents of the old automobile plants, the frozen copper cables strung across the roofs, some tired watch-engineer staring at an identical green phosphor cursor, waiting for the southern hemisphere to prove it was still breathing. Detroit’s storage nodes had carried the northern balance through four days of blizzards; if the Atacama failed to verify its own hydrologic generation now, Detroit's automated circuits would assume an invalid harvest and roll the ledger back to the last epoch.

"Sofia!" Carmen grabbed her tool harness, kicking open the rusted cab door. 

The wind hit her like an iron shovel. At four thousand meters, the air carried no moisture, only the caustic bite of pulverized borax and lithium carbonate that made her throat close involuntarily. Her eyes watered instantly, the moisture freezing on her cheeks before she reached the base of the headframe tower.

The service ladder was crusted with rime-frost. Thirty feet up, clinging to the steel angle-iron with one arm while fighting a frozen bronze wing-nut with a pipe wrench, was Sofia. Her woolen scarf had unraveled, whipping behind her like a dark pennant against the cobalt sky. Her knuckles were split, blood freezing dark and thick across the back of her hands before it could drip.

"The spindle is seized!" Sofia screamed down through the gale, her teeth chattering so violently the words collided. "The salt... it got into the worm gear!"

Carmen swung her boots onto the first rungs of the ladder. Her thighs burned, the high-altitude hypoxia turning her leg muscles into dead wood. With every rung, the vast white expanse of the salar expanded below her—twenty thousand people standing in absolute, terrified silence along the edges of the brine ponds, their upturned faces pale points against the turquoise evaporation terraces. They weren't cheering. They weren't waving banners. They were holding their breath, watching the iron-slag haul road where the twin-engine turboprop was slowly being dragged toward the brink of the open-pit chasm.

She reached the gantry platform, her breath tearing out of her chest in ragged whistles. The platform swayed beneath them, a sickening, elastic shimmy as the damaged tower leaned under the wind shear.

"Let me," Carmen gasped, shoving her shoulder against Sofia’s side to brace her against the lattice.

She reached into her coat, pulled out the heavy brass aerosol canister of high-dielectric contact cleaner, and jammed the plastic straw straight into the worm-gear housing. She held the nozzle down until the solvent hissed, foaming white as it dissolved the alkaline salt crust.

"Now!" Carmen gripped the cold iron rim of the manual trim wheel with both hands. "Turn it with me!"

They threw their combined weight against the spokes. The metal screeched, an agonizing brass-on-steel groan, then snapped forward a quarter-turn.

A hundred yards above them, at the very peak of the headframe, the optical transceiver housing clicked. The heavy quartz lens tilted downward by two degrees, catching the line of the high-altitude pass.

Through the radio clipped to Carmen's chest harness, Mateo’s voice erupted through a burst of static: "*LINK RESTORED! Detroit is pinging back! Susa has the carrier wave! Malacca is reading thirty-four percent optical clarity through the Pacific bounce!*"

Carmen collapsed against the steel ladder railing, her chest heaving, her forehead resting against the freezing iron. 

*167:58:44 UTC.*

Below them, on the slag incline, the turboprop had reached the flat bench of the haul ramp—the final fifty yards of level ground before the mountain simply stopped, plunging five hundred vertical meters down into the dry boulder wash of the canyon below.

The plane’s twin turbine engines howled into life, spitting plumes of blue-white smoke as they ingested the volatile bio-methanol distilled from the valley's agricultural waste. The props beat the thin air into a frantic roar that echoed off the rock walls like cannon fire.

Inside the cockpit, she could see Lucía Mamani through the perspex, her hands clamped over the navigation manifests, her face pale as lard. In the left seat, an old transport pilot from the mining service was already standing on the brakes, the aircraft shuddering against the blocks while the engines spooled toward maximum thrust.

"The winch cable is off!" Mateo roared over the channel. "Carlos cut the bridle! They're on their own power!"

*167:59:30 UTC.*

Thirty seconds.

Carmen watched the terminal screen strapped to her chest harness. The ledger lines were cascading now, an avalanche of cryptographic verification hashes:

*DETROIT_NODE_01: SYNC_CONFIRMED.*  
*SUSA_VALLEY_NODE_02: SYNC_CONFIRMED.*  
*STRAIT_MALACCA_NODE_03: SYNC_CONFIRMED.*

*167:59:57...*  
*167:59:58...*  
*167:59:59...*  

*168:00:00 UTC.*

Across the display, the red perimeter rings dissolved into deep, steady emerald. Gate 1 was formally closed, sealed by seven days of unbroken physical proof. Gate 2 locked into the global ledger, flashing its irrevocable sortition mandate across four continents.

"It cleared," Sofia whispered, tears cutting clean black channels through the salt-crust on her cheeks. "Carmen. It cleared."

Down on the iron slag, the pilot released the toe brakes.

The turboprop lunged forward, gathering speed along the three-hundred-foot strip of pulverized stone. The tail came up, but the nose gear was still pounding violently against the slag ruts, bounced by the mountain crosswind. 

One hundred feet from the edge.

Seventy feet.

The plane hadn’t reached rotation speed; the thin air wasn't catching the wings. 

"Pull up," Carmen whispered, her fingers digging into the steel rung of the gantry until her nails bent. "Pull up..."

The aircraft struck the lip of the five-hundred-meter precipice at full throttle. The nose wheel left the gravel, hanging for a terrifying half-second over the empty abyss, and then the right wing dropped sickeningly into the thermal updraft as a violent bang tore through the starboard engine cowling, trailing a dark streamer of fire into the void.
