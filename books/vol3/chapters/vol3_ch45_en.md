The wind off the salt pan tasted of pulverized gypsum and burnt diesel, coating the roof of Katherine Webb’s mouth like dry flour. She had covered copper strikes in Antofagasta and pipeline collapses in Neuquén, but at four thousand meters, the silence carried an unfamiliar physical weight. Her recorder was taped to the end of a bent aluminum monopod with black electrical friction tape, its foam windscreen torn away two days ago by the gale off the cordillera.

Beside her on the gravel embankment, twenty other foreign correspondents shoved their telephoto lenses against the chain-link perimeter of the Salar Mining administrative compound. Down in the basin, the vast white flat of the Atacama shimmered like a frozen sea under the midday sun. And cutting across that white expanse was a dark, rippling ribbon five miles long: twenty thousand people walking without banners, without megaphones, carrying only blue plastic water cans, terracotta pitchers, and galvanized pails.

Behind the chain-link fence, the corporate compound was dying in silence.

The four towering diesel generators that had hummed day and night for six years had sputtered out fifteen minutes earlier, starved of their automated fuel valve triggers when the digital credit lines severed. Without the engines, Katherine could hear the sharp *ping-ping* of hot exhaust manifolds contracting in the high-altitude chill.

The door of the double-wide operations trailer swung open, banging once against the corrugated siding.

Arthur Brand stepped onto the steel landing. The chief liquidator dispatched from Frankfurt by the consortium’s primary bondholders looked less like an imperial plunderer and more like an undertaker whose wagon had broken down in the desert. His gray linen suit was stiff with salt rime around the ankles. His collar was damp with cold sweat, his thinning hair plastered sideways by the wind. In his right hand he carried an accordion folder bound with frayed black elastic; in his left, a satellite handset that beeped continuously with the flat, metronomic cadence of a dropped carrier signal.

A French reporter from *Le Monde* thrust a microphone through the links of the fence. "Mr. Brand! Is the consortium declaring insolvency under Chapter Eleven or municipal receivership?"

Brand didn't flinch. His eyes were bloodshot, bruised with the grey fatigue of a man who had watched seven billion dollars in tradeable paper turn into unroutable ghost-bits while sitting on a chemical toilet in an Atacama trailer.

"There is no Chapter Eleven," Brand said. His voice was cracked, stripped of press-office varnish, barely rising above the whistle of the wind. "The Frankfurt clearinghouse declined the bridge loan at seven this morning. Amsterdam followed at eight. The reserve accounts are frozen under the Hague maritime conservatorship order. We have no operational liquidity."

"What about the extraction permits?" Katherine shouted, leaning against the cold wire mesh, her lungs aching from the thin, bitter air. "The provincial court in Antofagasta issued an injunction protecting the wells!"

Brand let out a short, dry sound that was almost a laugh. He looked toward the ridge where the convoy of armored gun-trucks sat idling, their dark windscreens reflecting the harsh sun.

"The provincial court does not supply diesel," Brand said softly. "The security contractor informed me five minutes ago that their wire transfers from Zurich were returned as unpayable. The guards are collecting their kit. Salar Mining Consortium Chile S.A. ceased active operations at twelve hundred hours."

A muffled clang echoed from the far gate.

Captain Rivas, the commander of the private security detachment, was not waiting for an orderly transition. His men were already throwing duffel bags into the beds of three unarmored utility pickups. They left the heavy armored transports where they sat, fuel needles resting on empty. No shots were fired. No tear gas canisters were primed. When the money stopped, the perimeter simply ceased to exist. Rivas climbed into the lead pickup, slammed the door, and the truck skidded out through the main gate, throwing a rooster-tail of white alkaline dust across the chain-link before disappearing down the western switchbacks toward the coast.

Katherine dropped her monopod and hauled herself through the gap where the security gate had been kicked open. The press pool surged after her, boots crunching on the brittle salt crust.

From the northern ridge, the first line of the community procession had reached the wellhead flumes.

Carlos Quispe walked at the front. His woolen poncho was gray with dust, his canvas-wrapped hands resting lightly on the rough shoulder of Sofia Arismendi. Sofia’s face was pale from the soroche, her steps slow and deliberate, but her gaze was pinned to the headframe tower where the infrared optical transceiver continued to pulse its steady, amber glow.

Behind them came the miners from the lower shafts, the women of Toconao in embroidered shawls, elders with skin cured by a lifetime of ultraviolet glare, and young mechanics wearing faded grease-stained denim. They did not rush the gates. They did not chant. They carried water.

A young indigenous girl, no older than ten, walked up to the dead intake manifold of Extraction Well Number Four. She knelt in the dust, tipped a chipped enamel pitcher, and poured a pint of clear, cold meltwater into the dry, rusted drain.

"Why are they doing that?" whispered the German stringer beside Katherine, frantically adjusting his shutter speed.

"They aren't destroying it," Katherine murmured, watching Carlos approach the operations trailer. "They're baptizing it."

Arthur Brand remained on the trailer steps. He did not run. He held out the frayed accordion folder as Carlos came to a halt five feet below him.

Between them stood no police, no court bailiffs, and no barricades. Only the wind and the white dust.

"The physical ledger of plant assets," Brand said, his German-accented English flat and exhausted. "Pumps, brine pans, reverse-osmosis arrays, electrical switchgear. The consortium’s debts remain with the parent entity in Luxembourg. The physical apparatus... by order of the international sortition decree validated at twelve hundred... falls to the municipal trust of the San Pedro watershed."

Carlos looked at the thick paper documents bound in the folder. He did not reach for them immediately. His knuckles, scarred from decades of drill kickbacks, twitched inside their canvas wraps.

"The water rights," Carlos said in his quiet, low baritone. "The deep aquifer licenses."

"Extinguished," Brand replied. "Voided by systemic default under the international registry. You are the receivers. If that is what you call yourselves."

"We are not receivers," Sofia said, stepping forward. Her voice shook with altitude sickness, but her chin remained high. "We are the watershed."

Dr. Carmen Morales pushed through the crowd, carrying an air-gapped field terminal running off a suitcase solar pack. Her face was taut, her spectacles smeared with dried sweat. She didn't look at the corporate liquidator; she looked at Sofia and Carlos.

"The amber packet," Morales said, her breath hitching in her throat. "The signal from Geneva wasn't just the default confirmation."

The reporters pressed closer, directional microphones bristling like porcupine quills around the three of them. Katherine shoved her recorder within inches of Morales’s shoulder.

"Explain," Carlos said.

"The global sortition drum completed the class-zero selection for the ratification tribunal," Morales said, reading from the liquid-crystal display. "To formally seal the municipal transfer before the World Trade Organization attempts a secondary asset freeze, three trustees from this watershed must be chosen by lot. Immediately. Today."

Morales turned toward the open bed of a municipal flatbed truck that had pulled up behind the crowd. Bolted to its wooden floorboards was the community’s brass sortition drum—the same scarred, hand-cranked cylinder used for generations to assign irrigation canal shifts among the ayllus.

"Three names," Morales whispered. "Odd parity. The global assembly in Geneva is convening at midnight European time to certify the maritime trust. If the local delegacy is not seated in the hall to sign the physical covenant, the commercial courts in London will place the assets under private receivership by default."

The crowd parted in absolute, breathless silence.

An elder from Peine—an old man named Don Tomás, whose eyesight was nearly gone from trachoma—climbed onto the flatbed. He spun the brass drum three times. The dry wooden beans inside tumbled with a sound like desert stones sliding down a dry wash.

He reached inside through the spring-loaded hatch.

He drew the first painted bean.

"Lucía Mamani," Don Tomás called out into the thin mountain wind.

From the edge of the crowd, the clinic nurse who had spent thirty-six hours triaging dehydrated elders in the subterranean shelter looked up, her face frozen in disbelief.

Don Tomás spun the drum again.

"Mateo Huamán."

The young mechanic with the missing finger stood frozen beside a tractor, his wrench still dangling from his belt.

A third time the drum spun, the brass catching the blinding glare of the salt.

Don Tomás pulled his hand free and squinted at the carved initials.

"Carlos Quispe."

Carlos did not move. He looked down at his brine-burned hands, wrapped in frayed rags, and then out across the twenty thousand people who had walked into the desert to reclaim their water.

Arthur Brand watched the old miner, his hand still extended with the accordion folder. "There is a charter transport waiting at the airstrip in Calama," the liquidator said quietly. "Fuel was pre-paid by the trust registry before the lines went down. You have two hours to clear the pass."

Morales looked at her terminal, and then her hand snapped to Carlos’s arm.

"Carlos," she said, her voice dropping into an urgent, terrifying register that only Katherine’s recorder caught. "Look at the routing on the flight clearance."

Carlos glanced down at the green screen.

The manifest showed the flight path from Calama to Santiago, and then non-stop to Geneva. But flashing beneath the international clearance was a red sovereign interdiction notice just logged by the maritime clearinghouse in Valparaíso: a private commercial warrant filed forty seconds ago, seeking the immediate impoundment of the delegates the moment their tires touched the tarmac.
