The red flashing cursor on Morales’s field terminal burned like a dropped flare against the pale salt crust. 

Carlos Quispe pressed his left thumb hard into the hollow beneath his collarbone, the small, habitual pressure he used whenever the silicosis began to claw at the bottom of his lungs. His chest felt like an old bellows lined with crushed glass. Over Morales’s shoulder, the line of text remained pitiless: *INTERDICTION ORDER / MARITIME LIEN 44-B / SANTIAGO AIRPORT RUNWAY DETENTION ON LANDING.*

"It’s not a transit stop," Lucía Mamani said. Her voice was flat, dried out by thirty-six straight hours of cleaning alkaline burns from children’s wrists in the mine clinic. She still wore her oil-stained canvas smock, her thumbs hooked into her pockets to keep them from trembling. "It’s a corral."

Arthur Brand did not take his eyes off the accordion file tucked beneath his arm. The wind off the high salt pan had stripped the last trace of city dignity from him; his gray woolen coat was stiff with white dust, and his ears were chapped crimson. "The creditor syndicates filed it the moment the optical relay locked onto the Geneva registry," the liquidator said, his breath hitching in the four-thousand-meter air. "Under the maritime transit code, any charter departing a domestic strip under provincial dispute can be grounded for asset verification at its first refueling point. If your wheels touch Santiago, the carabineros will impound the cabin. You will sit on the asphalt until the clock in Switzerland runs out."

"Then we don't refuel in Santiago," Mateo Huamán muttered. The young mechanic’s left hand, missing its index finger since a haul-truck hydraulic line had sheared through his glove three winters back, stayed tucked into the waistband of his trousers. His teeth were chattering, not from fear, but from the sudden crash of adrenaline after four days without sleep. "The twin-turboprop at the municipal strip has auxiliary bladders. We skip the valley entirely. We climb over the ridge, hug the eastern face of the cordillera, and cross into Salta or La Paz."

"You can't clear the pass on aux bladders with twenty-two knot crosswinds," Sofia Arismendi rasped from the fender of the flatbed. Her soroche had turned her lower lip a bruised violet, and she gripped an iron stay to keep her knees from giving way. "Not without the radio beacons. And the national aviation authority has already pulled the transponder relays from Calama to the border."

Carlos looked past them, out across the blinding white expanse of the salar.

Twenty thousand people stood in the rising wind. They were not an army. They were pit mechanics with chemical burns on their forearms, grandmothers wearing three layers of felt skirts against the sub-zero chill, and young drill hands whose boots were tied together with baling wire. In their hands, held close against bellies and chests, were vessels: tin lard buckets, cracked plastic carboys, clay jugs turned dark with well-water, glass cider bottles stopped with twists of rag. 

They were not shouting. The silence of the high desert was immense, broken only by the rhythmic clanking of the copper mine headframe behind them, where the salvaged optical laser transceiver hummed against its steel gantry, casting an invisible thread of light toward the north.

They were waiting for Carlos to step down. They were waiting to be told whether the bean drawn from the brass drum meant salvation or another generation of watching their daughters’ teeth turn yellow from fluoride and arsenic while the tailing ponds swallowed the bofedales.

Carlos looked down at his own hands. The brine had eaten deep fissures into the skin of his knuckles, sealed over with tallow and wrapped in coarse strips of cotton flour sacks. When he was nine years old, before the lithium consortium had laid its first black plastic pipelines across the salt grass, his mother had taught him to measure the seasonal flow of the meltwater streams with dry llama sinew. If the sinew tightened, the snowpack was holding; if it went slack before the feast of San Juan, the herd would have to be moved ten leagues south into the high scree. He had never forgotten the texture of that wet gristle between his fingers—the living knowledge that water was never property, only a guest passing through the stone on its way to the sky.

He turned to Morales. "Give me the speaker."

The physician reached into the truck cab and hauled out an old public-address horn mounted to a wooden block, its battered wire spliced into the battery terminals of the flatbed with two rusted alligator clips. She handed him the plastic microphone. Her fingers brushed his; they were cold as creek stones.

"Tell them what the covenant means, Carlos," she whispered. "If they do not understand what they are holding, fear will scatter them before the first convoy reaches the switchbacks."

Carlos climbed onto the bed of the ore truck. The steel plate groaned beneath his weight.

The wind hit him immediately, carrying the sulfurous stench of the geothermal vents below the ventilation shaft and the razor-edge chill of the snow on Licancabur. Twenty thousand faces turned upward. The glare of the noon sun off the salt was white and merciless, turning the crowd into a sea of dark wool and squinting eyes.

He tapped the metal grill of the microphone. A hollow *thump* echoed off the corrugated siding of the flotation mill.

"We have spent forty years asking permission to drink," Carlos said.

His voice was hoarse, rough as a rasp across hardwood, but the loudspeaker horn threw it flat and heavy across the salt flat.

"We asked the water court in Antofagasta. We asked the ministry in Santiago. We brought our deeds written on paper that had turned yellow before our grandfathers were buried, and they told us the paper was invalid because it lacked a tax stamp from an office that closed when the copper boom died."

In the front row, an elder from San Pedro lifted an earthenware jug with both hands, holding it high against her chest.

"They took the deep brine to float the batteries of cities we will never see," Carlos continued, the words coming steady now, pacing the tight, painful rhythm of his breathing. "They left us the dregs in the plastic barrels that smell of diesel. And when we complained that our children could not walk without their bones aching, they showed us a code of law written by men who have never had to boil alkali to wash a newborn child."

He pointed his wrapped hand toward the high ridges of the cordillera, where the jagged teeth of the Andes tore the blue out of the sky.

"The water does not belong to the Salar Consortium," Carlos said, his voice rising, cutting through the whip of the desert wind. "It does not belong to the banks in London. It does not belong to the state. The water belongs to the rock that caught the cloud, to the moss that held the melt, and to the living bodies that drink it so they may work, love, and die upon this soil!"

A low, deep murmur rippled through the thousands, a sound like gravel shifting under a sudden flood.

"By the authority of the sortition drum," Carlos shouted into the horn, feeling the blood pound in his temples as his lungs fought the thin air, "and by the blood of every miner who rots in the deep stopes of this mountain, we declare the waters and the soil of the Atacama an open, sovereign commons. No gate shall be shut upon them. No valve shall be turned for profit. No lease shall stand above the thirst of a human child!"

He lowered the microphone.

For three heartbeats, there was only the wind scouring the white crust.

Then, from the center of the crowd, a single tin bucket struck the iron bumper of a water tractor. A second bucket clattered. Within seconds, twenty thousand people were lifting their jars, their gourds, their dented plastic cans, the dry clatter of clay against metal swelling into an immense, thunderous roar that shook the salt beneath their feet.

Carlos stepped down from the flatbed. His legs were trembling; he caught the side of the truck bed to steady himself, coughing violently into his rag until he tasted the faint copper of blood.

Morales caught him by the elbow. "Carlos. Look."

She was pointing not at the crowd, but back toward the radio mast.

On her terminal, the green optical link was flickering violently. A string of high-latency warnings chased each other across the black glass.

"Packet drop is up to sixty percent," Sofia said, her voice taut as a wire. "The optical transceiver on the headframe is drifting off its axis."

"The wind?" Mateo asked, wrench already in his left hand.

"Not the wind," Morales said, her eyes locked on the telemetry graph. "Look at the base station load cell. The tension cables on the eastern guy-wires are going slack. Someone is cutting the high-tension anchors at the saddle."

Mateo cursed and turned toward the service ladder, but Brand caught the mechanic's sleeve with surprising force.

"Leave the tower," Brand whispered, his face drawn gray as lard. "Look at the low-frequency runway monitoring channel. The airstrip in Calama just went dark. The private tactical unit didn't wait for the court in Santiago. They’ve already moved four gravel-spreaders across the tarmac. The charter can’t land."

Carlos straightened up, wiping the salt-crust and blood from his mouth with the back of his flour-sack wrap. "Then we don't use the runway."

"There is nowhere else to lift a twin-engine transport within three hundred kilometers," Sofia said, panic finally breaking through her exhaustion. "The passes are blocked, the optical link to Geneva is dying, and the sortition credentials expire in ninety minutes."

Carlos looked past the milling thousands toward the decommissioned open-pit incline—the two-mile ramp of compacted iron slag that climbed out of the copper depths toward the northern plateau.

"The ore haul road," Carlos said. "The old electric trucks used to clear forty tons on that grade. If we tow the turboprop up from the maintenance barn with the heavy diesel winches, we launch off the lip of the pit into the thermal updraft from the vents."

Mateo stared at him, his mouth open. "That ramp ends on a five-hundred-meter sheer drop into the dry wash, Carlos. If the ground speed isn't two hundred kilometers an hour when the wheels clear the edge, the plane goes straight down into the rocks."

Carlos stepped toward the truck cab, his eyes hard and clear against the white glare of the salar. "Then make sure the winches don't slip. Lucía. Mateo. Get your packs. We leave this mountain now."
