The right wing did not catch the rock. It sliced through the boiling thermal updraft of the canyon, shuddering so violently the rivets screamed against the stringers. 

On the headframe gantry, Carmen Morales leaned out over the guardrail, her breath stopped in her throat. Through the salt-streaked glass of the cockpit, five hundred feet below the cliff lip, she saw the pilot’s arms wrench the yoke against the roll. The blown starboard engine belched a single gout of soot, its prop feathering flat, but the port turbine bit into the dense cold of the valley floor. The aircraft sank another twenty feet, skittered above the dry boulder wash, and then clawed upward along the canyon wall, dragging its shadow across the red basalt.

High above the tail, the airborne optical transceiver blinked amber, searching, then snapped into a blinding, steady pulse of infrared.

On Carmen’s chest harness, the screen didn't flash or celebrate. It simply cleared. 

The laser beam from the climbing turboprop had bypassed the mountain ridges, striking the high-altitude relay on the peak above Ollagüe. The three nodes—Detroit’s iron, Susa’s stone, and the Altiplano’s salt—locked into an unbroken ring. The global assembly had opened.

"She’s in the clear," Mateo said. His voice was raw, scraped down to the lining of his throat. He lowered the heavy steel wrench he had been gripping, his missing left index finger leaving an empty gap against the handle. His knuckles were gray with dried lithium brine, the skin split around the nails. "Pilot caught the updraft off the sulphur vents. Lucía is across the border ridge."

Carmen didn't answer. She was looking down.

The Atacama was not empty.

From the southern switchbacks, where the private access roads of the Salar Mining Consortium cut like knife wounds into the white crust of the flats, a line had appeared. It was not a convoy of gun-trucks. It was a dark, moving seam across the blinding expanse of lithium carbonate, stretching past the visual horizon toward San Pedro and Calama.

Twenty thousand people were walking into the basin.

They moved on foot, shoulder to shoulder, wrapped in thick wool *aguayos* and orange mining parkas stained with grease from the Chuquicamata haul-trucks. They did not carry banners. They carried water. Clay vessels shaped from red riverbed mud, plastic five-gallon jerrycans salvaged from irrigation pumps, rusted kerosene tins scrubbed clean and filled to the brim with sweet, cold condensation drawn from the mine’s geothermal misting heads.

Along the perimeter road, three AndesGuard tactical trucks stood motionless in the salt dust. Their heavy diesel engines were dead. When the ledger’s second gate had locked at zero hundred hours, the automated clearinghouse in Santiago had suspended the cartel’s operational credit lines. The private logistics depot in Antofagasta had refused to pump fuel into unverified tanks; the contractor cards were dead plastic. 

A mercenary driver in dusty desert digital fatigues stood on his running board, his face hidden behind mirrored ballistic glasses. He watched the first wave of miners approach. His weapon stayed slung across his chest, the muzzle capped. There was no order to fire, no radio voice left on the private tactical frequencies, and no diesel to turn the heavy wheels around. The mountain heat was climbing toward thirty degrees, but the desert air was dry enough to leach the sweat from a man’s neck before it could bead. 

An elderly woman in an alpaca shawl stopped twenty feet from his front bumper, set her red clay jar on the crust, scooped half a gourd of clear water, and set it on the hot steel of his hood. She did not speak. She picked up her jar and kept walking toward the headframe.

The mercenary looked at the wet clay bowl, then at the endless line of boots crunching through the salt. He took off his helmet and sat down on the running board in the thin shade of his useless door.

By noon, the assembly at the mouth of the number four ventilation shaft was absolute.

Under the towering steel legs of the headframe, the crowd did not chant. The sheer altitude—four thousand meters above the Pacific—kept speech scarce. Every lungful of air had to be earned. The only sound was the low, rhythmic groan of the mine's main ventilation fan, powered by the rumbling subterranean steam turbine, and the rustle of dry wool against salt-stiffened canvas.

Sofia Arismendi stood beside the gantry winch, her fingers trembling against her thigh. Her soroche had settled into a brutal, rhythmic throb behind her left eye, but she refused the oxygen mask Mateo offered. Looking out over the thousands of upturned faces, she remembered her father’s kitchen table in Antofagasta, the smell of purple carbon paper as he pressed three copies of every union card into a tin breadbox beneath the floorboards. *Never let them hold the only copy,* he had told her when she was seven, his thumbs stained violet. *The law is what’s written where they can’t reach.*

Now, the law sat on an oak trestle table in the middle of the sorting yard.

It was an ancient, five-gallon flotation drum made of beaten green-patinated bronze, salvaged from the abandoned concentration plant fifty years ago. Its cylindrical face was stamped with the inspection stamps of half a century of dead copper syndicates, but its axle had been cleaned, oiled with vegetable lard, and fitted with a simple wooden crank.

Carlos Quispe stood behind the drum. His gray woolen sweater was threadbare at the elbows, the canvas rags wrapped around his cracked palms stiff with lime. For five days, he had not slept more than two hours at a stretch, his chest wheezing with the first wet rattle of silicosis. He did not look at the sky or at the crowd. He looked at the drum.

Inside were one hundred and one turned bone tokens, each cut from the knuckles of dried llama joints gathered from the high pasture commons, each inked with a single census number from the water registry. One hundred and one electors, chosen by neighborhood lot across thirty-four valley communities, representing the odd-parity quorum of the regional tier.

"The Invisible Mind has opened the seat," Carmen said. Her voice carried across the silent yard through two megaphone horns clamped to the gantry rails. "Geneva convenes one thousand and one delegates. To preserve the parity of the global circle, South America holds three voices. Not three ministers. Not three attorneys. Three lives."

Carlos placed his palms flat on the bronze cylinder. He looked at Sofia. 

"Turn," he said.

Sofia stepped forward. Her hand found the smoothed wooden crank. The mechanism groaned, the internal baffles tossing the bone tokens with a dry, clattering rattle that sounded like gravel sliding down a wash. She turned it seven full rotations, counting each turn by the pulse in her temple.

She stopped. The drop chute aligned with the brass gate at the base.

Carlos reached his bare right hand into the narrow opening. His fingers, scarred by thirty years of handling detonator cord and acid slurry, searched the dark chamber. 

He drew out the first token.

He placed it on the white salt crust of the table. Carmen bent her head, reading the scratched numerals through her magnifying loop.

"Token twenty-two," Carmen spoke into the horn. "Elena Cruz. Huatacondo basin. Agricultural water steward."

A murmuring ripple moved through the front ranks of the assembly. A woman in a dark blue sweater, her hair woven in two thick braids threaded with green yarn, stood frozen near the edge of the pit. Her skin was the color of roasted coffee, her fingers permanently stained with the red earth of the terraced potato plots. She looked around, bewildered, until the two miners beside her touched her shoulders, gently urging her forward into the clearing.

Carlos did not pause. He reached in again. His hand emerged.

"Token seventy-one," Carmen read. "Mateo Huamán. Deep-shaft geothermal technician. Pucará."

Mateo blinked. He looked down at his left hand, at the missing finger, then at the grease on his canvas trousers. He let out a ragged, half-strangled laugh that turned into a dry cough. "I have no passport," he whispered, staring at Carlos. "Carlos, my papers expired in Calama three years ago."

"You don't need their paper," Carlos murmured, his voice heavy as basalt. "You carry the ledger."

The crowd remained motionless. The mountain sun stood directly overhead, casting no shadows.

Carlos reached his hand into the bronze mouth for the final time. His fingers closed over the last bone knuckle. He did not look at it as he set it on the salt.

"Token nine," Carmen said. Her voice caught for a fraction of a second before the horn smoothed it out. "Sofia Arismendi. Legal registrar. Antofagasta."

Sofia took a step back, her boot catching on the gantry plate. The mountain seemed to tilt beneath her. "No," she said, her voice small against the wind. "The assembly needs the court filings here. The water utility will appeal the default before midnight—"

"The default is sealed in Geneva," Carlos interrupted. He stepped around the table, his scarred, brine-burned hand reaching out to clasp her shoulder with a grip forged in the underground stopes. "There are no appeals in Santiago that matter now, Sofia. The river isn't Chilean anymore. It belongs to the earth."

A low, mechanical rumble vibrated through the crust under their boots. 

From the far end of the cleared salt runway, where the old mining service strip stretched toward the sulphur mountains, a converted high-altitude transport rolled slowly out from behind the slag heaps. Its propellers were spinning lazily in the heat shimmer, its fuselage stripped bare of airline logos, painted only with the dull green primer of the state transport service.

Lucía Mamani stood on the lowered cargo ramp, waving a red marshalling flag.

The three delegates—the farmer from Huatacondo, the mechanic with nine fingers, and the lawyer who had not slept in two days—turned toward the white glare of the runway. 

Twenty thousand people raised their clay jars toward the sky, the water catching the sun like chips of polished glass.

Behind them, on the gantry screen, the first transmission from Detroit pulsed across the ledger:

*GENEVA CONVOCATION: DOORS OPEN IN EIGHTEEN HOURS.*
