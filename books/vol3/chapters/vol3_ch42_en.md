The diesel didn’t explode; it simply coagulated. At four thousand meters, with the mercury dropping through twelve below zero and the dry Andean gale stripping every scrap of engine heat, winter-grade fuel without anti-gel additives turned to cloudy lard inside the fuel lines.

Mateo Huamán felt the chill through the soles of his split-leather work boots as he scrambled down the scree below the headframe. The wind tasted of pulverized borax and old sulfur. Tucked deep inside his left coat pocket, his mutilated left hand throbbed with a dull, rhythmic ache—the stump where his index finger had been pinched off by a diamond-drill chuck nine years ago always predicted a freeze ten minutes before the salt crust began to splinter. To keep his jaw from rattling, he clamped his molars hard against a splinter of dried cedar, an old habit from his early months underground when the copper dust made men grind their enamel to powder.

Two switchbacks down, the three AndesGuard gun-trucks sat dead in the brine mud.

They hadn't retreated to the valley. They hadn't made it past the second hairpin. The lead six-wheel transport was nose-down against the gravel berm Mateo’s crew had dropped with the sluice lever an hour before. Its exhaust pipe gave off a thin, pathetic curl of gray vapor that the wind ripped to shreds. Behind it, the other two rigs were halted bumper-to-bumper, their heavy steel carcasses settled into the rutted salt like beached iron whales.

Beside Mateo, Carlos Quispe walked with short, deliberate strides, his lungs wheezing like a ruptured bellows. Under his arm, Carlos carried a scarred aluminum clipboard clamped with three sheets of blue carbon paper. He didn't carry an iron bar or a wrench. In his canvas-swaddled hands, the flimsy board looked like an absurdity, but Mateo knew what was printed on those sheets.

A man was standing in the road beside the lead truck's open hood.

He didn't look like a mercenary from an American cinema reel. Commander Gabriel Varas was forty-six years old, thick-waisted, and wore a sheepskin-lined tactical jacket stripped of national insignias. His face was the color of cured beef, cross-hatched by decades of high-altitude ultraviolet glare, with pale, sunken rings around his eyes where his ballistic goggles usually rested. A three-day frost of silver stubble coated his jaw. Between his cracked lips, an unlit filter cigarette bounced as he cursed into an acoustic throat-mic, his voice flat with the bitter, dry rasp of a career gendarme who had spent twenty years guarding copper smelters only to end up on a private extraction firm's balance sheet.

Around him, a dozen private security guards were huddled against the lee side of the steel chassis. They were shivering violently, their modular plate carriers useless against the frost penetrating through their nylon seams. One of them, a boy no older than Mateo’s nephew with grease smeared across his chin, was frantically hammering a wrench against a frozen fuel pump housing, trying to shake the wax out of the secondary filter.

"Drop the spanner, Peña," Varas said. He didn't shout. The wind took his words anyway, but the boy stopped, his shoulders sagging in defeat.

Varas turned as Mateo and Carlos reached the gravel apron. The commander’s hand drifted toward the molded holster at his right hip—a purely autonomic reflex—before his fingers curled into an empty fist and fell away.

"The satellite uplink on my dashboard just flatlined," Varas said. He didn't spit his cigarette out; he just let it hang. "My operations manager in Antofagasta isn't answering the tactical channel. The regional billing token got revoked by the maritime clearinghouse in Valparaíso fifteen minutes ago. You know what that means, miner?"

"It means your fuel cards are dead plastic," Mateo said, his teeth biting into the cedar splinter. His breath plumed white between them, freezing instantly into tiny crystals that clung to his scarf. "And the depot pump at the bottom of the switchback won't release fifty liters without an authorized corporate transponder."

Varas looked past Mateo, his bloodshot eyes taking in the hillside.

Above them, on the jagged volcanic rim that cupped the geothermal vents, figures were appearing. Not a shock line of armed militants. Just the people of the salar. Women from the Chiu Chiu irrigation co-ops in thick lliclla shawls; elder pump-tenders with their skin blackened by copper slag; young mechanics from the battery sheds. They stood fifty yards up the slope, spaced five paces apart, entirely motionless against the yellow glare of the noon sky. In their hands, they held terra-cotta jars, plastic water canisters, and patched lengths of irrigation tubing.

They didn't chant. They didn't throw stones. They just watched, five hundred pairs of dark eyes reflecting the blinding salt crust.

"My fuel tanks have ten centimeters of sludge left in the sumps," Varas said, turning back to Carlos. His voice was steady, the calculation of an engineer who knew the limits of his steel. "My auxiliary cabin heaters died twenty minutes ago. If I keep sixteen men on this ridge through sunset, three of them lose toes. By midnight, two of the boys from the coast will have pulmonary edema. You think that frightens me? I’ve pulled frozen bodies out of the high passes since the ninety-eight strike."

"We’re not waiting for midnight," Carlos said quietly. His voice was hoarse, rattling with the old dust in his bronchi, but it held the unyielding weight of granite. He held out the aluminum board. "Article Eight. Restorative Exit."

Varas didn't reach for it. "I have a commercial contract with the provincial consortium. Five million dollars in rolling assets."

"You had a contract with an extraction license that was revoked by the global sortition registry at dawn," Mateo said, stepping forward. He pulled his left hand from his pocket, laying the three-fingered wool mitten directly against the truck's cold steel fender. "The consortium’s insurance underwriter in London defaulted on your liability policy the second the optical packet cleared the ocean cable. You fire a single round up this gulley, Varas, and you aren't a private security contractor under state protection. You're an unindemnified civilian committing armed trespass on a sovereign watershed commons. There is no bail fund in Santiago that can touch you."

Varas stared at Mateo’s hand—at the empty, sewn-up wool sleeve where the forefinger should have been. A flicker of recognition passed over the commander's weathered forehead. He knew the mark of the old underground shifts. He had spent his youth policing the very shafts where men traded their joints for weekly grocery vouchers.

"You expect me to walk sixteen men down thirty kilometers of switchbacks in combat boots?" Varas asked.

"Nobody walks," Mateo said. "You leave the keys in the ignitions. We tow the transports off the salt ruts with the electric mine haulers. Your men ride down in the flatbed ore wagons to the public bus terminal in Calama. Warm. Uncuffed. With thermoses of boiled coca tea."

"And the trucks?"

"The trucks stay here," Carlos answered, tapping the blue paper. "Their heavy traction batteries are being requisitioned under the emergency watershed covenant. We need their cells to balance the night load on the geothermal condenser pumps so the greenhouse mist doesn't freeze in the dark. In exchange, the sortition assembly signs your material release. You return to the valley without an arrest warrant, and your company takes the equipment loss as a recognized commercial write-off under international maritime salvage."

A gust of wind caught the blue carbon paper, snapping it like a pistol shot against the metal board.

Varas looked at his men. Young Peña was leaning against the wheel well, his lips already turning the gray-blue shade of mountain hypoxia, his breath coming in shallow, ragged gasps. The other guards had their hands shoved under their armpits, their boots stamping an uneven, desperate rhythm on the frozen dirt.

"They'll blacklist my firm," Varas muttered, more to the freezing stones than to the miners. "Every mining board from here to Toronto."

"The consortium is already liquidating its regional payroll," Mateo said softly. "By tomorrow, there won't be a board in Santiago with an active clearing account. Sign the manifest, Commander. Before the sun drops behind the headframe and the salt takes the road."

Varas pulled a thick brass ballpoint from his pocket. He tried to click the plunger, but the internal spring was stiff with cold. He shoved the pen into his mouth, warming the grease cartridge with his tongue for ten agonizing seconds, then pulled the clipboard toward him.

He signed on the hood of the dead truck. The brass nib tore through the top sheet on the third stroke, but the blue carbon underneath caught the heavy, jagged impression of his name: *Gabriel Varas M., AndesGuard Tactical.*

He unclipped the ring of heavy magnetic ignition fobs from his belt and laid them on the aluminum frame.

"Peña," Varas barked, turning to his squad. "Magazines out. Clear the chambers. Stack the long arms in the rear cargo bin and lock the padlock. We're riding the ore trailers."

The guards moved with frantic, clumsy haste, their stiff fingers fumbling with magazine catches. The sharp, mechanical clatter of non-lethal compliance echoed across the switchback—the sound of iron yielding to the cold arithmetic of necessity.

Mateo took the clipboard. Carlos nodded once to the slope above. On the rim, four young teamsters started down the gravel bank, hauling thick braided steel recovery cables salvaged from the old copper incline.

Mateo turned to head back toward the headframe to log the battery serials into the local mesh. His boots crunched into the salt crust.

Then, from the open cab of the lead truck, a radio speaker squawked.

It wasn't the civilian band. It wasn't the tactical channel Varas had been using. It was the high-priority transponder horn—a piercing, dual-tone emergency override patched directly through the national civil aviation relay.

The voice that cut through the hiss of static was crisp, military, and hurried:

*"All regional commands, sector two. Notice of immediate airspace closure. Under ministerial decree four-one-two, all civilian and non-scheduled commercial departures from El Loa and Antofagasta are grounded until zero-eight-hundred tomorrow. Border interdiction teams to all regional tarmac gates. Subject Sofia Arismendi is flagged for immediate sovereign detention. Repeat: no transport departs for the central valley."*

Mateo froze in the middle of the road.

Down on the lower shelf, Varas stopped, one foot on the bumper of the truck, his head jerking toward the dashboard speaker.

Up at the headframe cabin, the optical laser tower caught the hard midday sun, flashing like a severed mirror. Mateo looked toward the summit where Sofia was packing the three-delegate manifesto for the flight out of Calama.

The road down the mountain was clear. But the sky had just slammed shut.
