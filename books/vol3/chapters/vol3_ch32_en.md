The shudder through the floorboards was not a rumble; it was a dry, snapping percussion that rattled Sofia’s molars in her jaw. On the monitor, the seismic line spiked jagged scarlet and then flatlined as the bedrock three hundred meters south sheared off.

"Carlos!" Sofia shouted into the mic, pressing the hard plastic casing to her mouth until her split lip stung. "Carlos, answer!"

Static hissed like dry sand poured onto sheet metal. Then came a ragged cough, followed by the clatter of a heavy iron spanner hitting rock.

"We’re off the gantry," Carlos’s voice grated back, breathless, punctuated by the wet rattle in his chest. "Mateo’s got the winch locked down. But Sofia—the old hoist. The inspection cage in Borehole Six. The counterweight cable snapped when the shelf gave way."

Sofia dropped the radio onto the rubber pad and sprinted out of the dispatch shack.

The desert night hit her like a sheet of ice. At four thousand meters, the wind off the salar carried an alkaline bite that scorched the lining of her nostrils. Across the terrace, the headframe tower groaned against its guy-wires, its lattice steel humming a low, discordant note. A single amber halogen work-light, powered by the salvaged truck batteries beneath the workshop floor, threw long, lurching shadows across the crushed stone.

Mateo was already on his knees at the shaft collar, his work jacket stiff with frozen grease. The four fingers of his right hand gripped a steel pry-bar, while the three working digits of his scarred left hand fought to seat a dog-tooth ratchet into the drum teeth.

"Is the drum holding?" Sofia demanded, skidding to a halt on the rim. The cold air burned in her bronchial tubes, bringing the familiar, sickening ache behind her temples—the soroche that never entirely left her blood.

"Drum’s holding," Mateo snapped, his breath billowing in white clouds. "Cable’s wedged in the lower sheave. Cage is jammed sixty meters down, right where the freshwater fissure meets the salt sump." He wiped a smear of brine from his eyebrow with the sleeve of his forearm. "There’s someone in it."

Sofia dropped to her knees beside him, peering into the square black mouth of the vertical shaft. The smell that billowed up was rank: damp volcanic sulfur, old iron rot, and the bitter, sharp tang of lithium brine. From far below, muffled by the sheer mass of stone, came a rhythm of dull, frantic impacts. Metal hammering on metal.

"Consortium security," Carlos said, limping up behind them. He carried a heavy coil of braided polyester rope and a battered miner’s lantern. His knuckles, wrapped in salt-stiffened canvas, trembled with exhaustion. "One of their scouts. He must have slipped down the ventilation adit before the power went dark, trying to find the manual headgate for the irrigation bypass."

"Did he drop with the rock?" Sofia asked.

"Worse," Carlos muttered, setting the lantern on the edge. The yellow beam cut only twenty meters into the vertical murk before scattering against the rising mist. "The salt shelf collapsed underneath the sump. The cage is wedged diagonally between two basalt ribs. If the brine level rises another foot, the sump overflows into the cage. He’ll freeze in the brine before dawn."

Mateo spat into the gravel. "He came up here in an armored truck with a plate carrier and a tactical radio, Arismendi. His people shut off the school’s cistern in San Pedro three days ago. Let his dispatch send a crane."

"His dispatch abandoned their posts forty minutes ago," Sofia said coldly, leaning over the pit. "Their fuel cards were cancelled. They’re halfway to Calama by now. Nobody is coming from the coast."

Mateo’s jaw clamped shut, the tendon in his cheek twitching.

"Get the secondary harness," Sofia ordered. Her voice was flat, leaving no room for the panic that fluttered against her ribs. As a girl in the coastal port of Antofagasta, she had watched longshoremen retrieve crushed stevedores from the holds of bulk carriers; she knew the smell of men trapped in dark steel. It smelled of hydraulic oil and cold urine. "We rig the manual block to the headframe A-frame. We do not leave a human being to drown in five-degree brine. Not on this mountain."

Carlos didn't argue. He moved with the slow, deliberate economy of a man who had spent forty years learning that hurry inside a mine shaft was just another name for suicide. He looped the hauling line through an old three-sheave pulley, hammered a locking wedge into the guide rail, and dropped the lead line down the dark.

It took forty minutes of brutal, bone-grinding labor to lower the recovery sling and winch the bent steel cage up through the fracture zone. The hand-winch shrieked with every turn, its gears binding on grit. Sofia leaned her weight into the auxiliary handle, her palms blistering beneath her leather gloves, her boots slipping on the rimed gravel.

When the rusted top of the inspection cage finally breached the collar, it came up tilted at a twenty-degree angle, its wire-mesh sides crusted in yellow alkali mud.

Inside, huddled on the sloshing floor plates, was a man.

He was neither large nor terrifying. Stripped of the tactical theater of his convoy, he looked hollowed-out and shrunken. His black nylon vest was soaked through, graying around the seams with crystallized salt. His left arm hung limp at his side, the shoulder visibly displaced beneath his torn windbreaker. He looked no older than twenty-five, with high, sharp cheekbones and the dark, leathery skin of the northern valleys. His lips were the color of bruised plums, chattering against his teeth with a violent, rhythmic click.

Carlos reached down, caught the collar of the scout's vest, and hauled him out onto the timber decking.

The man collapsed onto his side, curling around his injured shoulder, his chest heaving in rapid, shallow gasps. A sidearm holster at his hip was empty; his tactical radio was dead, its casing cracked and dripping gray slurry. He looked up at them with eyes wide, glassy with hypothermia, bracing his neck as if waiting for a descending boot.

"Name," Sofia said, dropping to one knee beside him.

The man swallowed, his throat making a dry clicking sound. "Varas," he whispered, his dialect carrying the flat, clipped cadence of the lower Elqui valley. "Gabriel Varas. AndesGuard... third detachment."

"Can you stand, Gabriel?"

"My shoulder... it popped when the counterweight broke." He flinched away as Mateo stepped closer. "I don't have... I don't have weapons. The holster tore off in the sump."

"Keep your hands where we can see them," Mateo said, though his tone had lost its venom, replaced by the weary disgust of an exhausted mechanic.

"Carlos, take his boots off," Sofia instructed, ignoring the scout's trembling flinch. "Mateo, get the shearling blankets from the battery shed and light the kerosene heater in the tool crib. Now."

They moved him into the small, timber-walled office adjacent to the geothermal heat exchanger. The air in the room was warm, thick with the humid, green scent of the aeroponic spinach trays stacked along the eastern wall. Sofia cut the wet nylon sleeve from Varas's left arm with a pair of curved medical shears. The skin of his shoulder was mottled white and blue, the humerus pushed forward out of its socket like a blunt stone under wet canvas.

Carlos returned carrying a chipped enamel mug of hot water steeped with mint leaves and coarse sugar. He set it on the rough pine table beside the scout.

Varas stared at the mug, not touching it. His eyes darted between the three of them—to Sofia’s ink-stained cuffs, to Carlos’s scarred hands, to the open door where the bitter wind whistled through the corrugated siding.

"What is this?" Varas muttered, his voice cracking. "You’re... you’re taking me down to the union hall in Toconao?"

"No," Sofia said, soaking a rag in warm water to wipe the corrosive salt paste from his neck and jaw. "We don't have an escort, and the coastal road is washed out below the switchbacks. You are under Restorative Quarantine. Class Zero Covenant, Article Eight."

Varas squinted, his mind struggling through the fog of shock. "A detention?"

"A quarantine," Carlos corrected, his voice raspy. He reached into his coat pocket and placed a small, turned-wood sand-timer on the table between them. The pale sand inside sat poised in its upper bulb. "You are an uninvited combatant who entered a non-commercial water reserve during a structural emergency. Under our assembly rules, we don't hold trials in the dark, and we don't put men in cages."

"Then you're turning me over to the Carabineros," Varas said, a spike of defensive panic sharpening his voice. "My contractor license... it’s registered in Santiago. I was on site security. Lawful contract."

"The Carabineros aren't coming up four thousand meters tonight, Gabriel," Sofia said quietly, gently supporting his bruised elbow while she felt the joint. "And your contractor license didn't keep your company from shutting down your fuel cards sixty minutes ago. You were abandoned down a dark shaft to drown in your own employer's runoff. Drink the water."

Varas stared at her. Slowly, awkwardly using only his right hand, he lifted the steaming enamel mug to his lips. He took one sip, then another, his throat working violently as the heat hit his empty stomach. A long, shuddering sigh tore through him, and his head dropped forward, his forehead coming to rest against the edge of the wood table.

"The panel will convene at six," Sofia told Carlos, keeping her voice low. "Three arbiters drawn from the sortition drum. One from the greenhouse shift, one from the compressor room, and you."

"I shouldn't sit on it," Carlos murmured, his gaze fixed on the floor. "My daughter... Rosa worked the solar ponds they drained last winter. I have no patience for AndesGuard."

"That is why the drum chooses, not us," Sofia said. "And the rules require three. Odd parity. If you are drawn, you sit. If not, you tend the pump."

Varas raised his head, his face pale under the lantern light, his breathing shallow. "It doesn't matter," he whispered.

Sofia paused, her fingers on the medical tape. "What doesn't matter?"

Varas looked toward the dark window, toward the mountain ridges rising unseen into the freezing Altiplano sky.

"The assembly... the water... none of it matters," the scout said, his voice flat with the terrifying calm of absolute exhaustion. "You think my team left because the payroll failed? They left because the regional superintendent didn't want to be within fifty kilometers of this basin at midnight."

Sofia felt a sudden, cold drop in the pit of her stomach. "Explain that."

"The upper tailings dam," Varas whispered, his gaze drifting to the sand-timer on the table. "At the four-thousand-eight-hundred-meter shelf. It wasn't wired to the digital network. It's a manual, counter-weighted sluice gate. Before the superintendent got in his truck, he pulled the hydraulic release pin on the main scour valve. There’s no signal to stop it. In four hours, twenty thousand tons of acidic lithium tailings are going to breach the gravel dike and hit this entire valley."
