The wind through the Abra del Viento did not howl; it hissed, skinning the shale off the ridge like fat from salt beef.

At four thousand six hundred meters, the air was a starved thing that tasted of copper slag and powdered gypsum. Mateo Huamán clamped the stub of his left index finger—lost seven years ago to a runaway diamond core-drill—deep into the shearling lining of his coat. The phantom knuckle always burned before a freeze, throbbing in time with the mule hooves clattering over the scree behind him.

Below them, five miles down the purple throat of the gorge, three yellow pinpricks crawled along the salt-flats road. Even through the blowing dust, Mateo recognized the blunt, top-heavy silhouettes: volumetric industrial mixer trucks, their auxiliary diesel drums turning with agonizing slowness. Behind them came two twin-cab gun-trucks from AndesGuard Tactical, their halogen roof bars cutting long, pale scars across the dry lakebed.

They were heading straight for the mine’s primary ventilation tower.

"They're moving faster than the switchback limit," Tadeo muttered. The boy was fifteen, his cheeks scored into purple leather by the Altiplano sun, his dark poncho pinned at the shoulder with an iron safety pin. He held the braided rawhide lead of the lead mule, a sway-backed roan mare named *La Morocha*. "The drivers know the road is empty."

"They know Carlos won't drop the gravel sluices until they touch the outer apron," Mateo said. His voice was a flat rasp, scraped raw by the dry wind. "Keep moving, Tadeo. If the frost gets into the moss pack, the whole run is dead."

Behind *La Morocha* came four more pack mules, their wooden cross-trees wrapped in greasy burlap to mute the chime of iron buckles. Across their flanks hung long, cylindrical bundles—flexible monocrystalline solar sheets wrapped around split bamboo canes, scavenged from an abandoned agro-research cooperative outside Oruro. But the real weight swung low in the center panniers: two double-walled aluminum milk cans packed with dampened sphagnum moss, sheep's wool, and four hundred glass vials of neutral zinc insulin.

Lucía Mamani had seventy children and twelve silicotic elders huddled in the 400-level drift. Without the cold-chain, the vials would spoil into worthless cloudy brine in twenty hours. Without the panels, the aeroponic pumps would starve the moment AndesGuard cut the local feeder lines.

"Hold the flank," Mateo commanded, motioning to Yaku, the smaller boy bringing up the rear with the broom of desert scrub tied to his stirrup to brush out the hoof-prints. "If a stone rolls into the dry wash, the sound carries down the chimney shafts like an artillery shell."

They hugged the rock face, where the ancient Inca sheep-paths notched into the crumbly rhyolite. To their right, the mountain dropped eight hundred vertical meters into the shadow of the Quebrada del Toro. The mules stepped with uncanny, delicate caution, their iron shoes clicking against volcanic glass.

Mateo pulled the frayed wool cuff back from his left wrist. Strapped beneath his watch was an old silver medallion of Saint Christopher—not kept for prayer, but because his elder brother had traded an entire week’s sugar ration for it the winter before the underground gallery collapsed at Chuquicamata. Mateo’s father had been a harness-maker before the lithium consortium bought the surface rights to the waterholes, and Mateo still caught himself running his thumb over the rawhide splices of the pack saddles, checking for rot by muscle memory alone. The hemp rope was stiff with hoarfrost, brittle as dried bone.

High above the headframe tower across the valley, a needle-thin flicker of crimson light blinked three times against the cliff face, then vanished.

The Free Space Optics laser transceiver. Sofia was trying to hold the carrier beam across the ridge to the Bolivian relay, but the wind was churning up a wall of airborne borax. The red pulse stuttered, broke, and dissolved into the grey gloom—forty-eight percent packet drop. The air-gap was shutting down. The subterranean refuge was blind.

"Mateo," Tadeo whispered, his hand going rigid on *La Morocha’s* halter.

Up ahead, where the narrow shelf rounded a sheer tooth of red sandstone, the trail had vanished.

A fresh dry-wash slide, likely triggered by the afternoon freeze-thaw cycle, had sheared sixty feet of the path into the abyss. What remained was a forty-degree scree slope of loose slate chips, hanging precariously over the drop.

*La Morocha* snorted, her nostrils flaring pink and wet, her ears pinning flat against her skull. She backed two paces, her iron hind shoe skittering on the edge of the shelf. A fist-sized chunk of shale dropped away. Mateo counted four agonizing seconds before the distant, hollow *clack* floated up from the dry canyon floor.

"Easy, girl," Tadeo choked out, his voice cracking with the thinness of the air. He tried to haul on the bit, but his boots were slipping on the frost-glazed rock. "She won't take it. She smells the drop."

"Don't pull her head," Mateo snapped, lunging forward. His right hand caught the cheek-strap while his left—three fingers clamping with desperate, scarred force—seized the leather cinch beneath her belly. "If she turns her neck, she rolls the pack. Yaku! Grab the tail-brace! Now!"

The younger boy dove across the shale, digging his knees into the frozen dirt, wrapping both arms around the pack frame to balance the center of gravity.

The mule shuddered, her flanks heaving like bellows. The smell of hot, frightened horse-sweat and vinegar-cured leather filled Mateo's nostrils, sharp against the metallic cold of the high ridge. Below the belly straps, the aluminum pannier clinked. The dry moss inside shifted.

Mateo leaned his entire weight against the mare’s shoulder, pressing her ribs into the solid basalt wall of the cliff. "Look at me, old girl. Look at the wall, not the sky."

He reached down with his boot, kicking savagely into the loose slate, cutting a shelf three inches wide. His toe struck hard rock beneath the drift. He kicked again, his ankle rolling inside the stiff cowhide boot, pain shooting up his shin. He cleared a second step, then a third, carving a raw trench across the face of the slide.

"Lead her across the cut," Mateo whispered to Tadeo. "One hoof at a time. If the slate moves, don't look down. Keep her nose pressed into the stone."

Tadeo swallowed, his throat making a dry clicking sound. He stepped onto the trench. The slate groaned beneath his worn soles, a soft, powdery whisper that threatened to let go all at once. He took the halter short, leaning his forehead against the mule’s jaw.

"Come, *Morocha*," the boy breathed in Quechua, words ancient and rhythmic, used for centuries to coax heavy loads over high Andean passes. "*Pisi pisimanta.* Little by little."

The mare set her front left shoe into Mateo’s cut. The rock held. She placed the right. A shower of grey dust drifted off the rim into the dark.

Mateo held his breath, his three fingers hooked into the girth strap, his shoulder taking the scrape of the pack pannier. As the central weight of the aluminum containers crossed the break, the ground beneath his own boots gave way.

His left leg plunged through the shale to the mid-thigh.

The sudden wrench tore his grip from the leather. Mateo fell backward, his elbows slamming into the frozen scree, his boots dangling over the sheer drop. He clawed at the slate with his right hand, his blunt fingernails tearing against the stone, gouging through frost until they found a root of scrub-thorn.

The bush gave a sharp, fibrous *snap*, holding by a thread.

"Mateo!" Yaku cried from the solid ledge behind him.

"Stay back!" Mateo barked between clenched teeth. The blood roared in his ears, thick and hot, starved of oxygen. His chest felt as though an iron hoop had been drawn tight around his ribs. Below him, the Quebrada del Toro opened like an empty mouth.

Through the tears of cold in his eyes, he looked past his boots to the valley floor.

The three cement mixers had reached the lower apron of the mine headframe. Their high-beam work lights flooded the rust-streaked corrugated iron of the processing plant. A yellow boom was already unfolding from the chassis of the lead truck, swinging out over the perimeter fence like the neck of a predatory bird.

They weren't waiting for daylight. They were positioning the delivery chutes directly over the primary concrete collar of the ventilation intake.

If that hydraulic slurry hit the shaft, twenty thousand tons of quick-setting aggregate would drop straight into the central airway, plugging the thermal exhaust. The subterranean aeroponics, the condensed water lines, the sixty infants in the shelter—the entire closed-loop lung of the mountain would be choked shut in forty minutes.

"Mateo! Take my stirrup!"

Tadeo had turned on the far side of the slide. The boy was leaning out over the gap, holding out the thick, grease-darkened stirrup leather of the lead saddle.

Mateo didn't look at the drop. He lunged upward with his good right arm, catching the iron ring.

Tadeo threw his weight backward against *La Morocha's* flank. The mule braced her iron-shod hocks into the rock and pulled.

With a sickening scrape of canvas and denim, Mateo was dragged up over the lip of the shelf, rolling onto the hard-packed trail on the other side of the slide. He lay on his back for three gasping breaths, staring up at the indifferent, ice-white stars wheeling over the jagged spine of the Altiplano. His ripped fingers left red smears on the grey slate.

"The mules are across," Tadeo whispered, his chest heaving, tears of pure terror cutting clean tracks through the salt dust on his cheeks. "The vials didn't break. I checked the foam."

Mateo rolled over, pushing himself up onto his knees. Every muscle in his back screamed with exhaustion, but the phantom knuckle on his left hand was cold now. Dead cold.

Down in the gorge, the low, mechanical grinding of three industrial diesel drums started up, a heavy vibration that traveled through the bedrock itself, shuddering through the soles of their boots.

The chutes were swinging into place.

Mateo pulled his sheepskin collar tight, grabbed the roan mare's bit, and turned his face toward the steep descent of the rear goat trails.

"No more walking," Mateo said, his voice flat, steady, and stripped of fear. "We run the mules."
