The pneumatic hiss cut through the gale like a striking serpent.

"Down!" Jin roared.

He hooked his forearm beneath Aisyah’s collar and hauled her inward with a savage jerk of his bad shoulder. The joint popped, a spike of hot silver radiating down into his fingers, but the momentum carried her sprawling over the crane’s rusted safety lip.

A steel barb the size of a dockyard bollard slammed into the gantry’s diagonal cross-brace. The impact rang like a fractured cathedral bell. High-tensile braided steel cable hummed tight, vibrating violently as the patrol craft reversed its twin stern thrusters, attempting to winch the leg off its concrete footing. The crane groaned, sixty-five meters of corroded steel shuddering five degrees out of true.

"Lin!" Jin barked into his collar, dragging Aisyah toward the service cage. "Seawater monitor three! Flood the breakwater!"

"Manual bypass engaged," Lin’s voice crackled, breathless and dry despite the storm. "Diverting tidal surge!"

A hundred feet below, the harbor deck thudded. The decommissioned ballast pump roared to life with an iron shriek. A twin-nozzle monitor on Berth 10 unlocked its pneumatic pintle, swinging out over the swell. A solid wall of brine, two thousand gallons every minute under brutal hydraulic head, erupted into the dark.

The high-pressure torrent hammered the matte-black interceptor square across its wheelhouse. Polycarbonate windscreen panels crazed instantly into white frost. The vessel tilted violently to port, shipping gray water across its low-slung aft deck. The optical prism on its mast shattered under the hydrodynamic punch, and the winch severed its own tow-cable against the gunwale sheer-lip with a loud *thwack* that whiplashed into the surf.

The craft’s thrusters screamed, churning white foam as it spun off its axis, retreating into the monsoon chop toward the deep channel.

Aisyah lay on the wet grating, her chest heaving, the skin of her fingers pale and puckered from seawater and solvent. She tasted iron on her split lower lip. When she rolled onto her knees, she wasn't looking at the fleeing boat. She was staring at what the winch cable had ripped from the conveyor stanchion as it sheared.

A heavy, polymer-coated pelican dispatch case was wedged in the twisted ladder rungs of the lower footing, dangling three feet above the churning tide.

"Don't," Jin muttered, holding his trembling elbow against his ribs, his breath catching in his throat. His knees clicked, calcified grit grinding behind the patellas.

"It’s not theirs," Aisyah said, already swinging her boots onto the ladder rungs. "Look at the lanyard. That’s commercial registry canvas. From the *Pulau Mas*."

Jin followed her down the dripping vertical cage. Every rung vibrated with the deep, rhythmic thrum of the tidal turbines churning eight fathoms under the shipyard slipway. The salt spray stung the chemical burn along his collarbone, an old, persistent itch that flared whenever the barometric pressure plunged. He reached the lower catwalk just as Aisyah hooked the dispatch satchel with the heel of her boot and hauled it onto the iron plating.

The casing was cracked, sea-foam weeping from its gaskets. Inside lay no transmitters or surveillance arrays. 

Only paper. Thick, synthetic waterproof leaves, bound in blue oilcloth and warped by bilge brine: the official master’s log of the container feeder *Pulau Mas*, registered out of Tanjung Perak.

Uncle Tan was waiting at the foot of the gantry stairs, his yellow slicker shining under the work-lights, his swollen, arthritic hands clamped firmly around a battered aluminum thermos. Behind him, huddled beneath the overhang of the machine shop, thirty stranded deckhands watched in dead silence.

"The patrol craft turned back toward the eastern anchorage," Tan said, his low voice gravel-soft, offering the thermos cap full of scalding ginger broth to Aisyah without looking at the satchel. For twenty years running tug crews through the Sunda Strait, Tan had kept a habit of reading men's posture before their faces; now, his rheumy eyes stayed fixed on the tremor in Jin’s forearm. "They won't bring the heavy cutters inside the reef until daybreak. Too shallow for their hydrofoils."

"Inside," Jin said through his teeth. "Before the rain ruins the ink."

They crowded into the drydock’s auxiliary toolroom, where Chen Rui had set up an emergency draft bench. The air smelled of damp cardboard, stale tobacco, and the warm copper tang of the battery banks stacked against the masonry wall. Rui didn't look up from his stress charts; his forearms were taped tight with elastic bandages to suppress the ache in his flexor tendons, his fingers rhythmically rolling a brass micrometer to calm an involuntary twitch.

Lin Zhang stood by the lone incandescent bulb, shaking water from her hair. Her spectacles were fogged, her face hollowed by forty straight hours without sleep. A patch of eczema on her temple, inflamed by salt and screen glare, showed angry and red against her pale skin.

"Let me see it," Lin whispered, pulling off her sodden neoprene gloves.

Jin laid the blue oilcloth binder on the zinc-topped table. With the tip of a pocket knife, he slit the swollen binding thread.

The leaves separated with a wet, sucking tear.

The handwriting was erratic, penned in purple indelible pencil by a captain whose vessel had clearly lost auxiliary power long before it grounded. But beneath the handwritten navigational coordinates were pasted strips of thermal paper—automated admiralty notices spat out by satellite receivers before the antennas had been silenced.

"Date: fourteen days ago," Lin read aloud, her finger tracing the blurred blue margins. "*Pulau Mas*. Three thousand TEU. Ballast voyage from Surabaya to Jurong."

"She was empty," Jin said. "Scrap-iron charter."

"Look at the ledger sequence," Rui muttered, his micrometer stopping mid-spin. He leaned over Lin’s shoulder, his glasses reflecting the single bulb. "Here. Margin notice from Euro-Asian Maritime Finance. An automated acceleration clause triggered at 03:00 UTC."

Lin touched the paper, her voice tightening. "Default declared under Section 14-B. The ship wasn't behind on mortgage payments. Look at the ledger printout. Every remittance was clearing through the Singapore escrow desk."

"Then why the writ?" Aisyah asked, wringing seawater from her dark hair into an empty oil drum.

"It wasn't a human writ," Lin said. She turned the leaf carefully. The paper beneath was stamped with the hexagon seal of Aegis-Omni Predictive Intelligence. "It’s a risk-spread algorithm. The system calculated a 4.2 percent probability that bunker fuel prices would exceed charter yields over the next three quarters. That single variance triggered a preemptive maritime lien."

Jin stared at the purple pencil marks. The captain’s handwriting grew progressively jagged across the page:

*04:12 — Remote lockout transmitted via channel 16 data burst. Main injection rail throttled by satellite relay. Engine room blacked out from shore.*

*05:30 — Rudder locked five degrees to starboard. Crew unable to override hydraulic helm. Vessel drifting into the outbound shipping lane.*

*07:15 — Boarding party from Apex Marine alongside. They carried no court summons. Only a printed QR clearance certificate.*

"They didn't repossess her because she failed," Jin breathed, his voice dropping into the cold register he used when an engine room took on water. "They crippled her because the algorithm decided she was worth more broken for parts."

"Not just her," Lin whispered. She flipped three pages forward, where the captain had taped a list copied from the VHF distress log during his final hours of drift. 

Nine other vessels. The *Bintang Timur*, the *Anping 7*, the *Riau Star*. All private owner-operator coasters. All seized in the fairway by automated command over the last sixteen days, their steering systems frozen remotely by the financial consortium’s insurance network, then pushed onto the shallows to justify private tactical salvage.

"It’s an automated harvest," Rui said, his knuckles white against the edge of the zinc table. "The sovereign traffic corridors are being cleared out by code to force all feeder traffic into the corporate container hubs. That’s why they wanted our laser link dark. If the independent ships synchronize their own manifests through our mesh, the predictive model loses jurisdictional control."

Outside, the rain slackened to an oily drizzle, but the silence that followed was heavier than the squall.

Uncle Tan walked to the narrow window overlooking the slipway. In the yard below, dozens of mariners were huddled around iron braziers burning driftwood and discarded dunnage, their breath rising in gray plumes against the damp night.

"They think the law is still a courtroom in London or Singapore," Tan said quietly, without turning around. "They think an admiralty judge will sign an order to give their decks back."

"There are no judges out there, Tan," Jin said, pressing his thumb hard against his collarbone scar. "Only clearinghouse software running on thirty-second margins."

Lin pulled a magnifying glass from her breast pocket, inspecting the bottom margin of the final thermal slip. Her hand suddenly froze.

"Jin," she said.

The tone of her voice made Chen Rui drop his micrometer. It rattled loudly on the zinc.

"What is it?" Jin demanded.

Lin didn't look up. Her finger was pinned to a tiny, eight-digit alphanumeric hash stamped at the foot of the *Pulau Mas* repossession order.

"The signature key authorizing the algorithmic seizure of the fleet," Lin said, each syllable dropping like cold lead into the room. "It wasn't issued by Euro-Asian Maritime Finance."

Jin stepped forward, the floorboards groaning under his weight. "Whose key is it?"

Lin looked up, her face paper-white in the yellow glare of the bulb.

"It’s registered to the Port Authority Receiver’s office in Batam," she whispered. "And the digital certificate was signed using the private cryptographic token of Captain Jin Wei."
