The sea-glider sheared through the dark swell of the Sunda Strait, her hydrofoils singing a low, dry baritone that vibrated straight through the soles of Captain Jin Wei’s boots. His lower vertebrae, worn down by forty years of standing watch above shaking diesel blocks, absorbed every micro-fracture of the chop. Outside the curved acrylic viewport, the black cone of Anak Krakatau rose against the horizon like an unlit furnace, smelling faintly of sulfur and wet pumice.

"Three degrees starboard, Aisyah," Jin murmured, his throat raw from the lingering sting of bunker gas he’d swallowed two days ago in the Batam bilge. "The shelf comes up fast past the light-buoy. If we draw more than two fathoms on the foil, the reef will take our rudder off."

Aisyah binti Rahman didn’t look up from the steering yoke. Her knuckles, cross-hatched with faint white scars where high-voltage arcing had cooked the skin down to the dermis three seasons back, adjusted the hydraulic trim by touch alone. Her father had spent forty years tuning marine gearboxes by resting a bone-handled screwdriver against his front teeth to feel the tooth-mesh; she had inherited the same somatic skepticism of dials. "I feel the drag," she said quietly. "The port accumulator is pulling warm. The salt crust on the cooling intake is starving the heat exchanger."

"How long until the cells overheat?" Lin Zhang asked. She was wedged between the navigation bench and the bulkhead, her knees drawn up to support a grease-pencil slate. Her temples pounded with the dull, rhythmic ache of four nights without uninterrupted sleep. A cold sweat soaked the collar of her canvas smock.

"Forty minutes at glide speed," Aisyah replied. "Twenty if we run silent beneath the acoustic picket line."

Lin rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, leaving a smudge of graphite across her brow. On the slate lay three hand-written clauses—the maritime codicil of the Asia-Pacific commons, hammered out over thirty-six hours of radio silence between the floating machine shops of Batam and the barge assemblies of the Java Sea.

"Read it again," Jin said, not turning his head from the pitch-black water ahead. "Without the legal jargon. If an engine-room greaser from Samarinda can’t understand what he’s putting his thumb to, it doesn’t go on the wire."

Lin looked down at the slate. "Article Seven: The Open Waterways. No consortium, sovereign admiralty, or corporate registry holds title to the straits, trenches, or migratory shelves of the archipelagic seas. The right of non-commercial transit belongs to any craft whose draft does not poison the bed and whose manifest carries no instruments of sovereign execution."

"Keep going," Jin coughed, catching the phlegm in a gray cotton handkerchief.

"Article Eight: The Restorative Anchorage. Any vessel under stress of weather, machinery failure, or financial arrest by offshore creditor courts may claim sanctuary in any port governed by sortition. Seizure of cargo carrying grain, fresh water, or pharmaceutical base stocks is declared a structural breach of human trust. The local dock assembly—fifteen mariners chosen by odd lot—shall hold usufruct over the hull until repairs are sound and the crew is fed."

"And the threshold?" Aisyah asked, her voice tight as she felt the glider lurch slightly over an upwelling current.

"Three-quarters," Lin said. "Seventy-six out of the one hundred and one regional ships' delegates must ratify before the optical transmitter clears the horizon. If we fail the supermajority, the document remains a petition. It carries no weight as regional law when the Swiss assembly convenes."

"They'll sign," Jin said.

"You don't know that," Lin countered, her voice fraying at the edges. She had spent her early twenties in the windowless sub-basements of the transport ministry in Beijing, cataloging regulatory drafts that senior bureaucrats tossed into the shredder before noon. The sheer stubborn inertia of entrenched power was something she felt like a physical weight pressing on her sternum. "The barge captains in Surabaya are terrified of the credit freezes. The moment their transponders go dark on the Singapore exchange, their families' rice rations in port are locked out of the automated depots."

"They are already locked out," Jin said, his voice dropping into that flat, gravelly register that came from surviving three separate company bankruptcies at sea. "The private syndicates stopped honoring the sovereign grain vouchers yesterday at noon. If the men on the water don't hold the locks themselves, there won't be rice anyway. They know what the paper is worth. Read the last clause."

Lin swallowed, tasting the bitter dregs of chicory tea. "Article Nine: Non-Enclosure of Ocean Energy. The thermal gradients of the deep trenches and the kinetic pulse of the tidal channels belong to no state, concession, or private utility. Energy drawn from the water must be shared first with the littoral communities whose shores bear the wash, second with the working fleet, and never traded on forward derivative exchanges."

"Simple," Jin grunted. "Even an admiralty lawyer will choke on that."

"Aisyah," Lin said, leaning forward. "Can the laser head on the mast reach the repeater on Mount Rajabasa?"

Aisyah glanced at the gimbal display above the windshield. The amber crosshairs were shuddering violently. "The monsoon squall over the strait is shearing the beam. We're losing eighty percent of the light to rain scatter. And something else." She reached out and touched the passive acoustic monitor, a phosphor screen that registered underwater pressure waves. A rhythmic, metallic *ping-thrum... ping-thrum* was pulsing through the deep trench to the west.

"Apex patrol skiff?" Jin asked, his shoulders stiffening against the bulkhead.

"Worse," Aisyah said. "A hydrographic surveyor. They aren't shooting. They're mapping the channel floor with low-frequency chirps to locate our acoustic signature. If they bracket our cavitation, they'll cue the maritime police cutter out of Merak to lay a containment boom across the strait mouth."

"We can't dive the foils," Jin said. "The shelf is too shallow."

"Then we cut the drive," Aisyah said.

"If we cut the drive," Lin whispered, "we lose the stabilized platform for the optical transceiver. The beam will drift off the mountain tower, and the sortition session closes in seven minutes."

Jin looked at Lin, then at the small brass sortition plate lying next to her slate—the blank token with three hand-filed notches. He thought of his first command thirty years ago, an unflagged timber barge where he had stood at the wheel with salt sores festering on his wrists, entirely dependent on whether an unseen broker in Hong Kong decided his cargo was worth unloading or abandoning to rot at anchor. The world had not changed because people asked it to; it changed because the people who shoveled the coal or tended the batteries simply laid their tools down together.

"Take the helm, Lin," Jin said.

"What? I don't know the hydrodynamic trim—"

"Hold the wheel dead center," he commanded, already unlatching the heavy water-tight hatch that led to the forward deck well. "Aisyah, keep the battery output pegged at minimum steerage. I’m going onto the bow to clear the gimbal lock by hand."

"The wash will take you over the rail," Aisyah warned, her eyes darting between the sonar sweep and the temperature gauge on the port battery. "The sea is running three meters off the volcanic reef."

"Not if you hold her level," Jin said. He didn't wait for an answer. He pulled the rubber storm hood over his ears, shoved the dog-latches home, and forced the iron hatch outward into the roaring dark.

The cabin filled instantly with the deafening scream of the Sunda swell and the smell of sulfur-heavy spray. The sea-glider pitched sickeningly to port as a wave broke over the forward coaming.

Through the salt-streaked glass, Lin saw Jin's silhouette crawl onto the narrow forward deck, his yellow foul-weather jacket gleaming in the wash of spray. His bad hip hitched with every movement, dragging his left leg behind him across the non-skid plating. He reached the base of the short carbon-fiber mast, hooked his arm through the safety rung, and brought a heavy bronze spanner down against the salt-encrusted gimbal ring.

Once. Twice.

A shower of white salt flakes tore away in the wind. The small cyan emitter at the top of the mast jerked, hummed, and locked onto the high peak of Rajabasa, invisible behind the rain clouds.

Inside the cabin, the terminal chimed—a dry, mechanical tick.

*OPTICAL CARRIER SYNCHRONIZED.*

"Lin! The packet!" Aisyah shouted over the roar of the water rushing past the hull. "Send it now! The surveyor is turning toward our wake!"

Lin's fingers shook on the keypads. She didn't look at the tactical display. She didn't look at the acoustic sweep closing in from the west. She verified the hash of the three articles, opened the sortition tally buffer, and released the query into the cyan beam.

For forty-five seconds, there was nothing but the heave of the hull, the shudder of the port hydrofoil clawing through the shallow chop, and the dark shape of Captain Jin clinging to the mast against the green water foaming over his boots.

Then, the return carrier pulsed down from the mountain.

One by one, the cryptographic marks of the Asia-Pacific maritime sortition appeared on the liquid-crystal display. They were not corporate endorsements or ministerial stamps; they were the verified signatures of one hundred and one working vessels: the crew assembly of the grain carrier *Bintang Timur*; the engine collective of the ferry *Nusantara IV*; the thirty dockworkers of the Teluk Betung salt co-op; the seventy-four mariners of the Samarinda timber fleet.

*VOTES RECORDED: 101.*
*AFFIRMATIVE: 84.*
*NEGATIVE: 17.*
*SUPERMAJORITY ATTAINED (83.1%).*
*PACIFIC MARITIME COVENANT RATIFIED TO CLASS 0 REPOSITORY.*

The hatch slammed open, and Jin fell backward into the cabin, soaked to the skin, coughing violently as brine drained from his collar. Aisyah reached behind her with one hand, hauling him upright by his harness, while her other hand slammed the trim lever forward, dumping the hydrofoils flat to skim silently over the sandbar.

The hydrographic surveyor's acoustic chirp faded behind them, searching empty deep water where they were no longer floating.

Lin stared at the terminal. "It’s locked," she breathed, her voice cracking. "The maritime articles are part of the global draft. Even if they take Batam tomorrow, the ships are bound by their own covenant."

Jin sat on the wet deck plates, his chest heaving, his scarred hands trembling as he wrung seawater from his sleeves. He looked up at the tiny screen.

"That's the sea," he said hoarsely. "Now tell me what came from the land."

Aisyah reached past him to clear the optical buffer. A second transmission, routed through the Batam high tower before the squall closed in, was unspooling in terse, broken lines of text. It had originated six thousand miles away, bounced through three air-gapped alpine relays before reaching the coast.

Lin read the words aloud, her voice sinking into the hum of the cooling pumps.

*PALAIS DES NATIONS UNDER ADMINISTRATIVE SEAL.*
*CANTONAL GENDARMERIE HAS CUT WATER AND DISTRICT HEATING TO SORTITION WING.*
*LAKE GENEVA BOOMED AT OUCHY AND CHILLON.*
*EURO-ASIAN CLEARINGHOUSE HAS FILED CONTEMPT LIENS AGAINST ALL 1,001 DELEGATES.*
*THEY ARE FREEZING THEM OUT BEFORE THE PLENARY CAN OPEN.*

Aisyah pulled the throttle back to silent cruise, her face setting into cold, rigid stone. "Can the glider clear the Cape of Good Hope before the ice on the lake hardens?"

"No," Jin said, struggling to his feet and planting his boots wide on the deck. "We don't go around the Cape. We fly the electric transport from Colombo straight into the Mediterranean headwinds. If they think they can freeze a thousand people out of their own history with a municipal valve, they've never tried to keep a cold ship running in winter."

He wiped the salt from his eyes and looked through the glass toward the open ocean, where the storm was waiting.
