The vertical climb through sixty meters of rusted gantry lattice had drained the last reserve of heat from Jin’s thighs. Every lumbar vertebrae ground together like ungreased iron as he hauled himself through the cab’s bottom hatch, his shoulder catching the coaming with a dull thud. Rain caught them the instant Lin cleared the sill behind him, slamming the heavy iron hatch downward and dogging the dog-clamps until the rubber gasket hissed against the gale.

Outside, the monsoon struck the glass like a shovelful of wet gravel. 

The cab hung suspended between the drowned concrete of Drydock 4 and the black churn of the Malacca Strait. Below them, Keppel-Batam was a sprawling labyrinth of silhouettes. The high-voltage yard floodlights remained dead, killed by the sudden grid drop. Across the dark floor of the plate shop, two amber hand-lanterns bobbed toward the battery bunkers where Rui and Aisyah were fighting to bring the salvaged hybrid tugboat cells online. 

Out past the breakwater, the red running lights of the salvage flotilla were already cutting arcs through the whitecaps. The lead ocean tug had dropped its trailing gear; even through the howl of the squall, the scream of its heavy hydraulic drum reached the crane cab as a dull, bone-rattling vibration through the pylon legs. They were dragging steel riser chains across the channel mouth, a blunt mechanical snare to choke the yard’s draft before the morning tide.

Lin leaned her forehead against the damp glass of the cab’s southern pane. Her breath blossomed against the salt-crusted surface, instantly vanishing in the cabin’s draft. She pulled a dented stainless thermos from the inner sling of her oilskin coat, her fingers trembling with the rhythmic twitch of severe nerve fatigue. 

"Oolong," she said, her voice dry, stripped of its usual crisp authority. "Cold. Brewed before the dawn watch."

Jin took the cup she poured into the screw-cap lid. The tea was stone-cold, astringent and bitter on the back of his tongue, tasting of copper and old tin. It did nothing for the hollow ache in his ribs, but the ritual of holding something solid kept his thumbs from locking. 

"They won't ram the caisson," Jin said, his voice a gravel rasp against the roar of the wind. He pointed a scarred index finger through the wiper arc. "Not in a eight-knot cross-swell. The salvage master wants the tidal turbine mounts. If they foul the subsea cables with those drag chains, the rotor shafts will twist on their bedplates. Once the copper windings sheer, the whole basin loses base-load power for good."

Lin didn't answer immediately. She pressed the heels of her palms into her eye sockets, trying to crush the visual migraine flickering across her optic nerve—a jagged crown of blinding silver that had haunted her for forty-eight hours. "They aren't just salvors, Jin. Euro-Asian Maritime Finance bought the court-appointed receiver's debt note three hours ago in London. Aegis-Omni holds the secondary lien. This isn't an eviction. It's a calculated foreclosure on our legal refuge window."

Jin let out a short, wet cough, pulling an oil-stained rag from his collar to catch the phlegm. "A lien doesn't pull a four-inch link chain across a channel in a squall, Doctor. Men do. Men getting paid six hundred Singapore dollars a day by an agency in Tanjong Pagar, praying their own company housing doesn't get seized while they're at sea."

He turned away from the glass, sinking back onto the operator’s bench. The worn vinyl padding had split decades ago, spitting yellowed foam through duct tape seams. From his chest pocket, he pulled a small, smooth splinter of polished horn—a net-mending shuttle, barely the length of his thumb, notched at both ends with microscopic grooves worn smooth by nylon twine.

Lin lowered her hands, watching the tiny relic in his scarred grip. "What is that?"

"My mother carved it from a water buffalo rib," Jin murmured, his eyes distant, fixed on the horn rather than the storm. "Hainan, before the land-reclamation concrete covered the mangrove flats. She used to mend drift-nets by the kerosene burner while the tides turned. When the state syndicate consolidated the artisanal fleet into forty-meter trawlers, she gave it to me. Said if a man can weave a knot that holds against a running tide, he never has to beg a harbor master for work." 

A harsh, self-deprecating smile touched the corner of his mouth. "Three weeks later, I signed my first contract as third wiper on a chemical tanker. Spent thirty years burning heavy bunker oil, poisoning the very shelf she fished. When the company went into restructuring off Colombo and dumped the crew without pay, I sold my sea chest, my watch, my certifications. Kept only this. A bone stick for catching fish that don't exist anymore."

He set the shuttle on the rusted steel console between them, beside the silent terminal of the cyan laser rig. 

Lin looked at the piece of bone, then down at her own pale hands, the skin scrubbed raw around the cuticles where she had picked at them until they bled. A shudder passed through her shoulders—not from the draft leaking through the window frames, but from an older, colder draft.

"I built the ledger engine that flagged your tanker," she said softly.

Jin did not move. He kept his gaze locked on the red running lights moving across the outer basin.

"Five years ago," Lin continued, the words coming out flat, measured, stripped of defense. "At the logistics institute in Tianjin. We were designing the dynamic liquidity scoring for corporate receivers. We called it 'operational pruning.' If a vessel’s voyage margin fell below seven percent against the fuel index, the clearinghouse script automatically canceled the port bunkering credit and froze the crew escrow accounts. It took twelve milliseconds. No human eyes ever looked at the crew manifest. No one asked if the third wiper had enough fresh water to make harbor."

She leaned her back against the cab door, her knees pulling up toward her chest. "Two years later, my sister was running an independent pediatric clinic in the western suburbs. The municipal utility moved to an automated bond auction. Her clinic missed two algorithmic balancing payments during a regional drought. The water main was shut off by a remote telemetry valve on a Tuesday morning while three infants were in incubators. I sat at my terminal forty miles away, watching the load-shedding log tick green. I knew the code. I helped optimize the queue."

She swallowed, the sound loud against the roar of the rain on the cab roof. "I didn't quit out of noble principle, Jin. I quit because I realized I had spent my entire adult life forging the shackles, thinking if I made them precise enough, they wouldn't pinch."

A gust of wind caught the gantry, groaning through the steel box girders. The cab swayed three inches to the leeward side, the cables singing a shrill, harmonic note of strained tension. 

On the console, the point-to-point cyan laser transceiver clicked violently. The small motorized gimbal hummed, fighting the violent sway of the tower to keep its optical lens aligned with the distant ridge across the strait in Batam. The green tracking LED sputtered, dropped to amber, then flashed an urgent, rhythmic crimson.

*Optical link degraded: 14% line-of-sight clarity.*

"Squall mist is scattering the beam," Lin said, instantly back on her feet, the somatic vulnerability vanishing beneath the sharp instincts of a technician under fire. She reached for the optical collimator dial, steadying her trembling wrist against the casing. "If we drop below ten percent, the cryptographic handshake with the offshore trust node will break. Geneva won't receive the ratified registry of the commons before dawn."

Jin stood beside her, his binoculars raised to the rain-lashed glass. The wiper arm thumped back and forth, clearing a half-moon of greasy clarity through the spray.

Below them, past the outer channel buoys, the lead salvage tug swung its stern toward the tidal turbine pens. But through the lenses, Jin saw something that made the muscles in his jaw turn to stone. 

The tug wasn't deploying a standard salvage drag. Suspended from its heavy stern A-frame was a black, cylindrical apparatus, thick as a fuel pipe, fitted with four counter-rotating cutting blades and an umbilical bundle thick as an anchor hawser. It was a subsea directional trenching cutter—an industrial tool used to sever and bury pipeline conduits, not salvage hulls.

"They aren't trying to foul the rotors," Jin whispered, his voice cold. "They're cutting the granite moorings. They mean to set the tidal turbines adrift into the shipping lane. If those four-hundred-ton units drift into the separation scheme, international maritime safety law gives the coast guard full jurisdiction to board and clear the entire yard as a navigational hazard."

Lin froze, her fingers on the collimator. "How long until they reach the cable trench?"

"Ten minutes. Once they drop that cutter into the silt, the drag will pull the cables taut."

Then, from the dark shadow of the yard floor directly below their tower, a single, sharp flash of blue-white light pulsed upward through the squall. 

It was not an arc from the battery banks. It was a handheld infrared signaling strobe, clicking in rapid, disciplined five-beat bursts from the open doorway of Berth 9—aimed directly at the bridge of the advancing ocean tug.

Someone inside the yard was guiding the cutter to the mooring coordinates.
