The descent out of the alpine cloud base felt less like flight and more like scraping the belly of an iron stove across gravel.

Freezing fog had crusted the leading edges of the sea-glider’s composite foils with a three-inch rind of milk-white rime. Every five seconds, the starboard electric prop gave a wet, shuddering hitch, shedding balance as its heating strips drained the last reserve cells.

Captain Jin Wei held the twin yokes with his elbows tucked tight against his ribs. The relentless high-frequency vibration of forty hours in the air had turned his lower lumbar into an agonizing pinch of ground glass, an old ballast-tank injury flaring red-hot in the sudden alpine frost. Through the scratched acrylic windscreen, Switzerland did not look like the postcard sanctuaries of his boyhood atlas. It looked slate-gray, dead, and razor-sharp.

"Altitude three hundred meters," Dr. Lin Zhang said. Her voice was flat, dried out by stale recirculated air, but her knuckles were white where she gripped the edge of the navigation console. She hadn't slept since the Malacca strait; her left eye was bloodshot, twitching to the beat of an untreated sinus migraine. "Lake surface ahead. But it isn't reflecting the shoreline beacons."

"Because it’s skimmed," Jin grunted, working the manual pitch lever. The mechanical cables fought him, stiff with thickening grease. "The cold snap out of the Jura. Look at the shallows along Versoix."

Beneath them, Lake Geneva did not roll in deep, sheltering swells. A week of the bitter north *Bise* had locked the Petit-Lac into a sheet of fractured, jagged shelf-ice. Gray plates of pack, crushed together by shore currents, gleamed like broken bottle glass under the heavy ceiling of snow clouds.

"Flaps to full depression," Jin ordered, teeth clutched tight against the shudder of the airframe. "If we catch a pontoon tip on an ice ridge, we flip her. We take the touchdown flat on the reinforced keel. Hold on to the bulkhead."

The glider crossed the shoreline trees at ninety knots.

There was no clean hydrodynamic skim. The keel met the crust of the lake with a sound like a galvanized sheet being torn in half. Violent, concussive deceleration threw Lin against her four-point harness, knocking the breath from her lungs in a harsh gasp. Slabs of slush and gray ice exploded outward, slamming against the fuselage windows in blinding cataracts of gray mud and frozen grit. The port outrigger skipped twice, screeching as carbon fiber abraded against the pack, before the sea-glider finally slewed forty degrees to port and settled into a frozen furrow, twenty yards short of the timber pilings of the Port des Mouettes.

Silence slammed down on the cockpit.

The twin props hissed as their residual heat melted the rim of ice clinging to the cowlings. Steam curled off the forward battery cowl, smelling faintly of scorched silicone and river silt.

Jin let out a long, ragged exhalation that plumed white inside the unheated cabin. He wiped a smear of cold condensation from his forehead with the back of a calloused thumb. His hands were shaking—not from fear, but from the sudden, jarring drop in ambient temperature. Tropical blood, thinned by thirty years of equatorial salt spray, felt thick as tar in the zero-degree draft creeping through the hatch seals.

"System lock," Lin whispered, her trembling fingers clearing the terminal buffers. "Batteries are at three percent. We don't have enough juice left to spin the bilge pumps."

"We're down," Jin said. "That’s all the juice had to do."

He reached overhead, popped the mechanical latch of the emergency escape hatch, and shoved. The composite door cracked open against a crust of frost, letting in an alpine draft that felt like a bucket of ice water hurled directly into their chests.

Standing on the fractured ice shelf fifteen paces from the sea-glider’s scarred nose were two figures wrapped in heavy, oilcloth-lined coats.

The taller of the two—his broad shoulders rounded slightly against the driving sleet, a wool watch cap pulled down over graying temples—stepped onto the pontoon with the careful, balanced tread of an old machinist who knew precisely how much weight a cracked timber could bear. Marcus Hayes held an unlit wooden match between his teeth, an old habit from his early shop-floor days when smoking was forbidden near the solvent tanks. Beside him, Giulia Bellini’s breath puffed in steady, rhythmic clouds. Her dark eyes were sharp behind frost-flecked spectacles, her woolen scarf tucked tightly into the collar of a salt-bleached Alpine rescue anorak. A noticeable stiffness in her left knee, a legacy of the bitter winter blockades along the northern rail spurs, made her lean slightly on an iron-tipped walking pole.

"You took your time crossing the Rhone valley, Captain," Marcus said. His voice was a low, gravelly rasp that carried effortlessly through the damp chill. He offered a massive, grease-calloused hand down into the hatch well.

Jin gripped it. The warmth of Marcus’s woolen glove struck him with the force of an electric shock. With a single grunt, Marcus levered the captain up onto the slippery composite deck, catching him by the elbow as Jin’s sea-legs momentarily buckled on the glaze of ice.

"The headwind over the Alps was forty knots," Jin rasped, his throat raw from the dry air. He reached back down into the opening to haul up Lin’s canvas kit bag before extending a hand to help her onto the wing. "And the Swiss radar towers were painting our tail all the way from Chambery."

Giulia caught Lin’s arm, steadying the younger woman as she stumbled on the frozen pontoon. For a fraction of a second, the two women simply looked at one another—the weary, red-eyed cryptographer from the tropical docks of Batam and the veteran legal coordinator who had spent five years running decentralized supply rings across the cantons. Giulia didn't offer a polite smile; she simply unclasped an insulated thermal flask from her belt and pressed it directly into Lin’s stiff fingers.

"Black tea, chicory, and sugar," Giulia said, her cadence crisp and measured. "Drink it before your lips split. The frost here bites bone before you feel the skin tear."

Lin unscrewed the cap with numb thumbs. The steam hit her face like an open kitchen door. "The delegate register," she said between hurried sips, the hot liquid burning her throat in a welcome, agonizing line. "Our link showed three hundred seats dropped while we were over the Mediterranean. What happened to the sortition count?"

Marcus took the wooden match from his mouth, turned it between his calloused fingers, and pointed toward the eastern ridge of the lake.

Half a mile off, through the gray curtain of sleet, the granite facade of the Palais des Nations sat dark against the forested hills of Pregny. No security searchlights swept the grounds, but a neat, unbroken line of blue utility sawhorses and cantonal police vans blocked every access gate along the Avenue de la Paix. Out on the open water beyond the pack ice, a solitary 120-foot cantonal hydrofoil patrol boat idled in the channel, its twin water jets churning a froth of gray slush, its radar mast rotating with mechanical indifference.

"The Federal Council invoked Article 102," Marcus said calmly. "They didn't deploy tear gas or water cannon. They simply classified the global sortition assembly as an unscheduled foreign convention posing a systemic public-health and civil-order liability. The cantonal building inspectors condemned the assembly hall at midnight. Blocked the heating mains, severed the municipal fiber link, and locked the turnstiles."

"On what authority?" Jin demand, his hand instinctively dropping to the empty belt where his deck-knife used to hang. "The sortition drum drew those names under the Hague usufruct covenant. One thousand and one seats, odd parity, verified across four continental meshes."

"On the authority of the municipal fire code," Giulia said, her voice dry as autumn birch bark. "The cantonal administration is rational, Captain. They don't fight revolutions with riot police if they can starve them out with insurance riders and frozen municipal permits. They know the global delegates are arriving in Geneva on expired transit visas. If they freeze the assembly venue for seventy-two hours, the delegates run out of legal lodging, the cantonal hotels are barred from extending credit, and the local courts will deport the entire lottery back to their ports of origin under standard vagrancy statutes."

"We don't have seventy-two hours," Lin said, pulling the canvas satchel tighter to her ribs. "The sovereign mortgage syndicates in Singapore just initiated admiralty foreclosure on thirty-two container hulls. If the Global Assembly doesn't ratify the usufruct sanctuary charters by tomorrow noon, the port receivers will sell those ships for scrap. Three thousand mariners will be turned onto the street without discharge papers."

"Then it's a good thing we never planned to meet in their palace," Marcus said.

He turned toward the shore. Tucked behind the municipal boat slips was a cluster of nineteenth-century timber boat-sheds—cavernous, moss-shingled structures where local fishermen had repaired lake trawlers and dried hemp nets since the days of the old confederacy. Smoke was rising from three tin chimney pipes jutting through the cedar shingles.

A low murmur of voices, thick with a dozen different accents and punctuated by the clinking of tin mess kits, drifted out through the timber seams.

"The Fishermen's Usufruct Union holds the water-rights lease on these sheds until 2048," Marcus said, stepping down from the pontoon onto the packed snow of the slipway. "Paid in advance under old cantonal law that the federal receivers can't touch without unanimous cantonal court consent. We brought three hundred and four delegates in through the freight rail yard at three this morning."

Jin paused on the ice, his gaze sweeping from the grand, darkened silhouette of the Palais up on the hill down to the low, smoking timber roof of the boat-shed.

"And the odd parity?" Jin asked.

Giulia adjusted her spectacles, her mouth curving into the faintest shadow of an iron smile. "Three hundred and five now that you two have stepped ashore. The assembly is quorate."

Before Jin could reply, a sudden, high-pitched air horn cut through the falling snow.

Out in the frozen channel, the cantonal hydrofoil patrol boat surged forward, its reinforced steel prow cracking through the surface shelf-ice with a sharp, rhythmic boom. A bright, halogen searchlight flicked on from the cutter’s bridge, its white beam slicing through the sleet to pin the four of them against the scarred hull of the sea-glider.

Over the patrol craft’s deck speaker, a voice echoed across the frozen water in measured, polite, and unyielding French:

*"Civil vessel on the Versoix ice shelf. You are moored within a municipal restricted shipping lane during cantonal curfew. Stand by your vessel. The maritime receiver is coming aboard."*
