The displacement wave did not look like water; it looked like a shelf of dark, torn slate sliding across the harbor.

When the scuttled bulk hull settled onto the seabed, four hundred feet of dead steel choked the dredged gut, and the incoming monsoon tide—already forced into a fury by sixty-knot gusts out of the South China Sea—had nowhere left to go. The channel narrowed to a jagged throat. The sea surged upward, piling upon its own back, and came roaring toward the entrance of Drydock 4.

Lin’s warning horn died mid-wail atop the crane tower as salt mist choked the air intake, leaving only the brute physical thunder of three hundred thousand tons of displaced seawater slamming against the outer breakwater. Spray leaped fifty feet into the squall, tasting of diesel slick and rotting estuary mud.

"Jin!" Aisyah screamed against the wind. Her soaked tunic was plastered to her ribcage, the fabric tearing at the shoulder where she had snagged it on a cable clamp ten minutes prior. "The outer sill! The miter gates can’t take ten meters of head! The pintle pins will shear right off the concrete!"

Jin was already moving, his boots skidding across the grease-filmed limestone apron. His lower spine felt as though a dull chisel had been hammered between his lumbar vertebrae, each heavy stride sending a hot, sickening throb into his hips. "The flat-deck!" he roared back, spitting brackish rain. "The scrap barge at Berth Nine! We put her across the throat!"

Behind them, Keller and the disarmed contractors stood waist-deep in the sudden wash creeping over the lower slipway. Vane, his tactical vest discarded in the mud, grabbed the arm of his shivering comrade and hauled him toward the dry mouth of the machine shop. For a second, Vane looked back at Jin—not with defiance, but with the hollow, animal terror of a man who realized that no contract covered drowning inside another man’s cage.

"Get your men inside the turbine house!" Jin bellowed at him without breaking stride. "Up to the crane tracks! Don't touch the floor switches!"

Aisyah was already ten paces ahead of him, her light frame cutting through the sheets of rain toward the rusted silhouette of the *Batam-9*. The barge was an eighty-meter slab of unpainted, pitted iron, moored with three frayed nylon hawsers to the outer bollards. It had spent five years hauling bauxite before the receivers abandoned it; it was empty, high in the water, riding the incoming chop like an enormous hollow tin can. If the surge struck its flank while it was buoyant, it would rip the bollards clean out of the quayside and act as a battering ram, smashing the miter gates inward and drowning the three refitting hulls resting on the drydock keel blocks below.

"We drop her!" Aisyah shouted, vaulting the gap between the concrete apron and the barge's wet gunwale. Her scarred fingers caught the cold rim of the coaming. She winced, the high-voltage flash burns across her palms tearing open against the raw rust, leaving thin streaks of crimson on the orange iron. "If we don't open the sea chests, the wave will pick her up and toss her into the basin!"

Jin landed on the deck behind her with a sickening jar to his knees. The deck was bucking, tossing side to side as the first low swell of the surge rolled into the slips. Out in the fairway, the sound was deepening—a low, bowel-shaking drone like a freight train running through wet gravel. The scuttled freighter had created a hydraulic ram; the tide was climbing the harbor walls inch by agonizing inch.

"The hydraulic actuators on the scuttle valves are dead," Jin said, dragging the back of his hand across his streaming face. The chemical burn at his collarbone burned like lye in the salt spray. "The lines were cut when they stripped the deck machinery in April. It’s all manual wheel-and-worm."

"Number three and number four compartments," Aisyah said, dropping to her hands and knees to yank up a heavy circular dog-hatch. The stench that boiled out of the dark void was suffocating—stagnant bilge water, decomposing seaweed, and the suffocating tang of trapped rust. "If we flood the stern tanks, her tail will ground against the limestone shelf right in front of the gate apron. She'll act as a breakwater. She'll eat the crest."

"And if she twists?" Jin asked, hauling a heavy iron spanner from the tool rack welded beside the coaming.

"Then she takes the gates with her," she said, her brown eyes meeting his through the pelting rain, wide and utterly cold with focus. "Down. Now."

They dropped through the hatch into the stifling dark.

Inside the double bottom, the heat was ferocious, clinging to their skin like wet wool. The monsoon raged outside, but down here the hull amplified every impact of the rising water against the exterior plates into a series of deafening, metallic clangs that vibrated through the marrow of their bones. The air was thin, tasting of rust dust and old oil.

Jin led the way along the narrow center walkway, his shoulder brushing against the web frames. Every rib of the ship was sweating. At the bulkhead separating tanks three and four, two cast-iron valve wheels, each two feet in diameter, sat locked to their vertical stems.

"Left is open!" Jin barked. He wedged the open jaw of the iron spanner into the spokes of the starboard valve, throwing his full body weight against it.

Nothing moved. The salt air of the strait had welded the bronze stem to the iron gland in a crust of green corrosion.

"Come on, you bastard," Jin hissed through his teeth. His degenerative back gave a vicious jolt, pain radiating down the back of his left thigh until his toes went numb in his boot. He threw himself against the lever again. His knuckles scraped the raw edge of the frame, tearing the skin, but he didn't feel the cut—only the terrible, rhythmic thumping of the outer swell hammering against the steel plates three inches from his skull.

Aisyah threw her shoulder against the spanner beside him. Her small boots skidded on the greasy floor plates. "On three!" she gasped, her voice raw. "One—two—three!"

With a sound like a pistol shot, the rust seal broke. The wheel jerked half an inch, shearing a crust of lime and salt.

Jin didn't wait for his breath. He grabbed the rim with both hands, working the wheel in frantic quarter-turns. Down below their feet, deep in the sea-chest chest manifold, the iron wedge lifted.

The sea did not enter quietly. It burst through the intake pipe with a concussive roar that shook the deck plates beneath their boots. A jet of foul, freezing seawater geysered up through a weeping gasket on the valve bonnet, drenching them both in gray foam. The barge shuddered, a heavy, dead sensation creeping into the motion of the deck as the first hundred tons of the strait rushed into the cavernous empty tanks.

"Port side!" Aisyah yelled, already scrambling over the center pipe to the opposite wheel.

She hauled on the wheel with both hands, but her burned skin betrayed her; she slipped, her knees slamming hard against the diamond-plate steel with a hollow thud that made her gasp in agony.

Jin caught her by the waistband of her trousers, hauling her upright with one arm while his other caught the spokes. His breath was coming in ragged, burning gasps, smelling of old tobacco and acid. "I’ve got the wheel! Clear the ladder! When this tank takes three feet of head, the suction through the air vents will pull the hatch shut!"

"I'm not leaving you down here to drown in the dark, Uncle!" she spat back, her face white with pain, her hands instantly locking onto the rim right beside his own, their torn fingers interlacing on the cold iron.

Together, they hauled. The second valve groaned, fighting them tooth and nail, threads screaming as sand and shell-grit ground through the brass sleeve. Inch by inch, the spindle rose.

The ocean struck the port hull with a blow that threw them both sideways against the bulkhead.

Above them, the sky seemed to tear open. The sound was not wind—it was the great wave cresting over the outer barrier, hitting the barge's high freeboard like an artillery shell. The entire seventy-meter hull rolled twelve degrees to starboard with a sickening, lazy groan. Outside, mooring lines were snapping with the crack of rifle fire.

"She's dragging!" Aisyah screamed, scrambling up the steel rungs toward the square patch of stormy gray daylight.

Jin hauled himself out behind her, his left leg dragging slightly, his boots sloshing with water that was already knee-deep on the lower walkway.

They tumbled out onto the open deck just as the main surge hit.

The wall of dark green water swept clean over the barge's low bow, ripping a metal storage locker off its welds and hurling it across the apron like a cardboard box. But the stern, bloated with four hundred tons of fast-flooding seawater, refused to lift. It dragged hard, its keel grinding into the submerged limestone shelf with a scream of tortured rock and tearing plate that could be felt through the soles of their boots.

The barge swung broadside across the mouth of the drydock, wedging its dead weight directly between the two stone piers just as the crest peaked.

A mountain of foam struck the barge's outer flank. The hull took the blow, groaning along every rivet, buckling inward six inches at the midship frame, but it held. The deadly kinetic hammer that would have shattered the drydock gates shattered against the barge's sacrificial flank instead, spilling into the basin not as a catastrophic ram, but as a harmless, boiling cascade of white water that drained toward the sump pumps.

Aisyah collapsed against the coaming, her chest heaving, rain plastering her hair across her eyes, a wild, breathless laugh tearing out of her throat. "She grounded! She took the hit!"

Jin did not answer. He was standing on the listing deck, his eyes fixed not on the water, but on the torn iron where the barge’s bow had crushed the wooden fender of the eastern pier.

Wedged deep in the splintered timber beneath the smashed concrete lip, revealed for the first time as the storm tide scoured away ten years of accumulated silt and black marine growth, lay a thick, steel-armored bundle—a primary umbilical conduit, stamped with the unmistakable yellow bands of a sovereign military communication cable, cut clean through with the jagged, unmistakable marks of a high-temperature underwater hydraulic shear.

And from the severed, oil-filled core of the cable, a steady, rhythmic pulse of tiny, brilliant green indicator lights was still winking through the murk.
