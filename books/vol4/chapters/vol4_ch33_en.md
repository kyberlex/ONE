The seabed shuddered before the sound arrived.

Through the salt-encrusted steel of the catwalk, Jin felt the vibration travel upward through the drydock pilings, a deep, grinding shudder that rattled the teeth in his jaw. Two miles out in the separation channel, the dark water churned into white foam as the port-side anchor of the *Caspian Venture* struck the submerged shelf. Five tons of forged iron dragged through silt and riprap, its flukes hunting for the thick armored umbilical that carried current from the tidal turbines into the yard.

Lin caught the rail, her knuckles white against the flaking primer. "They’re dragging blind," she breathed, her voice raspy from twenty straight hours over glowing monitors. "If those flukes hook the high-voltage jacket, the arc will boil twenty yards of seawater and blow the circuit banks across the whole bay."

"They aren't blind," Jin said.

He turned toward the open hatch of the gantry’s bridge deck. Decades of diesel vibration had left his lower back a tight knot of calcified bone, and every step up the grated stairs sent an electric stab through his hip. He didn't rush. Running on an iron ladder over thirty feet of stagnant bilge was how deckhands broke their necks, a lesson he’d learned forty winters ago when he was still an apprentice on coastal coal scows. Back then, before the reclamation companies paved over the green terraces of his home village, his mother had kept three wooden hives behind the vegetable patch. She had taught him to place a bare palm against the cedar boards at dawn; by the frequency of the buzzing alone, she could tell whether the queen was healthy or if the colony was preparing to starve.

He felt that same predatory hum now, not in bees, but in the shuddering radio carrier waves vibrating through the shipyard’s emergency bridge.

Inside the glassed-in bridge cab, Uncle Tan sat beside the old marine VHF console. His swollen knuckles were wrapped in damp linen strips against the monsoon humidity, but his hands rested steady on the frequency dials. Flanking him were three young deckhands—two men from the Batam lighter fleet and a woman who had spent five years running fuel barges down the Siak River—their faces slick with sweat, their numbered brass sortition tokens hanging from their necks by grease-darkened cord.

"Channel sixteen is jammed with corporate squawk," Tan said without looking up. "The lead ship is the *Caspian Venture*. Her master is Malek. An old company man. Thirty-two years under corporate charter."

Jin picked up the heavy black bakelite handset. The cord was coiled tight, stiff with age and salt. He didn't key the switch immediately. Through the salt-streaked windscreen, he watched the lead Apex tactical skiff. The patrol boat was pinned twenty yards off Berth 9, entirely hemmed in by three dozen wooden sampans, rusted tugs, and fiberglass runabouts. The contractor pilot remained behind his tinted polycarbonate glass, searchlight trembling, his twin three-hundred-horsepower outboards idling in neutral. He had the speed to cut down two or three skiffs, but doing so under the unblinking gaze of forty civilian bridge cameras meant an open admiralty murder indictment in three different sovereign jurisdictions. The pilot was frozen by his own survival calculus.

Farther out, the three ten-thousand-ton heavy-lift freighters loomed like black cliffs against the dawn light. Their navigation bridges were dark, running running-lights only, pretending to be unguided derelicts to skirt liability while their anchors plowed the seabed floor.

Jin pressed the thumb switch. The microphone click sounded like a dry branch snapping.

"Motor Vessel *Caspian Venture*, this is Keppel-Batam Harbor Refuge. Channel sixteen. Over."

Static washed through the cab’s overhead speaker, a hiss of rain and sea spray. Then a voice broke through—strained, clipped, carrying the thin, mechanical echo of an offshore bridge wing.

"Station calling, identify. You are transmitting on an international distress frequency. Clear the channel."

"Captain Malek," Jin said, his voice dropping into the quiet, rhythmic cadence he had used for forty years across typhoons and blown cylinder heads. "This is Jin Wei. We cleared berths together at Jurong in ninety-eight, when you were second mate on the *Straits Star*. Let the anchor chain rest, Captain. You’re sixty meters off the trench."

Silence hung over the channel. The only sound was the drumming of rain against the glass and the low, hollow rumble of the heavy-lift ship's diesels laboring against their dropped ground tackle.

On the radio, Malek’s breath came through as a ragged hitch. "Jin? You’re on the blacklists, man. Every terminal between Busan and Rotterdam has your name on the lien registry. If I drop anchor inside your coordinates, the syndicate cancels my mortgage indemnity. They’ll seize my vessel at the pilot station."

"They’ll seize her anyway," Jin said evenly. "Look at your charter party. Clause thirty-four. If you damage subsea municipal infrastructure under blacked-out running lights, your third-party liability insurance is voided on the instant of impact. You carry the entire civil judgment on your personal ticket. The syndicate won't pay a single guilder. You know how the paper is cut."

In the corner of the bridge cab, the three deckhands on the sortition watch exchanged glances. The young woman from the Siak barge leaned over the chart table, her finger tracing the red hatched line of the power trench where the depth sounder showed the seabed rising toward the shoals.

"He's stalling," she whispered to Tan. "His starboard winch is still paying out link. You can hear the brake drum squealing across the water."

Uncle Tan raised a hand, gesturing for quiet. His eyes remained fixed on Jin.

Jin did not raise his voice. He kept his grip loose on the handset, letting his thumb ease off the switch just long enough to let Malek hear the absolute absence of panic in the shipyard's control room.

"Malek," Jin said softly. "You have twenty-four crewmen aboard. Eight of them are cadets from the academy in Iloilo whose families haven't seen a remittance transfer in three months because the syndicate froze the clearing accounts. If your anchor flukes cut that trench, the tidal current will ground through your hull plating before the circuit interrupters trip. You won't just black out my dock. You'll cook your own auxiliary switchboards."

"I have orders from maritime receivers!" Malek’s voice cracked, the thin veneer of corporate authority fraying into naked terror. "They gave me a legal writ of salvage and clearance! You are harboring unsanctioned hulls!"

"Then look down at the water off your port quarter," Jin replied.

Through his binoculars, Jin saw the master on the *Caspian Venture’s* bridge wing raise his own glass.

Below the freighter’s massive flared bow, the water was no longer empty. The flotilla of civilian sampans and river lighters was fanning out. They weren't charging. They were drifting into a crescent across the freighter's stern, cutting their engines, dropping small stone killicks into the shallows. Behind them, the lead Apex tactical skiff remained dead in the water.

Then, over the open radio frequency, a new voice cut through the hiss. It was flat, carrying the dry, unaccented tone of a private military contractor who had spent too many years reading tactical operational manuals.

"This is Apex Interceptor One to *Caspian Venture*," the pilot said. His voice was piped through a cockpit throat-mic, mechanical and cold. "Be advised. We have forty-two civilian skiffs directly in our wash. Propeller entanglement hazard is one hundred percent if we apply thrust. We cannot secure your port side. Furthermore, Node Four has illuminated our hull with active safety monitors. If you drop additional ground tackle, you risk fouling our outboards. We are cutting our secondary throttles and holding position."

Lin let out a long, shuddering breath. "The mercenaries are pulling their horns in," she muttered. "They aren't paid enough to get sued for sinking forty fishermen."

"They're paid in corporate scrip that won't clear if the ship gets arrested," Jin murmured back. He keyed the mic once more. "Malek. The sortition council of this harbor has registered your ship under the humanitarian refuge articles of the Law of the Sea. We have water, we have power from the tidal turbines, and we have an open berth at the south mole. Cut your winch brake. Let the chain go slack. Come in on your bow thrusters and tie up alongside us."

A dead silence settled over the channel, so heavy that the rhythmic slap of small waves against the pilings seemed to fill the room.

For three agonizing seconds, nothing moved.

Then, across the gray surface of the strait, came the sharp, metallic *clank* of a hydraulic windlass locking into its pawl. The boiling foam around the *Caspian Venture’s* bow subsided into a gentle swell. The chain went limp, hanging in a long, drooping bight between the hawsepipe and the seafloor, inches clear of the subsea cable.

Uncle Tan closed his eyes, his lips moving in a silent prayer.

Jin lowered the radio handset, his shoulders dropping an inch. The ache in his spine was a dull throb now, manageable, like the familiar weight of wet canvas.

"He's holding," Lin whispered. "He's not dropping the second anchor."

Before Jin could answer, the red beacon atop the gantry tower flashed twice, followed by a dry, rhythmic staccato from the optical transceiver mast.

The young barge woman lunged for the teleprinter terminal on the chart table. The mechanical needles chattered furiously against the roll of paper, driven by the laser pulse from the Johor shore across the open strait.

She ripped the paper from the carriage, her eyes wide as she read the lines.

"Jin," she said, her voice turning completely hollow. "It’s not over. Look at the southern horizon."

Jin snatched the binoculars and swung them past the drifting freighters, out toward the open mouth of the Singapore Strait.

Plowing through the morning mist at thirty knots came no commercial tugs or tactical skiffs. Emerging from the deep shipping lane was a gray-hulled naval corvette, its bow wave tossing smaller craft aside like driftwood, its long-range search radar sweeping the Keppel basin with blinding, unceasing pulses of cold white fire.
