The twin enforcement cutters did not charge the slipway; they did something far worse. They throttled down to five knots, their reinforced steel collision bows carving two curling, slate-colored trenches into the chop, and swung abreast across the mouth of the channel.

The sudden backwash slammed against the rusted caisson gates of Berth 9 with the concussive thump of a depth charge. Deep in the drydock’s belly, three thousand tons of seawater surged against the concrete sills, rattling the timber shoring until the iron drift-pins screamed in their housings.

Jin Wei clamped his molars down on a bitter clove leaf, an old habit from his early days as a deck boy when bunker oil seeped into every ration of rice. The raw spice stung the roof of his mouth, cutting through the reek of ozone and scorched quartz drifting from the gantry tower above. 

"They aren't boarding," he muttered into the salt spray.

Beside him on the bridge wing, Uncle Tan adjusted his grip on the rusted iron railing, his swollen knuckles whitening under the strain. The old mariner squinted through the halogen glare lancing off the lead cutter’s flying bridge, watching the rhythmic pitch of its rounded bow. 

"Riding high forward," Tan rasped, his voice scraping like gravel tossed in an empty drum. "Look at the red lead on her waterline. She’s dumped her forepeak ballast to clear the southern shallows. She’s skittish in a cross-rip, Jin. She’s built to intimidate, not to fight the tide."

High above them, sixty-five meters in the air, the wind was an open hand shoving the crane platform toward the channel. 

Lin Zhang pressed her shoulder hard against the steel upright of the optical transceiver housing, her thumbs hammering a silent, rapid count against the brass buckles of her harness—three, five, seven, eleven—the old reflex she had relied on through endless fluorescent nights in the university’s lower sub-basement when the ventilation failed and panic threatened to close her windpipe.

"Link’s fading," she called down through the crane’s sound-powered telephone, her voice snatched away by the squall before it reached the horn. She hauled the handset back to her mouth, shouting directly into the brass diaphragm. "Chen! Rain scatter is eating the beam! Optical degradation is past forty percent!"

On the platform deck behind her, Chen Rui was down on his knees in a pool of foul bilge water, ignoring the salt spray soaking through the shoulders of his canvas smock. He leaned his face within two inches of the open junction box, his eyes narrowed, his nose twitching as he sniffed the rubberized seals. It was a nervous compulsion he had picked up in the refit yards of Pasir Panjang—burnt copper and overheating neoprene carried distinct scents long before an instrument needle flickered toward zero. 

"The mirrors are tracking!" Chen yelled, his forearms locked in stiff, aching tremors from wrestling the manual elevation gearing against the gale. "The Alpine node is holding the high-orbit bounce, but the photons are smearing against the downpour! If the cutters throw their wash into our line of sight, the whole packet stream collapses!"

Through the binoculars, Jin saw the lead vessel’s deck crew moving in tight, yellow-slickered teams. They were not unlimbering boarding ladders or preparing grapple mortars. Instead, two hydraulic cranes swung out over the cutter’s stern, lowering massive, segmented steel cylinders linked by heavy steel mesh into the churning water.

Floating containment booms. Industrial, harbor-grade interdiction barriers designed to corral burning tanker slicks—or to hermetically seal a contaminated berth.

A voice cut through the drumming squall, blasted from the cutter’s bridge-wing hailer. It was flat, mechanical, and scrubbed of anger—the unyielding cadence of an admiralty receiver reading from a legal script.

*"Berth Nine vessel and drydock complex. You are in direct violation of the Malacca Strait Traffic Separation Scheme. Under international admiralty injunction and port safety authority decree, your facility is designated an unnavigable hazard. A protective cordon is being established. Stand down all unmonitored power generation and prepare for harbor master mooring inspection."*

"Unnavigable hazard," Jin spat, the clove leaf pulp bitter on his tongue. "They aren't storming the gates because they don't have to. They’re walling us in."

A wet, dripping hand slapped down onto the bridge-wing scupper. 

Aisyah hauled herself over the gunwale, shedding salt water like an otter. She collapsed onto one knee, chest heaving, the black neoprene of her diving suit slick with grease from the outer pilings. She reached down with a trembling thumb to touch the smooth river stone she kept wedged inside the sheath of her dive knife—a rounded piece of quartz she had pulled from her family's drowned kelong traps years before the mega-terminals had dredged the sandbars out from under them.

"They're dropping sinkers," she gasped, her voice raw from breathing compressed air through a pitted regulator. She coughed, spitting a mouthful of briny phlegm onto the steel deck. "Two heavy chain drags... right across the deep trench. They’re pinning the outer perimeter."

Jin leaned over her, his hand gripping her shoulder. "The channel turbines?"

"Not yet," Aisyah panted, pointing a gloved finger out toward the churning gray rip where the tidal current boiled past the point. "The chains are fifty yards upstream. But the drag lines are weighted with dead-weight scrap. When the tide turns in forty minutes... the sweep will drag those lines right into the turbine runners. If the blades foul under full tidal torque, the main shafts will twist like licorice."

Without the tidal turbines anchored in the channel bed, the shipyard was dead. 

The salvaged battery banks in Drydock 4 could carry the internal mesh and the bilge pumps for twelve hours, maybe fourteen. But without the heavy current of the channel driving the generators, the reverse-osmosis desalination plant would seize. Three hundred mariners and dockers, bottled up behind a steel-mesh curtain with the monsoon salt fouling every freshwater cistern, would be drinking brackish sludge before tomorrow’s dusk.

"They want us to shut down the water intake ourselves," Jin said, the chill of the realization sinking deep into his belly. "No shots fired. No martyrs for the foreign press. Just twenty-four hours of dry throats and a fouled turbine, and we invite their tugs in to tow us out to the scrap anchorage."

From the top of the gantry, Lin’s shout cracked through the sound-powered telephone.

"Jin! Look at the second cutter! Look at her stern!"

Jin lifted his glasses. The second vessel, the smaller of the two enforcement craft, had backed down into the lee of the outer breakwater. Her crew wasn't setting booms. A heavy, braided steel towing pendant, thick as a man's thigh, was being payed out from a massive hydraulic drum on her afterdeck. At the end of the cable, a heavy three-pronged grappling anchor swung from a derrick.

They were not trying to hook the iron caisson gates. The gates were anchored into fifty thousand tons of pre-stressed concrete. 

They were lining up on the base of the gantry crane.

"The crane's footing," Jin whispered, the blood draining from his weathered face. 

The crane was an antique, riding on twin steel rails laid down four decades ago to service panamax container hulls. Its south bogies were pinned to the seawall, less than twenty paces from the open water. If the cutter snagged the lower truss with that towing hawser and backed her twin diesels into the six-knot ebb, the lateral shear would tear the mounting pins from the wet concrete. 

The sixty-five-meter tower would topple like a severed pine straight into the harbor channel, taking the optical transceiver, the laser link, and Lin and Chen with it.

"Uncle Tan!" Jin barked, his voice dropping all hesitation. "Get below to the ballast manifold! Man the manual valves on the seaward ballast tank! Aisyah, take two runners from the boiler gang—divert the steam return line from the auxiliary turbine straight into the fire monitor headers!"

Aisyah blinked, wiping salt from her lashes. "The monitor pumps can't reach their bridge from here, Jin. The wind's against us."

"We aren't aiming for their bridge," Jin said grimly. "We’re blinding their helm. Uncle Tan—when I drop the gantry wedge, I want forty tons of harbor water dumped into the south caisson. Drop the dock's bow three degrees. Pull the crane's rail below the wash line."

Tan’s eyes widened, then a slow, yellow-toothed grin broke across his weathered face. "The hawser won't bite if the truss is under three feet of surf. It'll slide right off the iron."

"Go!" Jin roared.

On the gantry above, the wind screeched through the cross-bracing. 

Lin stood poised on the narrow maintenance grating, her hands locked around the manual aiming yoke of the laser transceiver. The lead cutter had turned its forward searchlight bank directly into the crane cab, bathing the steel platform in a blinding, white-hot glare that washed out every reference point against the black horizon. 

Beside her, Chen was screaming over the howling squall, pointing toward the channel.

The second cutter had gunned its diesels. A thick plume of black exhaust erupted from its low-profile stacks. The three-pronged grappling anchor plunged into the foam forty feet off the slipway, its heavy chain paying out with a sound like tearing sheet metal.

The cable snapped taut. 

The gantry tower shuddered beneath their boots with a sickening, metallic groan. Two of the three-inch steel drift pins holding the southern rail shoe sheared off with reports like rifle cracks, the broken bolts whistling through the night air and splashing into the dark water below.

The entire sixty-five-meter crane tilted two degrees to the seaward, throwing Lin violently against the safety railing. 

The optical beam to the Alps snapped. 

On the diagnostics screen beside Chen’s knees, the green stream of cryptographically verified blocks froze, hitched, and died.

Down on the water, the cutter’s twin screws thrashed the harbor into boiling foam, the tow-line rising dripping and rigid out of the black sea, pulling the ancient iron tower inch by agonizing inch toward the edge of the abyss.
