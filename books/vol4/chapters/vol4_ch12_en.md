The grinding bite of tungsten carbide against the ship’s bottom plating sent a shudder straight up Jin Wei’s shinbones. It was not an explosion, but something far more intimate: a cold, hydraulic boring through the dead-center of sea chest three. 

“Aisyah!” Jin’s voice was a gravel rasp, stripped of breath by the sudden spasm in his lumbar spine. 

Aisyah was already moving. Her fingers, scarred white across the pads from an alternator flash burn, caught the four-foot cheater pipe resting against the tank framing. She didn’t speak. Under the violet glare of the grow-lights, her skin was slick with sweat and foul bilge mud. She rammed the pipe over the handle of the sea-chest emergency flapper valve and threw all forty-eight kilos of her frame against the lever.

Through the hull, the magnetic crawler hummed like a nest of hornets. 

*Clack-clack-clack.* The brass flapper swung inward, fighting the outward rush of displaced air, then slammed home against its neoprene seat with a dull, wet thud that vibrated through the marrow of Jin’s teeth. Beneath them, the core-drill screeched as it hit the backed bronze plate, shuddered, and sheared its bit with an ear-splitting snap. 

Silence fell across the ballast floor, save for the frantic darting of the silver estuary bass among the submerged mangrove roots. 

Then the radio on Jin’s harness died.

It did not click off; it dissolved into a violent, hissing wash of carrier hash. A wide-band RF carpet sweep. 

“They’re in the channel,” Uncle Tan wheezed from the access ladder. The old man’s knuckles were swollen to the size of walnuts, his knees trembling as he took the iron rungs one at a time. “No horn, no running lights. Skiffs. Three of them, hugging the caisson shadow.”

Jin hauled himself up the vertical ladder, his vertebrae grinding like coarse sand in a gearbox. He had grown up on the muddy flats of Pulau Ubin, where the smell of incoming rain always brought the scent of salted duck eggs drying on bamboo mats behind his mother’s kitchen; now, the air entering his lungs tasted solely of vaporized heavy fuel oil, ozone, and the electric sulfur of a closing monsoon front.

He broke onto the open weather deck of Berth 9 into a wall of falling water. 

The tropical squall had hit with the weight of an overturned lake. Torrents of warm rain swept sideways across the slipway, drumming against the steel skin of stacked containers. Out in the black chop of the traffic lane, two miles south, the running lights of three commercial gunboats churned the water into frothing phosphorus. They were running an interdiction perimeter, blacking out every civilian frequency from here to the Johor coast.

Sixty-five meters above the slipway, perched in the exposed maintenance cage of the gantry crane, Dr. Lin Zhang clutched the rusted guardrail with both hands.

Her eyes burned behind salt-streaked glasses. The migraine that had lived behind her left eyebrow for three days was now a hot spike driven through her temple. Below her, the shipyard was a dark labyrinth of wet iron; above her, the sky was an impenetrable roof of violet-black thunderheads. 

Beside her, Chen Rui knelt in three inches of standing rainwater on the crane platform, his forearms wrapped tightly in strips of black bicycle inner-tube to keep the inflamed tendons from tearing off the bone. His hands gripped the vernier adjustment wheels of the optical transceiver—a salvaged telescope barrel fitted with high-gain cyan emitter diodes, seated on an old surveyor’s leveling plate.

“The jammers took the UHF backhaul!” Lin yelled into the gale, her voice ripped away by the wind. She leaned over Chen’s shoulder, reciting prime numbers under her breath—*seventeen, nineteen, twenty-three*—the only mental tether keeping the height and the panic from emptying her stomach over the rail. “No telemetry from the tugs. The harbor mesh is severed.”

“Don’t need the radio!” Chen shouted back, spitting rain from his cracked lips. “Look at the meter!”

Lin wiped her glasses on her soaked shirt, smearing grease across the glass. The analog needle on the transceiver’s quadrant detector was twitching, dying, leaping back to zero. Salt crust was already fouling the quartz window. The heavy rain was scattering ninety percent of the light, turning the transceiver’s focused beam into a useless, diffused green glow that illuminated nothing but falling raindrops.

Down at the water level, the first tactical skiff struck the caisson.

The sound was a hard, rubberized slap against the outer timber fenders, followed instantly by the metallic bite of grappling grapnels catching the edge of the dock gate. Men in dark neoprene armor, devoid of flags or insignia, were hauling themselves up the wet timber pilings, carrying pneumatic cutters to breach the main sluice locks. If they dropped the caisson gate, four million gallons of harbor sludge would rush into Berth 9, capsizing the bulk carrier on its keel blocks and drowning three hundred families in the lower holds.

“Monitors!” Jin bellowed through a salvaged brass megaphone from the bridge wing. “Tan! Man the monitors!”

Uncle Tan didn’t run. His legs wouldn't let him. He leaned his entire body weight into the bronze wheel of the salvaged salvage-pump manifold. 

With a hollow, thundering groan that shook the pier, the twin tidal-fed fire pumps kicked in. Seawater, drawn straight from the four-knot channel rip, surged through the four-inch deck pipes. 

At the drydock rim, two dock hands seized the twin monitor nozzles. A solid column of green ocean water, roaring under two hundred pounds of pressure, smashed through the dark. It hit the lead boarding skiff not as spray, but as an iron bar. The vessel dipped violently, rolled forty degrees on its beam ends, and took fifty barrels of harbor water over the gunwales in four seconds. The boarding ladders tore free, spilling two tactical operatives into the foaming slipway, their heavy gear dragging them under until their auto-inflating vests violently popped them back to the surface, gasping and disoriented in the blinding wash.

The second monitor opened up, laying down a blinding curtain of heavy sea mist directly across the intruders' infrared sights, turning their thermal optics into a wall of useless gray soup.

Up on the crane, the wind sheared violently from the northeast.

“The mirror's loose!” Chen cried out. His wrench slipped on a corroded bronze bolt, stripping the skin from his knuckles. “The wind load is turning the gantry head! I can't hold the azimuth!”

Lin didn’t think. She threw her hip against the heavy steel housing of the optical barrel, pinning her small, shivering frame between the shuddering machine and the cold iron truss of the crane. The sharp edge of a mounting bracket cut through her linen shirt, slicing into her ribs. The pain was sudden, sharp, and cold.

*Twenty-nine, thirty-one, thirty-seven.*

“Turn it!” she screamed, her breath catching in her throat as the blood ran warm down her side, immediately washed thin by the downpour. “Rui, crank the declination! Three degrees up! The cloud ceiling is breaking over the channel!”

Chen Rui locked his bleeding hands onto the brass vernier. His muscles knotted, his teeth bared in a grimace of pure agony as the tendonitis fired white-hot along his arms. He forced the barrel up against the shrieking crosswind. One notch. Two notches.

Between two boiling banks of monsoon clouds, a narrow, jagged rift opened—a vertical chimney in the storm where the pressure dropped and the stars burned like ice through the thinning vapor.

“Now!” Lin choked out. “Fire the emitter!”

Chen dropped the manual knife-switch.

A needle of pure, concentrated cyan light stabbed out from the crane tower. 

It was not a radio wave that could be choked by Aegis-Omni’s carrier jammers; it was a physical lance of coherent photons. It sliced through the churning sea mist, pierced the eye of the cloud rift, and shot straight into the high-altitude atmospheric inversion.

For three agonizing heartbeats, the receiver needle on the quadrant meter lay dead against the stop-pin. 

Then, two thousand miles to the northwest, bounced from the orbital mirrors and caught by the cold granite spine of the Swiss Alps, the return carrier struck back. 

A pulse of deep, invisible infrared light answered the cyan beam. 

On Chen’s portable diagnostic screen, wrapped in clear plastic to keep off the salt rain, the raw terminal stream erupted into life. Not in graphics, not in corporate interfaces, but in a relentless, cascading waterfall of cryptographically verified ledger blocks. Detroit’s foundries. The Alpine hydro-penstocks. Berth 9’s battery banks. The three isolated nodes struck a single, immutable triangle of consensus across the curve of the dark planet.

The air on the crane platform smelled suddenly of scorched quartz and ozone.

Down below, on the slipway, the second Apex gunboat cut its searchlights. The tactical skiffs, wallowing half-submerged in the wash of the fire monitors, cut their grapnel lines and fell back into the boiling channel, their crews dragging their sodden boarding teams from the rip.

Lin slumped against the gantry rail, her ribcage throbbing, her chest heaving as she pulled the thick, wet sea air into her lungs. Chen fell back onto the rusted deck plates, his forearms locked in rigid, trembling cramps.

Jin Wei lowered his megaphone on the bridge wing. His knuckles were gray from gripping the rail. A ragged cheer, muffled by the rain, rose from the dark lower deck where the mariners stood watch.

Then, from the outer roads beyond the breakwater, a sound rolled across the water.

It was not the high whine of a skiff’s outboard. It was the deep, seismic, three-tone blast of an ocean-going salvage tug’s horn.

Through the curtains of falling rain, Jin saw them. 

Not tactical skiffs. Not light private craft. 

Rounding the northern light, two massive, black-hulled maritime enforcement cutters—each displacing three thousand tons, their bows reinforced with steel collision wedges—steamed abreast into the channel, their wake flattening the chop as their searchlights ignited simultaneously, locking their blinding beams directly onto the iron gates of Berth 9.
