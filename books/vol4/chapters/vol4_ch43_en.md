The spray hit the acrylic canopy like gravel fired from a sling.

At six feet above the open swells of the Chagos Trench, ground effect was not flight; it was a brutal, bone-jarring calculation against the curvature of the earth. The composite wing beneath Jin’s boots flexed with a sharp *crack*, shuddering through the airframe as the sea-glider skimmed a crest of black foam. 

Lin did not look up from the brass-bound slate bolted between their jump seats. Her fingers, stained around the cuticles with bitter gentian violet from treating saltwater ulcers in the Batam bilge, worked across the row of mechanical thumbwheels. A sudden updraft dropped her stomach into her throat, and she swallowed hard against the metallic sourness rising behind her molars. 

"Port engine bearing temperature is climbing three points every sixty seconds," she said. Her voice lacked its usual crisp precision, frayed by eighty sleepless hours and the dull ache behind her temples where her spectacles bit into her scalp. "The salt crust is baking onto the stator housing. If you pull us into another trough, the vapor seals will blow."

Jin didn’t blink. His jaw was locked tight, his teeth grinding on an old, blunt molar that had split during a drydock cable failure a decade ago and had never been properly crowned. Decades on rolling iron decks had taught him to balance on the balls of his bare feet, letting his knees absorb the sickening vertical jerks of the hull. 

"If I climb twenty feet out of the surface cushion, our drag triples," he said, his gravelly voice fighting the slipstream howling past the canopy seams. "We lose our lift-to-drag advantage, the reserve pack bleeds out before the Gulf of Aden, and we become a three-hundred-foot radar target for those skiffs. We stay in the trough."

A mile to their northwest, an arc of cold magnesium light cut through the mist. 

It was not a warning beacon. It was a high-intensity marine searchlight, its pencil-thin beam sweeping the surface of the Indian Ocean in tight, predatory diagonals. Behind it, the dual scream of supercharged marine diesels echoed off the water—the distinctive, razor-sharp pitch of Apex Tactical’s deep-draft interceptor skiffs.

The private contractor vessels weren't rogue raiders; their AIS transponders were chirping legitimate sovereign enforcement codes, broadcasting the Admiralty Red Notice issued out of Santiago. They were enforcing a lawful maritime lien on behalf of the creditor banks holding paper on the Malacca drydocks. To the men at those helms, Jin and Lin were not revolutionaries; they were industrial absconders piloting stolen assets across international waters, threatening the collateral underlying half the sovereign bond issues in Southeast Asia.

"Range?" Jin asked.

"Two nautical miles and closing at eighteen knots over our ground speed," Lin said. She reached into her canvas satchel, pulling out a handful of dried bitter melon slices—a childhood remedy her mother had pressed into her hands whenever fever threatened to blur her arithmetic during the humid summer exams in Wuhan. She chewed mechanically, the astringent bite shocking her nervous system back into focus. "They're attempting a flanking pincer. If they bracket our wake, the turbulence alone will trip the sea-glider's attitude gyros. We’ll cartwheel into the chop."

"They won't ram," Jin grunted. "They want the cargo intact. They want the physical paper."

He pointed a calloused index finger toward the center console, where a heavy, zinc-lined leather courier pouch lay strapped down with double steel bands. Inside sat the physical, ink-signed deeds of the Four-Continent Usufruct: the parchment covenants signed by the Detroit autoworkers, the Alpine hydro-syndicates, the Atacama lithium cooperatives, and the mariners of the Malacca Strait. 

"The ledger isn't just paper, Jin," Lin murmured, her hand drifting over the cold slate display. Her eyes softened with a terrifying vulnerability he had never seen in her during the shipyard sieges. "Look at the synchronization loop."

Across the screen, rendered in amber phosphorescent lines that flickered with every drop in laser signal from the trailing relay buoy, the global balance breathed. It was an intricate web of thermodynamic obligations, an invisible lattice binding human labor to the physical energy of the planet.

To the west, across the darkened continent of Africa, the Alpine reservoirs were feeding power downward into the Mediterranean lines, offsetting a cold snap that had threatened the olive-processing presses in Provence. To the east, the Atacama solar arrays, basking in high-altitude noon, were dumping synthetic ammonia production credits into the trans-Pacific shipping ledger. 

Every calorie was accounted for. Every watt had a name, a location, and a human face attached to its release. It was not money; it was the thermodynamic reality of the earth made visible—an accounting system where one could not steal from tomorrow to balance the ledger of today.

"Detroit's forging plants are running on the evening tide from the Bay of Fundy," Lin whispered, her fingers tracing an amber branch that dipped toward the Atlantic margin. "The assembly there held. Even after the federal receivers padlocked the substation gates, the shop floor voted to redirect their secondary battery banks into the municipal cold-storage lockers. They chose the city's insulin over their own tool-lines."

Jin glanced sideways, his eyes catching the faint green reflection of the flight display. "They took the risk because we promised them the Malacca grain ships would clear the blockade."

"And if we don't deliver these signatures to Geneva by dawn," Lin said, the cold truth settling between them like ballast, "the maritime lien attaches. The clearing banks will seize the bulk carriers in Colombo and Durban. The entire circuit collapses under debt default before the assembly ever sits."

A violent jolt tore through the sea-glider's starboard wingtip. 

A high-pressure spray curtain erupted five yards off their bow as the lead interceptor fired a water-monitor burst across their course. The sheer kinetic force of twenty tons of displaced seawater crashed against their side-skirt. The glider yawed violently, the horizon spinning forty-five degrees into a sickening blur of foam and black sky.

"Trim!" Lin yelled, her hands bracing against the dashboard as her spectacles were ripped from her ears, clattering into the footwell. "Port pontoon is dragging!"

Jin didn’t reach for the flight controls. Decades of working the rusted winch drums on typhoon-battered bulkheads had taught him that when metal screams under tension, pulling harder only snaps the cable. 

Instead, he reached down to the mechanical ballast dump lever—a thick, unpainted iron bar salvaged from an old river barge. He yanked it upward with the full weight of his shoulder, ignoring the sharp, familiar agony that fired down his lumbar spine from his crushed vertebrae.

A cloud of pressurized nitrogen hissed from the glider’s belly. Two hundred gallons of reverse-osmosis freshwater—their drinking reserve for the Red Sea crossing—vented in a roaring white plume straight into the sea-skimmer’s air cushion.

Lightened by nearly a ton in less than three seconds, the glider leapt.

The nose rose six feet, snapping out of the suction of the trough just as the second interceptor surged across their wake. The displacement wave from the tactical skiff passed directly beneath their keel, the turbulent backwash acting not as a tripwire, but as a hydraulic ramp.

The sea-glider soared into the clean, untangled air twenty feet above the swells, its twin ducted fans screaming as they bit into dry wind.

Behind them, the lead interceptor, blinded by the sudden fog of their vented freshwater dump and the boiling wake of its own partner craft, sheered hard to port to avoid a catastrophic collision. The searchlight beam spun wildly into the empty sky, illuminating nothing but low monsoon clouds.

In the sudden, eerie silence of the upper air layer, the glider leveled out, riding the smooth laminar airflow above the spray.

Lin scrambled in the dark, her trembling hands sweeping the deck plates until her skin found the wire frames of her glasses. She shoved them onto her face, blinking through smudged glass at the console.

The amber ledger on the screen was pulsing. 

A new, unpredicted knot had formed at the center of the network mesh. The line wasn't coming from Detroit, or the Alps, or the drydocks of Batam.

It was originating from the Gulf of Aden—from the unflagged, mothballed tanker fleet anchored off the coast of Yemen. 

"Jin," Lin said, her breath catching in her throat as she read the incoming cryptographic hash. "Look at the frequency. It’s not Apex. It’s not the Admiralty receivers."

Jin stared at the coordinates blooming on the nav-plotter. A single cluster of twelve low-frequency acoustic pings was arrayed across the strait ahead, blocking the mouth of the Red Sea.

"Who is it?" Jin demanded, his hand frozen on the throttle.

Lin pressed her fingers against the glass of the terminal, her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird.

"The tanker crews," she whispered. "The abandoned mariners. They’ve scuttled their anchor chains across the channel. They’re holding the strait—not for the banks, and not for us."

A single transmission flickered onto the screen in raw, unformatted text:

*PROVE THE COVENANT HOLDS WATER, OR WE DROP THE BOOM.*
