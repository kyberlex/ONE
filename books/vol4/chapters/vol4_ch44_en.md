The warning from the tanker fleet didn’t fade from the console; it burned there in jagged green phosphors, a tripwire stretched across the mouth of the Red Sea. But the water below was no longer their only border.

Two thousand feet above the swells, the glider’s ducted props thrummed with a hollow, laboring whine. The machine was never built for true high-altitude flight. Designed to ride the cushion of compressed air skimming the waves, every yard of elevation cost them dearly in precious battery reserve. The cabin grew bitterly cold, the damp tropical heat of the Malacca Strait bleeding away through the carbon-composite skin into the dry desert updraft of the Horn.

"We can't clear their boom," Jin said, his throat raw from the salt spray that had soaked him during the breach. His left hand locked on the dual pitch-trim wheel, his bad shoulder trembling as the airframe buffeted in the shear winds off the Yemeni cliffs. "If the tankers dropped their heavy salvage lines across the shipping channel, they’ll catch our rudders the moment we drop back into ground effect."

Lin didn’t look up from her lap. She had tucked a dried, bitter tea leaf beneath her inner cheek, a habit from her university laboratory days to stave off the sickening gray haze of screen-blindness. Her fingers, stained blue at the knuckles from an ink leak in her emergency nav-log, tapped furiously against the cracked edge of the optical coupler.

"Then we don't go down," she said, her voice paper-thin. "We climb."

"The cells won't take it, Lin. We pull forty degrees of pitch, the bus bars overheat before we cross Perim Island."

"Jin. Look up."

Through the salt-scratched acrylic of the canopy, twin pinpricks of high-intensity strobes burned through the twilight haze. They were dropping out of the upper stratosphere, descending from the north at five hundred knots—not tactical skiffs or port authority launches, but two sleek, twin-engine corporate executive jets, stripped of civilian livery, painted the dull, predatory gray of private maritime syndicates.

They weren't trying to shoot. International law over the strait was still an entangled knot of sovereign jurisdictions and maritime corridors, and an air-to-air strike would trigger automated military tracking from three nearby naval bases.

Instead, they were hunting with mass and wake.

The lead jet banked sharply, angling down toward the glider’s ten o’clock. Even at two miles out, the scream of its high-bypass turbofans reached them through the canopy—a heavy, tearing roar that vibrated straight through the glider’s aluminum floor ribs.

"Apex tactical charters out of Dubai," Lin whispered, her breath fogging the corner of her spectacles. She recognized the transponder footprint—or rather, the deliberate absence of one. They were running dark on the commercial ADS-B channels, masquerading as private corporate repositioning flights. "They aren't trying to blast us out of the sky. They’re going to pass fifty yards ahead of our nose."

Jin’s jaw tightened until the tendons stood out like cordage along his neck. He knew the tactic from old harbor tug encounters with oversized container ships: the invisible wall of wake turbulence. A forty-ton business jet cutting across the flight path of a lightweight, composite electric glider would roll them into an inverted spin before Jin could even kick the rudder pedals. At this altitude, recovery was physically impossible; they would hit the dark water of the gulf like a dropped stone.

"Trim down five degrees," Jin grunted. "We dive under."

"No!" Lin lunged across the center console, her forearm brushing the cold throttle quadrant. "If you dive, you bleed the remaining kinetic lift. You’ll stall before you reach the surface, and the tankers have the sea blocked anyway. We hold altitude."

"They're coming around for the cross, Lin! Ten seconds!"

The corporate jet expanded in their forward windscreen, a silver-gray knife cleaving the evening dusk, trailing twin vortices of kerosene heat that shimmered against the red sunset. The sound was an avalanche, pressing down on the glider’s plexiglass until it creaked.

Lin tore the protective rubber guard off the emergency civilian transceiver—an old, maritime VHF unit cross-wired into the glider's optical packet-mesh.

She didn't broadcast on the private tactical frequencies. She didn't appeal to Apex Marine or Euro-Asian Maritime Finance.

Instead, she flipped the selector switch straight to 121.5 megahertz: International Air Distress.

"All stations, all stations," Lin spoke into the collar mic, her voice shedding its tremor, dropping into the clear, measured cadence of open-sea emergency protocol. "This is glider craft *Perak Sanctuary*, maritime humanitarian auxiliary under the Hague Perpetual Asset Trust, currently traversing the Bab-el-Mandeb transit corridor."

"Lin, they aren't listening to distress calls," Jin shouted over the howling slipstream as he fought the controls, the sea-glider vibrating violently as the lead jet’s outer pressure wave brushed their left wingtip. The port motor surged, its thermal sensor flashing an amber warning on the primary panel.

"They aren't," Lin said through clenched teeth, her eyes locked on the amber text scrolling across her secondary display. "But the regional traffic towers are."

She leaned closer to the mesh link, her finger hammering the manual transmit key, broadcasting not just voice, but an automated cryptographic token directly into the public civilian aviation clearinghouses of Sanaa, Djibouti, and Jeddah.

"Broadcasting open-skies non-scheduled humanitarian transit under Article 5 of the Chicago Convention and the Hague Commons Maritime Protocol," she called out, every syllable carved with legal precision. "We are an unpowered and low-energy auxiliary carrying non-combatant civil delegates under international sortition mandate. We are declaring an active emergency flight corridor between coordinates twelve-forty north and thirteen-twenty north. We report imminent, deliberate wake-turbulence interdiction by two unflagged corporate transports maneuvering within civilian separation limits."

In the sky three hundred yards ahead, the lead jet suddenly wobbled.

Its right wing dipped sharply, the silver belly of the craft rolling upward toward the darkening sky.

"What did you do?" Jin panted, his hands fighting the wild updraft created by the jet's passage.

"The Hague Trust doesn't just hold ship titles, Jin," Lin said, the tea leaf bitter against her tongue as she tasted copper from a bitten lip. "The sortition assemblies in Geneva filed an open-access public trust charter three days ago. It binds every civil air-traffic authority that signed the maritime safety pacts. If those private charters cause an incident while we’re broadcasting on open civil distress channels, their parent registry loses its sovereign landing rights in every port from Rotterdam to Singapore before midnight."

The second corporate jet, closing fast from their starboard quarter to finish the boxing maneuver, abruptly pulled its nose up. Its twin jet pipes glowed dull orange in the twilight as its pilots slammed the throttles forward, climbing steeply to break off the interception. On Lin’s monitor, a cascade of regional air-traffic control interrogations lit the screen: Jeddah Control was demanding immediate identification and flight-plan justification from the private aircraft, broadcasting directly across the shared emergency frequency.

To corporate balance sheets, legal liability and sovereign airport impoundments were far deadlier than a lost contract. The risk calculus had flipped in six seconds.

The two jets banked hard toward the northern horizon, their navigation strobes vanishing into the haze over the Saudi desert, leaving behind only the dissipating trails of their exhaust.

Silence rushed back into the glider's cockpit—a cold, thin silence broken only by the steady hum of their twin props and the rhythmic rattle of a loose rivet in the port door frame.

Jin let out a long, ragged breath that rattled in his chest. His fingers slowly unclenched from the yoke, the joints swollen and white from the strain. He reached down with his right hand to massage his lower spine, groaning as the deep ache of the seat's vibration settled back into his bones.

"They’ll be waiting at the other end," Jin murmured, his eyes scanning the empty sky ahead. "They won't let us clear the Suez airspace without a fight."

"They won't have the jurisdiction," Lin said softly, leaning her head back against the worn canvas headrest. Her temples throbbed with a migraine that felt like a rusty nail driven behind her left ear. "Once we cross the Sinai, we enter the Mediterranean commons. The sortition delegate flight from Marseille is already airborne to escort us into Swiss airspace under civil neutrality."

She reached out and tapped the glass of the nav-plotter, where the green text from the abandoned tanker fleet below had stopped blinking.

On the sea surface two thousand feet beneath them, the string of rust-streaked, decommissioned tankers was swinging slowly on their massive chains. Through the gathering dark, their deck lamps flickered on one by one—not blinding tactical searchlights, but warm, yellow halogen work lights, illuminating a wide, unobstructed lane right down the center of the Red Sea channel.

They hadn't scuttled their booms to trap the glider. They had drawn their hulls together to form a floating harbor wall, barricading the surface waters against any pursuing tactical skiffs from the southern straits.

Jin watched the lights drift beneath their wings, a constellation of rusted iron and defiant human labor floating on the black water. For the first time in three days, the tight, defensive set of his shoulders eased by an inch.

"They opened the gate," he said quietly.

Lin didn't answer immediately. Her gaze was fixed on the far corner of the primary terminal, where the mesh connection to Geneva was supposed to be idling.

A fresh diagnostic pulse was shivering across the display. The signal wasn't green. It was a sharp, unyielding crimson.

Lin straightened, the fatigue vanishing from her spine in a single, cold jolt.

"Jin," she said, her voice barely a whisper against the hum of the dying batteries. "The delegate register for Geneva. It just dropped three hundred seats."

Jin turned his head, his brow furrowed in the dim cabin light. "What do you mean, dropped? The sortition drum picked them yesterday."

"The Swiss Federal Council didn't ratify the assembly venue," Lin said, reading the encrypted dispatch as it unspooled across the glass. "They've cordoned the Palais des Nations. The cantonal police just sealed the lakefront."
