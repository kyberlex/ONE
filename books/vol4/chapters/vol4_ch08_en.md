The name on the beneficiary line burned into the damp glass of the screen: *Harun bin Idris*. 

Captain Jin Wei did not blink. The salt crust around his eyelashes pulled tight, a sharp, granular sting that anchored him to the slick timber of the wharf. Harun had been his chief engineer for eleven years, a man whose left shoulder sat two inches lower than his right from hauling boiler pipes, a man who had gone down with the crushed stern of the *Bintang Laut* in the deep channel off Karimun. Jin had signed the maritime death declaration himself. He had watched the salvage barges abandon the wreck when the monsoon swells made diving impossible.

Yet here, registered thirty-four minutes ago in the digital clearinghouse of the Singapore receiver, was Harun’s personal provident fund account, accepting the debt-assignment rights to every mooring bollard and crane rail in Berth 9.

"Is it a mirror?" Lin Zhang whispered. She knelt beside him, her damp cotton shirt clinging to her collarbones, the smell of burnt battery acid and river mud rising from her hair. She reached toward the screen, her fingers twitching with a nervous, rhythmic flutter—one, two, three against her thigh, the old counting tic she used to drown out the hum of surveillance sweeps. "A shell trust? A dead man's identity bought off an admiralty salvage registry?"

"No," Jin rasped. His throat burned with the sulfurous tang of the back-siphoned harbor water. "The authorization key. Look at the trailing digits. Harun never used prime numbers for his security hash. He used the bearing angles of the Horsburgh Lighthouse. He said no machine algorithm would guess an old navigator’s compass error." He looked up, his gaze cutting through the greasy tropical drizzle toward the drydock. "He didn’t die in that stern section."

Behind them, the shipyard was stirring with a ugly, nervous heat. 

The high-pressure fire monitors had beaten back the tactical skiffs, but victory had brought no peace. Across the narrow pontoon bridge, the heavy iron gangway of Drydock 4 was groaning under the tread of dozens of boots. Four hundred stranded mariners, dock fitters, and fuel-depot workers were swarming up the scaffolding toward the weather deck of the *Pulai Indah*, the rusted, five-thousand-ton break-bulk freighter that sat propped on timber keel blocks like a slaughtered whale. 

They were soaked, shivering in the thirty-degree monsoon soup, and terrifyingly armed with spanners, iron pipe segments, and scrap plating. 

"They’re breaking the valve house padlocks," Chen Rui panted, jogging down the crane ramp. His forearms were wrapped in oily canvas strips to compress the flaming agony in his tendons. "The southern crews say we’re trapped like rats in a trench. They want to open the sea gate and flood the caisson so the receiver can’t bring salvage tugs into the inner basin. If they do that, the *Pulai Indah* will roll on her bilge keels and crush the bottom hull."

"They're panicking," Jin said, snapping the waterproof case shut and rising. His lumbar spine grated with a dry, sickening click that sent a white bolt of pain into his left heel. He leaned heavily on Rui’s shoulder for a fraction of a second, hiding the tremor, before catching his own balance. "If the ship rolls, we lose the tidal turbine cables running through her shaft alley. The whole yard goes dark by three in the morning."

"Then tell them that," Lin said, her breath shallow, her knuckles white against the shielded lantern. "Before someone throws an incendiary bottle into the bilge."

They crossed the dock floor in silence, the towering steel walls of the drydock rising forty feet above them, dripping with black kelp and barnacle crusts. High above, suspended between the gantry tracks, the storm clouds were bruised violet by the distant glare of the Jurong refinery flares across the strait.

On the main deck of the freighter, under the cavernous overhang of the forward crane house, the noise was deafening. 

Two men were shouting nose-to-nose over an open hatch cover. One was an Indonesian pump-man, his torso bare and stained with black bunker sludge; the other was a Singaporean marine electrician whose spectacles were tied to his ears with fishing twine. 

"We cut the shore cables now!" the pump-man screamed, slamming an open-ended wrench onto the steel plating. "When the harbor patrol comes back, they won't bring water nozzles! They'll bring legal injunctions and boarding cutters from the coast guard! If we’re wired to the mainland, they freeze our licenses by sunrise!"

"And if you cut the shore cables, the desalination plant stops!" the electrician yelled back, spitting through broken teeth. "My three children are drinking tank water that hasn't been boiled since Tuesday! You want us to drink harbor slime?"

A dozen voices roared in assent, another dozen roared in fury. The crowd surged, shoulder against wet shoulder, the raw somatic stink of fear, diesel exhaust, and unwashed wool suffocating under the tarp awning.

*CLANG.*

The sound was not an explosion, nor the report of a weapon. It was the sharp, mechanical strike of an engine-room telegraph gong, ringing out from the bridge wing.

Uncle Tan stood beside the brass pedestal, his swollen, arthritic hands resting on the curved wooden handle. The brass was green with verdigris, but the internal escapement was clean. The clockwork mechanism inside the housing ticked with a measured, inexorable clack-clack-clack.

"Five minutes," Uncle Tan called out. His voice was not loud, but it carried the gravelly density of sixty years in the yards, a voice that had cut through boiler room steam and typhoon squalls. "Article Four of the Covenant. The telegraph is set. The floor belongs to the watch."

The shouting sputtered. The pump-man wiped greasy sweat from his brow, glaring at the deck, his chest heaving. The electrician stepped back, breathing hard through his nose.

Jin climbed the ladder to the cargo hatch, the plastic flight case gripped in his right hand. He placed it squarely on the rusted iron coaming. He did not offer an apology, nor did he raise his voice in a rally. The rules were cut into the iron of the yard: five minutes of uninterrupted presentation, zero interruption, zero cross-talk until the brass chime fell.

"The skiffs were not coast guard," Jin said, the mechanical tick of the telegraph beating behind his words. "They were private recovery contractors hired by Euro-Asian Maritime Finance. They were repelled because our seawater monitors had more head-pressure than their hulls could take. But they left this."

He opened the case. The amber glow of the terminal lit the tight circle of faces: exhausted deckhands, young mothers holding infants wrapped in dry engine rags, aging crane operators with cataracts in their rheumy eyes.

"The warrant against Berth 9 has been bought," Jin said. "Not by the port authority. By an offshore trust operating under the maritime identity of Harun bin Idris."

A sharp murmur rippled through the older men. An oiler in the second row crossed himself. 

"Harun is dead," someone muttered. "The sea took him."

"His body was never recovered," Jin countered, his gaze sweeping the deck, refusing to let them look away. "And his trust just executed an administrative mortgage lien against this drydock. Whoever holds this terminal didn't come to sink us. They came to trigger an automated receivership default before dawn."

He pointed toward the narrow channel where the tidal turbines spun in the black depths.

"If we scatter into the mangroves in lifeboats, the trust assumes uncontested ownership of every kilowatt we generate. If we flood the dock to block them, we destroy our own shelter. That is what an algorithm wants: chaos that forces an abandonment order under the salvage codes. We have forty minutes before the tidal shift reverses. We do not run, and we do not break our own pumps."

The brass telegraph gave a sharp, metallic *ping*. 

The five minutes were up.

A heavy silence descended over the four hundred people on the deck. The rain pattered softly against the taut canvas above them, steady, cold, indifferent.

Uncle Tan reached into the wooden box beside the telegraph and drew the brass sortition drum forward. "Parity requires three," the old man said, his knuckles stiff as he spun the cylinder. Three numbered boxwood balls rattled within the perforated metal. "Three arbiters to inspect the log and confirm the course."

Uncle Tan opened the gate. Three wooden spheres dropped into his palm: *Nine. Twenty-seven. One hundred and three.*

Aisyah binti Rahman stepped forward from the shadows near the crane footing, her diving knife sheathed at her hip, her fingertips raw from high-voltage work. Behind her stepped a retired slipway master from Batam, and the young mother whose child was asleep against her collarbone.

Aisyah reached out, her scorched thumb brushing the edge of the tablet screen to verify the digital cryptographic ledger. 

Suddenly, the screen did not refresh its ledger.

The amber text flickered, collapsed into a single horizontal scan line, and flared blindingly violet.

A deep, pneumatic thud echoed from the bottom of the drydock beneath their boots.

The freighter lurched three inches to port with a sound like tearing sheet metal. Keel timbers shrieked under the sudden shifting of five thousand tons of deadweight. Far below, in the darkness beneath the hull, a high-volume sea valve was grinding open, throwing a massive geyser of harbor water against the concrete floor.

The terminal had not merely delivered an admiralty notice. 

It had executed a remote command line directly into the yard’s submerged pneumatic ballast manifolds—and the drydock was beginning to fill.
