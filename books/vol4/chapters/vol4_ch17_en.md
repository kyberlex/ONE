The monsoon squall had bled out into a breathless, leaden heat that felt heavier than the rain. Inside the vehicle deck of the *Dharma Jaya*—a rusted double-ended ferry moored in Berth 9, stripped of its passenger benches and retrofitted into an emergency nursery—the air was thick with the scent of wet rattan, cooling diesel, and damp laundry hung from overhead cable trays. 

Uncle Tan sat cross-legged on a split-bamboo mat, his stiffened knees grinding with the dull, familiar ache of forty years spent on slippery iron decks. His knuckles, swollen at the joints like gnarled mangrove roots, cradled a pair of blunt wire-strippers. Across from him, huddled around two upturned fiberglass fish boxes, seven children were arguing over pieces of buoyant blue insulation foam, their voices thin and sharp against the rhythmic thud of the lower bilge pumps.

"Not with your teeth, Mei," Tan said softly, his voice raspy from a youth spent breathing bilge fumes. He gently nudged a nine-year-old girl’s shoulder. "Copper will notch the enamel, and once you swallow salt water, the tooth goes black before you see land again. Use the sheer edge. Steady your wrists against the box."

Mei released the stripped copper wire, her lower lip trembling. She was the daughter of a second engineer who had vanished into an admiralty holding facility three weeks earlier when their container feeder had been seized at the eastern anchorage. Around her, twenty other displaced children sat in small clusters on the deck, assembling miniature boats. There were no plastic hulls or toy motors here. They worked with what the tide and the yard’s scrap bins yielded: hand-carved slivers of packing foam, flattened strips of copper salvaged from crushed terminal switches, and fingernail-sized photovoltaic wafers scavenged from shattered navigation buoys. 

Tan held up a laminated pictorial wiring card, its edges frayed and gray with grease. There were no equations on the card, only clean black lines showing how the red lead of the silicon wafer met the tiny vibration motor lifted from a dead pager. 

"Two suns on the wafer, one drop of pine resin on the stern," Tan murmured, showing eight-year-old Kian how to press the lead into place without cracking the brittle blue crystal. 

Kian didn’t press down. His small fingers, scarred by oyster shells from his family’s days on the southern stilt villages, hovered above the wafer. His chest hitched in a dry, silent spasm—the phantom startle of a child who had spent four consecutive nights listening to patrol skiffs circle their anchorage with searchlights blazing through the ports.

"It won't run," Kian whispered. "The sun is gone."

Tan looked up toward the vehicle deck's open stern ramp. Beyond the safety netting, the sky over the strait was a bruised purple, suffocating and flat. The monsoon clouds were trapping the refinery smog from the western islands, turning the daylight into an oily yellow glare. 

"It doesn't need the sun you can see," Tan said, resting a heavy, warm palm between the boy’s shoulder blades, feeling the frantic, fluttering pulse beneath the cotton shirt. "It only needs the sky to be awake. Look here."

He tipped the scrap foam boat toward the dull light coming off the bay. The salvaged motor gave a sudden, raspy buzz, vibrating so hard on the fish box that it skittered two inches across the plastic, leaving a faint scratch in the salt crust. 

The children gasped. For three seconds, Kian’s face lost its taut, hunted hollows. A faint smile flickered at the corner of his mouth.

A crash of shattered stoneware shattered the momentary peace.

Across the ferry’s open deck, beyond the hanging canvas tarpaulins that partitioned the nursery from the family berths, a man’s voice roared in fury.

"You took the three-quarter valve! Don’t lie to me, Basuki! You hid it under your bedding!"

"I took what was allocated to the lower deck pump!" another voice snarled back, raw and venomous. "My sister’s baby is coughing up brown phlegm because the bilge in Section Three is six inches deep! Your bunk is dry!"

Tan was on his feet before his knees could fully register the complaint. He didn't rush; sprinting across a wet steel deck only invited broken ribs. He walked with a heavy, rolling sea-stride, his bare feet gripping the diamond plating.

Behind the third canvas partition, two men were grappling over an iron pipe fitting. Basuki, an oiler whose freighter had been abandoned by its owners off Singapore two months prior, had the younger deckhand pinned against a structural stanchion. The younger man’s elbow was cocked back, a rusted adjustable wrench gripped in his fist. Surrounding them were a dozen women and elderly mariners, their faces pale, hollowed by weeks of rationed rice and the constant dread of the harbor police clearing the basin.

"Five minutes," Tan said. 

He didn't shout. He spoke at the exact pitch of an engine room watchstander reporting a hot bearing. From his belt, he unhooked a circular brass wind-up timer, salvage from a freighter's telegraph station, its glass cracked across the face. He twisted the bezel until the internal spring clicked, a heavy, metallic *clack-clack-clack* that immediately cut through the men’s ragged breathing.

He set the brass puck on an overturned steel drum between them.

"First stage," Tan said, placing his broad body directly between the raised wrench and Basuki’s throat. "Both hands off the brass. Palms on the bulkhead. You know the covenant."

"He's hoarding fittings, Uncle," Basuki spat, though his grip loosened. Salt sweat was running into his eyes, stinging them red. "We drown down there while his people sleep on dry wood."

"Palms on the steel," Tan repeated, his tone immutable as an anchor fluke. "Five minutes. Feel the hull. If the pumps stop, the valve doesn't matter, does it? Neither of you has slept since the storm surge broke the outer boom. Put the iron down, Harun."

The younger deckhand hesitated, his jaw working violently, before the wrench slipped from his sweat-slick fingers and clattered against the plating. He pressed his palms against the damp, painted steel of the hull, his forehead resting against the cold bulkhead, shoulders heaving in jagged sobs of pure exhaustion. Basuki cursed under his breath, turned, and did the same ten paces away.

The brass timer ticked with cold, mechanical indifference. Around them, the displaced families slowly let out their breath. A woman stepped forward with a tin mug of lukewarm rainwater and handed it to Tan. He took a sip, then offered it to Harun’s trembling neck.

"The machine shop in Drydock 2 is milling three new threaded sleeves from scrap shafting," Tan told the bulkhead quietly. "They will be ready before the evening tide. You fight over dead scrap because your heads are full of the patrol sirens from three nights ago. Leave the sirens in the strait. You are inside the gate now."

Before Harun could answer, a small tug on Tan’s sarong drew his attention downward. 

It was Mei. She was clutching her foam boat, but her wide eyes were fixed on the bilge-cooling trench that ran down the center of the ferry's deck, where runoff water drained into the lower collection sumps.

"Uncle," she whispered, pointing toward the water.

Tan followed her small, grease-stained finger. 

The test trench was four inches deep with gray seawater. Several of the children’s finished miniature craft were floating in the still eddy near the drain grating, their tiny motors buzzing weakly as they caught the yellow gloom of the sky. 

But Kian’s boat was not drifting with the faint current. It was vibrating violently, pivoting on its foam keel as if caught in a magnetic funnel. 

Tan frowned, stepping over the brass timer, which was still clicking down its five-minute cooling period. He knelt beside the trench. 

The photocell on Kian’s miniature boat wasn't just catching daylight. Its tiny green diagnostic photodiode was blinking in a sharp, rhythmic cadence—two short bursts, one long, then three rapid pulses. 

*Dot-dot, dash, dot-dot-dot.*

It was identical to the pulse sequence Jin Wei had spotted an hour earlier on the severed military umbilical pinned beneath the grounded barge.

Tan reached out and dipped two fingers into the bilge water. A faint, prickling vibration ran up through his calloused skin—an oscillating high-frequency current bleeding through the harbor basin, grounding itself directly into the ferry’s steel skin. It wasn't the regular thrum of the yard's low-speed diesel generators, nor the smooth whine of the tidal turbines. 

It was an interrogation sweep. 

A shadow cut across the vehicle deck's stern ramp, blocking out the yellow glare of the sky. 

It wasn't a cloud. 

Out in the main dredged fairway, half a mile beyond the scuttled barge wedged across the drydock mouth, three low-profile patrol interceptors had cut their engines. They weren't flying the blue flag of the maritime police, nor the red pennant of the port authority. Their hulls were painted an unreflective matte slate, their superstructure devoid of markings save for the bulbous, stabilized radomes of corporate tactical surveillance arrays.

They were drifting in dead silence on the falling tide, their bow-mounted hydrophones submerged, listening to the very pulse currently twitching the tiny scrap of foam between Kian’s feet.

The brass timer on the drum reached zero with a dry, final *ping*.

Across the ferry, the internal sound-powered telephone on the forward bulkhead let out a single, urgent shriek.
