The trenching barge did not show running lights. Out past the breakwater, its black hull wallowed like an iron whale in the cross-chop of the Malacca Strait, anchored by four heavy spuds driven deep into the seabed. Its diesel winch gave a low, seismic shudder that traveled through two miles of black water, vibrating up into the wet concrete soles of Berth 9. 

The hydraulic claw was already submerged.

In the operations cabin beneath the caisson lip, Lin Zhang’s voice was stripped of breath, flattened by the dry hum of overtaxed cooling fans. "The Batam-South subsea trench is failing. Signal-to-noise is collapsing across all twenty-four pairs. They aren't trying to board us, Jin. They’re dragging the trench."

Jin Wei hauled himself up the ladder of Berth 9’s master gantry, water streaming down the yellow rubber of his slicker into his boots. Every rung sent a dull, familiar spike of iron agony through his lumbar spine. When the pain spiked into his left hip, he clamped his teeth and counted silently to ten, matching each number to the beat of his pulse in his ears—an old diver’s habit learned forty years ago when clearing fouled propellers in the black shallows of the Riau Islands. 

Beside him, Aisyah binti Rahman scrambled up the vertical cage with the twitchy, furious grace of a rigger half his weight. Her hands were wrapped in torn electrical tape to protect the raw, blistered skin where high-voltage arcs had bitten into her palms six hours earlier, but she climbed without pausing to look down. When the wind caught her, she didn't fight it; she pressed her chest against the cold galvanized rungs and let the iron absorb the squall.

"How long until the claw bites?" Jin shouted through the roaring sheets of rain.

"Two minutes!" Lin’s voice crackled from the handheld clipped to Jin’s collar, the battery indicator blinking amber. "The physical trenching head is fifty tons. Once it hooks the bundle, it’ll shear the armored casing like rotten hemp. If that cable snaps before the optical bridge locks, the whole southern mesh goes dark. Batam, Johor, every refugee berth in the basin—we’ll be blind. Aegis will register the entire sector as abandoned derelict by morning."

"The crane transmitter!" Jin barked. "Why hasn't the rooftop optic fired?"

"I’m firing it!" Lin yelled back, her vocal cords raw. "The beam is out, but the return signal is dead. The collimator is completely blinded! You've got fifty percent scatter on the primary prism! It’s like firing a flashlight into curdled milk!"

Jin hauled his torso over the safety lip of the forty-meter platform. The wind here was not rain; it was a horizontal wall of warm brine ripped straight off the channel crests, moving fast enough to peel the skin off a man’s eyelids. Above them, the final twenty-five meters of the gantry boom arched out into empty air, a skeletal steel arm jutting over the void of the shipping channel.

At the very tip of that arm, bolted inside an aluminum weather-pod, was the Free Space Optics transceiver: a high-precision cyan-spectrum laser designed to bridge eight nautical miles of open water to the international shipping lane. It was their only way past the severed seabed cables, a beam of coherent light aimed at the masthead receiver of an independent freighter drifting under Panamanian registry in international waters.

The blue beam was there, but it wasn't a clean lance of light. It was a bloated, flickering cone of turquoise fog, bleeding uselessly into the monsoon squall.

"Salt crust," Aisyah said. She spat a mouthful of warm rain onto the decking. Her eyes were slitted against the spray, fixed on the transceiver housing fifty feet out along the narrow catwalk. "Three days of trade wind baking brine against the front glass, and now this mud-wash. The beam is refracting across the faceplate. It's scattering before it even leaves the dock."

"The wiper arm?" Jin reached the outrigger's maintenance rail, his fingers locking onto the slippery iron.

"Stuck on the reverse stroke. Solenoid must have fused when the generator surged." Aisyah reached into her canvas tool roll, pulling out a scraper with a brass blade and a bottle of laboratory-grade ethanol. "I have to clear the glass by hand."

"No harness rail past the forty-five-meter mark," Jin said, his hand clamping down on her shoulder. The rubber of her coat was freezing, slick as kelp. "The maintenance crew stripped the lifelines during the salvage auction last month."

Aisyah looked down at the hand on her shoulder. Jin's thumb was scarred by an old battery explosion, the nail split permanently in two. For all his limp, his grip had the heavy, unyielding torque of a mooring winch.

"If that freighter moves three cables north without picking up our carrier wave, it clears the channel," she said. She didn't shout. Her voice was flat, pressed thin by the roaring squall. "They drop their clearance papers. They sail for Tanjung Pelepas. Then we’re just another blacked-out yard waiting for the bailiffs."

She slipped out from under his hand before he could tighten his fingers.

The catwalk was sixteen inches of expanded steel mesh, trembling violently under the hammer of the monsoon. To her right, Drydock 4 was an abyss of black concrete and foaming scuppers sixty meters down. To her left, the open water of the strait churned with gray foam, the distant running lights of the container barge sliding steadily south toward the subsea trench.

Jin stepped onto the mesh behind her. 

His boots made a hollow clatter on the steel. He didn't try to look graceful; he kept his center of gravity down, knees bent, letting his bad spine absorb the sway of the crane tower. The structure was rolling three feet to either side with every major gust, groaning like an old iron bridge under a runaway locomotive.

"Jin! Go back to the platform!" Aisyah called over her shoulder, her voice torn away by the wind.

"Shut up and walk!" he rasped, his lungs burning with the sharp salt-air reek of bunker oil and ozone. "I’m your counterweight!"

They crept along the steel spine, one hand on the single waist-high pipe rail, until the rail simply ended at a sheared-off flange twenty feet from the tip. 

Ahead, the transceiver housing sat atop a narrow outrigger shelf. The blue laser erupted from its round snout, boiling the raindrops into tiny, sparkling flashes of steam, but the lens itself was an opaque cataract of white salt and greasy river silt.

From the radio at Jin's neck, Lin’s voice rose to a scream over the static: "Subsea pair twelve just dropped! The trenching claw hit the outer armor! You have thirty seconds before the physical core tears!"

Aisyah dropped to her knees on the bare girders. The metal here was slick with algae and diesel scum. With no lifeline, the wind tried to roll her sideways off the six-inch beam into the channel below. 

Jin moved up behind her, dropped his weight across the structural crossbeam, and jammed both his steel-toed boots hard into the triangular truss below. He locked his left arm around the main vertical stanchion, hooking his forearm through the iron gusset until the steel bit through his wet wool sleeve into the bone. With his right hand, he seized the heavy nylon webbing of Aisyah’s harness ring, hauling her tight against his thigh.

"Go!" Jin roared. "I have you!"

Aisyah leaned out into the open sky. 

The drop below her was absolute. She didn't look at the white crests breaking against the caisson wall; she fixed her eyes on the six-inch circle of quartz glass. 

She soaked the rag in ethanol, the sweet, cold sting of the alcohol cutting through the heavy smell of the sea. The first stroke of her scraper hit the glass with a screech of calcified brine. The crust was thick, baked hard by the heat of the transceiver's internal diodes. She leaned further, her boots dangling in sixty-five meters of empty air, Jin’s arm her only connection to the earth.

The scraper chattered across the quartz. White flakes of salt blew back into her eyes, blinding her with a ferocious, chemical burn. She didn't wipe them; she blinked through tears of agony, using the edge of the brass blade to carve the caked salt from the bezel.

Down in the strait, a sudden, terrible flash of blue light illuminated the water beneath the trenching barge. 

The subsea fiber had snapped. 

A muffled thump traveled through the sea, followed instantly by Lin’s voice through the radio, breaking into desperate fragments: "...cable severed... Batam is dark... we have zero... we’re dead..."

"Not yet!" Aisyah screamed into the rain.

She lunged forward, smashing the saturated rag directly against the face of the prism.

The ethanol dissolved the remaining haze in a hiss of steam. 

Instantly, the bloated, misty cone of blue light collapsed. 

The beam sharpened into a needle of brilliant, searing cyan. It punched clean through the monsoon squall, a straight, razor-thin line of electric blue that cleaved the driving rain and drove eight miles out into the center of the international channel.

For three agonizing seconds, there was nothing but the howling of the gale and the creak of the crane’s swaying boom.

Then, from far out in the black waters of the traffic separation scheme, a faint pinprick of green light blinked once from the superstructure of the drifting freighter.

The radio at Jin’s collar went dead for half a beat, swallowed by pure carrier-wave frequency. 

When Lin’s voice returned, it wasn't a scream. It was a choked, trembling whisper, vibrating with an impossible return signal: "Handshake confirmed. Sovereign mesh synchronized. The ledger is crossing the strait."

Jin let out a ragged, shuddering breath, his forehead falling against the cold iron of the stanchion, his right arm trembling violently from the strain of holding Aisyah’s weight. 

"Aisyah," he whispered, his chest heaving. "Come back. We have it."

Aisyah didn't move. She was still hanging over the edge, her hand braced against the cold aluminum pod of the transceiver.

"Jin," she said, her voice completely flat.

"Aisyah, climb back!"

"Look at the green beam," she said, her hand pointing not at the freighter eight miles out, but down into the channel directly beneath the crane.

Jin forced his head up through the blinding rain and looked down.

The green confirmation signal hadn't come from the merchant ship. 

Sitting motionless two hundred yards inside the harbor breakwater, masked by the shadow of Berth 11's coal conveyor, was a low-slung, matte-black patrol craft flying no flag. Its forward mast held an active optical interceptor array, drinking in the cyan data stream as fast as it left the gantry. 

Mounted on its foredeck, an industrial steel cable-launcher was already swiveling upward, its pneumatic cylinders charging with a loud, mechanical hiss, aiming directly at the crane's primary support leg.
