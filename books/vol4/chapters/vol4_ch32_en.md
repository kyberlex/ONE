The rain did not fall in drops; it came down as an unbroken sheet of grey iron, hammering the corrugated roof of Drydock 4 until speech was impossible below a shout.

Jin Wei did not wipe his face. The seawater and sweat running off his brows tasted of heavy sulfur and burnt oil, stinging the corners of his eyes where the crows' feet had cracked from three days in the salt gale. "They're running forty knots," he said, stepping fully inside the annex and pulling the iron latch shut with a hollow *clunk*. "Triple outboards on each skiff. Kevlar skirts. They aren't trying to board under the cranes. They’re angling for Berth 9, where the tidal turbine cables tie off."

In the corner, under the yellow glow of an inspection bulb wrapped in wire mesh, Uncle Tan handed the bundled infant back to the mother. His large, knobby knuckles—swollen like ginger roots from fifty winters in damp bilge spaces—trembled slightly as he straightened his spine. A muffled snap sounded from the small of his back, a familiar, dry pop that had accompanied every shift since the shipyards privatized in the nineties.

"Berth 9 has no seawall," Tan said. His voice was sandpaper against dry wood. "Only the timber pilings we drove last month to cradle the turbine pontoon. If they put their rams into those pilings at speed, the shear pins will snap. The whole array will drop into the mud."

"They don't want the pontoon," Dr. Lin Zhang said from behind the sorting table. She was kneeling on a damp rubber mat, her fingers white-tipped as she cinched zip-ties around an air-gapped optical terminal, wrapping the entire chassis in three layers of tarred sailcloth. Her temples throbbed with a migraine that had settled behind her left eye twelve hours ago, turning every flash of lightning into a white-hot needle driven through her skull. "They want the telemetry loggers. If they physically cut the fiber hub off the crane foot, the maritime registry in Singapore automatically registers an abandonment. The mortgage trustees take possession at dawn."

Aisyah stood by the workbench, her burnt fingertips stinging where the salt-laden mist drifted through the door seals. She picked up the heavy brass signaling key—an old manual relay wired directly to the crane tower's optical mirror. "The fog is too thick for Johor to see us. The cyan beam is scattering sixty percent of its lumens before it hits the outer channel marker."

"Then don't talk to Johor," Jin Wei said. He reached for his hard hat, hanging from a rusty peg beside the muster board, and jammed it over his greasy, thinning hair. "Talk to the roadstead."

Outside, the first engine roar ripped through the squall.

It was a low, snarling whine, the sound of thousand-horsepower marine blocks running open-throttle against the surge. Apex didn't use sirens; they used high-output searchlights that sliced through the driving rain like blue surgical blades. The beams swept across the dark water of the basin, catching the oily crests of four-foot chop before slamming against the rusted flanks of the drydock gate.

Jin threw the iron door back open. The wind caught it, slamming it against the concrete footing with a clang that rattled the grease guns on the wall. He stumbled down the wooden steps, his right knee hitching under the weight of his sea boots. Decades of standing on vibrating deck plates had ground the cartilage to powder, and every step into the tropical gale sent a cold ache shooting straight into his groin.

Chen Rui was already at the foot of the gantry, soaked to the bone, his forearms wrapped in vulcanized rubber sleeves. He had two fire monitors hooked into the auxiliary seawater main, their two-inch brass nozzles pointed toward the narrow throat between Berth 8 and Berth 9.

"Water pressure is only seventy pounds!" Chen screamed over the drumming deluge, his voice cracking. "The low-tide intake is choked with silt from the storm surge! If I open both valves, the head drops to thirty!"

"Don't fire yet!" Jin bellowed, lunging forward and grabbing Chen’s shoulder. The younger man was shivering violently, his teeth clicking together in rapid, involuntary spasms. "You’ll just wash their deckhands down. Apex runs enclosed cockpits. Those rams have six inches of hardened steel behind their rubber bumpers. You hit them with thirty pounds of water, they’ll laugh and shear the risers."

The first skiff broke through the outer curtain of rain three hundred meters out.

It was long, black, and sat terrifyingly low in the water, throwing two massive, foaming wings of white spray that glowed under its own prow lights. Behind it, two more hulls carved parallel furrows through the channel, their blunt, snout-nosed bows angled directly toward the wooden cradles where the community’s power cables entered the dock.

Jin Wei reached into his foul-weather coat and pulled out the small brass telegraph horn—an old, dented mouth-siren he had carried since his first third-mate watch on the bulk carriers. He put it to his lips, his lungs burning with the sharp, acidic reek of bunker dregs that forever haunted the mud flats, and blew three long, flat blasts into the wind.

Silence from the bay. Only the rising scream of the approaching outboards.

Then, fifty meters to the east, behind the black silhouette of an unpowered palm-oil lighter anchored in the shallows, an engine coughed.

It was a wet, heavy, two-stroke chug—an old coastal diesel with an unbaffled exhaust. A tiny yellow bulb blinked to life at its masthead.

Twenty meters beyond that, another engine caught: the high, rattling clatter of an air-cooled agricultural diesel mounted on the deck of an open wooden sampan. Then another. A rusted tugboat, its wheelhouse patched with blue construction tarp, kicked up a great boiling cloud of black soot from its funnel as its screw bit into the current.

"Jin!" Tan’s voice cut through the rain from the dock walk above. The old man was leaning over the guardrail, pointing his arm out across the roadstead toward the deep-water anchorage. "Look at the channel!"

Through the streaming rain, the darkness was coming alive with light.

They were not the blinding, cold halogen beams of private security. They were the kerosene lanterns of night-squid jiggers, the dim orange glow of sodium deck floods from anchored cement barges, the flickering LED work-lights of salvage lighters and bunkering sloops that had sat idle for months, trapped under the admiralty liens that choked the strait.

One by one, the hulls slipped their moorings.

They didn't come in formation; they came in an unruly, lumbering mass, drifting across the narrow neck of the channel like an island of timber and rusted iron torn loose from the earth. A seventy-foot flat-bottom ferry, its car deck crowded with drums of freshwater and scrap plate, swung broadside across the lead skiff’s approach vector, dropping its heavy Danforth anchor with a scream of chain that echoed off the crane towers.

On the lead Apex skiff, the throttle pitch changed instantly.

The snarling roar dropped to a harsh, stuttering cough as the pilot threw the triple drives into reverse. The black hull dug its stern deep into the chop, a wall of white foam climbing up its transom as it slid sideways, missing the iron flank of a diesel sampan by less than six feet.

The second skiff swerved hard to starboard to avoid the lead boat's wash, its powerful spotlights sweeping across a tangled wall of floating hemp lines, wooden gunwales, and tire fenders.

There was no gap.

A hundred hulls—battered coastal ketches, twin-screw harbor launches, timber longboats loaded with coiled mooring warp, and fifty-year-old steel tugs—had nested themselves into an impassable, floating reef across the entire two-mile entrance to the shipyard basin. They were tied horn-to-cleat with greasy polypropylene hawser, their engines idling in a steady, thrumming chorus that vibrated through the harbor water and through the very soles of Jin’s boots.

On the open deck of the ferry, three deckhands in torn yellow ponchos stood by the bitts, holding wooden pike poles tipped with rubber dock bumpers. They didn't shout. They didn't raise weapons. They simply stood with their shoulders hunched against the rain, their weathered faces illuminated by the blue searchlights of the trapped skiffs, watching the tactical craft bounce helplessly against the churning surface.

The lead Apex boat drifted within ten feet of an old river lighter. Inside the skiff’s tinted polycarbonate wheelhouse, Jin could see the silhouette of the contractor pilot, his tactical helmet illuminated by the green glow of his radar screen. The screen was solid red—an unbroken shelf of maritime targets, dense as a coral bank, running all the way from the mud shoals of Berth 11 to the deep-water separation buoy.

The contractor reached up, his gloved hand hovering over the engine toggles. He looked out through the glass, his searchlight beam resting on the open hold of the lighter.

Sitting on a wooden crate inside the hold, sheltered from the downpour by a piece of corrugated plastic, an old woman was calmly gutting small mackerel with a short, rusted bait knife, dropping the cleaned fish into a plastic bucket between her feet. She did not even look up at the searchlight.

The contractor’s engines dropped to idle. The high-tension hum of the outboards died into a wet, hollow gurgle.

Jin Wei stood on the edge of the dock, his hand gripping the cold, vibrating iron of the fire monitor. The rain was finally beginning to slacken, thinning to a fine, gray drizzle that drifted across the harbor like smoke.

Behind him, from the annex door, Lin Zhang stepped out onto the catwalk. Her face was pale, the skin around her eyes drawn tight with exhaustion, but her hands were steady as she looked past the sea of masts toward the outer channel.

"Jin," she said, her voice barely carrying over the idling diesels.

He turned his head. "They're boxed. They can't ram without sinking twenty civilian boats under the admiralty cameras. They'll have to back out on their auxiliary drives."

"No," Lin said, pointing toward the eastern spit where the deep-water channel curved around the southern light. "Not them. Look at the radar mast on the separation buoy."

Jin raised his binoculars, wiping the salt smear from the lenses with his wet thumb.

Beyond the civilian flotilla, beyond the stalled Apex skiffs, three massive commercial vessels—ten-thousand-ton heavy-lift ships flying the corporate blue-and-silver ensign of the maritime mortgage syndicate—had altered course from the main Singapore shipping lane. They weren't turning back toward the open sea.

They had cut their transponders, blacked out their navigation bridges, and were dropping their five-ton sea anchors directly into the trench where the shipyard’s subsea power link met the international grid.
