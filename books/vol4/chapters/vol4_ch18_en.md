Uncle Tan snatched the handset from the bulkhead cradle before its metallic ring could echo twice off the ferry’s steel ribs.

"Speak," Tan said.

Jin Wei’s voice came through the unpowered diaphragm, hollowed by copper wire and distance, stripped of all timbre except raw urgency. "They aren't boarding. Not yet. Look up."

Tan thrust the receiver back onto its brass fork and stepped out onto the car deck’s starboard apron. The equatorial sky was a bruised, sulfuric ochre, the humidity pressing against the skin like wet fleece. Out in the fairway, the three matte-slate interceptors had dropped their sea anchors, but their crews remained behind sealed polymer visors. They weren't launching boarding skiffs. They were loitering as relay nodes, their bow-mounted hydrophones and passive masts turned skyward.

A low, collective whine—like a hornet’s nest dropped into an iron culvert—descended through the cloud ceiling.

From four hundred feet up, dropping through the haze above the drydock’s towering gantry crane, came eight slender, carbon-composite airframes. They had no rotors; they rode ducted fans that hissed with the venomous, high-pitch scream of overdriven impellers. Beneath each belly hung a twin-jaw pneumatic cutter, designed not to shred flesh, but to sever fiber conduits and crack the quartz prisms of optical transceivers.

"Gantry tower!" Tan bellowed toward the machine shop hatch. "They’re going for the Batam beam!"

High above them, sixty-five meters in the air atop the skeletal arm of the decommissioned crane, Aisyah did not need the warning. She was already tethered to the catwalk’s rusted safety rail by an eight-millimeter climbing harness, her knuckles white around a pair of rubber-sheathed cable grips. 

The heat up here was worse than on the deck. The sun-baked iron radiated a suffocating warmth that tasted of scorched primer and gull droppings. Her work shirt clung to her back, soaked through, her chest heaving as she fought the dizzying vertigo that had haunted her ever since an offshore rig blast five years ago left her inner ear permanently damaged. She never spoke of the nausea, not to Jin Wei, not to the arbiters. She simply took short, calculated breaths through her nose, counting the rivets between her boots to keep the horizon from spinning.

Directly in front of her, bolted to a vibration-damped turntable, the optical laser housing was humming. Through its glass aperture, a pencil-thin filament of cyan light pulsed outward, bridging the eight-nautical-mile strait toward the island of Batam. 

A red LED on the transceiver’s control box was blinking fitfully. 

*Packet loss: thirty percent. Atmospheric scatter.*

Down in the ferry’s converted chart room, Dr. Lin Zhang jammed both palms against the teak counter to steady her shaking hands. Her migraine had settled behind her left eye like an embedded fishhook, pulsing in lockstep with the flickering diagnostic screen. She reached out with a trembling finger, adjusting the gain on the receiver array.

"Lin, report!" Jin Wei’s boots hammered down the companionway stairs, his heavy frame filling the doorway. The captain’s breath rattled in his chest, bunker-fuel cough rasping like dry leaves across sheet metal.

"The interceptors are bathing the channel in an acoustic baseline sweep to mask the swarm’s descent," Lin said, her voice clipped, breath shallow. "The drones are autonomous. Optical pursuit algorithms. They aren't looking at us. They’re tracking the cyan beam back to the gantry focal point. If those pneumatic jaws snip the emitter head, the Batam clearinghouse loses synchronization. Every grain shipment, every battery ledger swap with the southern islands halts instantly."

"How long until contact?"

"Ninety seconds," Lin whispered. "Maybe eighty. Aisyah can't outrun them down that ladder."

Jin Wei did not waste breath on a curse. He turned toward the sound-powered phone hanging beside the ship's telegraph. He cranked the rotary magnet three vicious turns and pulled the receiver to his ear. 

"Chen Rui," he barked into the brass cone. "Tidal turbine bypass manifold. The four-inch bleed valve on the thermal accumulator. Open it wide. Dump the dump line into the crane's dry riser."

From the cavernous engine spaces below the waterline, Chen Rui’s voice answered with monosyllabic fury. "Line’s fouled with salt crust, Captain! Handle’s locked solid!"

"Break the shear pin," Jin Wei ordered. "Do not argue. Use the slugging wrench."

In the damp belly of the hull, six feet below the harbor’s low-tide mark, Chen Rui dropped the phone and grabbed a twelve-pound iron hammer from the shadow board. The humidity down here was ninety-eight percent; condensation wept down the curved steel plates in dirty streams. The twin tidal turbines anchored in the fairway were pumping hundreds of gallons of superheated water through the closed-loop heat exchanger, holding the batteries at operating temperature while dumping excess load into the ballast sumps.

A thick bronze wheel, coated in white, chalky mineral blooms, stood between the primary steam chest and the crane’s vertical fire-suppression riser. 

Chen Rui wedged the box-end wrench over the squared valve stem and swung the hammer. 

*CLANG.*

The iron vibrated through the bones of his forearms, setting off an electric fire in his chronic tendonitis. He grimaced, taste of copper rising on his tongue, and swung again.

*CLANG.*

The salt seal fractured with a sound like a rifle shot. Hot bronze groaned. Chen Rui threw his whole body weight against the wrench, dragging the valve open four full turns.

Sixty-five meters above, the sea breeze suddenly died.

Aisyah felt the iron beneath her boots shudder. It was not the wind. It was an internal hydraulic hammering traveling up the crane’s hollow vertical legs. 

From the north, the swarm dipped below the cloud base. She could see them now with the naked eye: eight black wedges, flying in a tight staggered wedge, their ducted fans whining in unison. The forward drone had its titanium cutters extended, snapping open and shut in a calibration cycle. Fifty yards out. Thirty.

Aisyah refused to drop to the deck. She gripped the transceiver’s mounting bracket, stepped in front of the optical path, and pulled her safety line taut. If they cut the housing, they would have to cut through her chest first.

Then the drydock floor erupted.

Ten feet below her catwalk, the crane’s forgotten emergency misting nozzles—plumbed decades ago to wash chemical spills from container gantries—screamed to life. 

It was not cool sea spray. 

Fed by the turbine accumulator’s high-pressure discharge, a dense, roaring curtain of superheated steam and vaporized brine blasted skyward through the slotted nozzles. The column of white vapor hit the cooler tropical air and expanded violently, blooming into a towering, sixty-foot thermal wall that swallowed the crane’s jib, the transceiver, and Aisyah in a blinding, opaque cloud.

The lead drone hit the thermal curtain at forty miles per hour.

Its forward optical sensors, calibrated for the crisp contrast of the cyan laser against open water, were instantly blinded by the turbulent wash of ninety-degree vapor. The sudden density change and thermal updraft caught the tiny carbon ducted fans, stalling their intake vortexes. 

The machine banked violently, its flight computer struggling to compensate for the sudden loss of laminar air. The cutter snapped blindly at empty steam.

A second drone, trailing two yards behind, slammed directly into the lead unit's tail section. The collision was a dull, plastic *snap*. Both airframes tumbled end-over-end through the scalding fog, their gyro-stabilizers whining in panic, before plunging sixty meters straight down into the oily mud of the drydock floor, shattering on the granite blocks.

Inside the mist curtain, Aisyah could not see her own boots. The steam scorched her skin through her denim trousers, and the air was thick with the scent of boiled seaweed and sulfur. But the laser housing was still vibrating beneath her fingers. 

She wiped her fogged goggles with a wet sleeve. The red diagnostic light on the optical box flickered once, twice, and then clicked solid green. 

The steam was thick enough to blind the drones’ wide-angle cameras, but the narrow-beam cyan laser, pulsing at a tight terahertz frequency, bored directly through the vaporous tunnel, burning a clean hole through the fog toward the distant Batam receptor.

Below on the ferry’s apron, Tan and Jin Wei stood shoulder-to-shoulder, watching the remaining six drones scatter like frightened sparrows, their autonomous logic breaking under the thermal interference, climbing frantically back toward the yellow cloud cover to re-establish telemetry with the interceptors in the channel.

A ragged cheer rose from the car deck behind them. Kian and the mariner refugees were hanging over the gunwales, pointing up at the billowing white plume that made the old crane look like a volcano rising from the harbor mud.

Jin Wei did not join the cheer. 

He lowered his binoculars, his scarred collarbone tightening under his salt-stiff collar.

Out in the dredged fairway, the three matte-slate interceptors were not retreating. Seeing their aerial probe neutralized, their low-profile hulls suddenly surged forward, twin waterjets churning the dirty water into white foam. 

They weren't trying to cut the optical line anymore. 

Steering hard around the scuttled barge, the three craft were cutting straight for Drydock 4’s inner gate, their heavy steel bow-bumpers angled to crush the wooden pontoon barriers that protected the ferry’s exposed cooling lines.

And on the lead interceptor’s forward deck, two crewmen in charcoal tactical gear were unlimbering a heavy, pneumatic line-throwing cannon, aiming its barbed grapple directly at the ferry’s main sea-chest intake.
