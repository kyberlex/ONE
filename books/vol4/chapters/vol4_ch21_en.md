The squall took the shipyard broadside. Rain hit the galvanized roof of Berth 8 with the dull roar of gravel tipped into an empty hopper, washing away the grease-sheen on the staging planks in foaming gray ribbons. 

Kasim didn’t use the gangway. He vaulted the low gunwale of the shallow-draft work skiff, his bare feet slapping the ridged aluminum decking. Behind him came Lin, both arms clamped across a battered, foam-lined cooler of pediatric antivenom and refrigeration-dependent cephalosporins. Her knuckles were bloodless from the grip, her wet spectacles sliding down the bridge of her nose.

"Don't set it on the bare deck," Aisyah hissed from the stern, not looking up. She was already on her knees in bilge water, hauling soaked lengths of jute burlap out of a brine bucket. "The battery hull conducts. If the casing warms those vials by two degrees, the whole batch curdles before dawn."

Kasim caught the cooler by its nylon webbing, wedging it onto two split rubber tires lashed amidships. "Tide’s dropping four inches every ten minutes," he said, his voice flat, stripped of everything but the rhythm of the water. "If the Apex skiffs have jammed the container across the main gut, they think they've bottled the basin. They aren't looking at the stilt-creeks behind the fish traps."

"Not with binoculars, they aren't," Aisyah muttered. Her fingers, raw and peeling at the tips from old electrical flash burns, worked furiously around the rectangular battery bank nestled beneath the thwart. She was lashing blocks of dry, porous clay and wet swamp silt directly to the aluminum heat sinks. "Aegis-Omni’s thermal bird clears the horizon in nine minutes. If that pack radiates more than twenty degrees above the water temperature, we’ll show up on their terminal like a flare in an alley."

Lin dropped onto the thwart beside the cooler, her breathing shallow, rattled by the migraine behind her temples. The air smelled of rotting mangrove tannins, ozone, and the sour chemical bite of heated insulation. "Can we run dead silent?"

"No such thing as dead silent in three feet of water," Kasim said. 

He cast off the bowline. The current caught the blunt nose immediately, slewing them out from beneath the shelter of the crane tracks into the churning black gut of the channel. 

The mainland lights were already dying behind them, swallowed by the monsoon squall. Ahead lay the eastern lip of Pulau Semakau: a wall of tangled nipa palms and stilt-rooted red mangroves that looked, in the darkness, like an unbroken palisade of iron rebar. 

Kasim took the tiller of the low-profile electric drive. He didn't engage the throttle to its stop. He nudged it forward until the propeller blades gave a low, dull hum through the bottom plating—a sound felt in the shins rather than heard. The skiff surged forward, hugging the shelving mudbank where the deeper patrol hulls dared not venture.

To their left, four cables out in the open shipping fairway, the scuttled container ship lay wedged at an angle across the reef channel. Even through the sheets of downpour, the red and green running lights of two Apex tactical skiffs were visible, idling against the rusted hull plates like predatory water beetles, their twin outboards muttering in low, rhythmic growls. They were waiting for anyone attempting to run the deep-water gate.

"Five minutes to overhead transit," Lin whispered, checking the mechanical pocket chronometer tied to her wrist with sailing twine. Her fingers twitched against her oilskin coat, a nervous tic left over from months in windowless logistics basements where the hum of cooling fans had measured out the hours of her life. "Once the sweep starts, it lasts three hundred seconds. If we're under the canopy by then, the foliage scatters the signature."

"If the prop doesn't bind," Aisyah said. She had her palm flat against the motor housing, monitoring its temperature by touch. The skin of her forearm was covered in goosebumps, shivering against the tepid rain. "The silt’s thick here. The cooling intake is drawing mud."

"Then blow it clear with the hand pump," Kasim replied without turning his head. 

His eyes were locked on a faint, irregular break in the tree line—a notch where the brackish runoff of the high interior creeks cut a passage through the roots. As a boy, before the petrochemical berths had swallowed the coastline in corrugated steel and high-pressure bunkering pipes, he had set wire crab pots in these cuts with his uncle. Back then, the old man had warned him that the mud had an appetite: lose your way on a falling tide, and the swamp would suck the shoes off your feet and leave your boat perched high on stilt-roots like a crow’s nest.

The skiff slipped beneath the overhanging boughs. 

Instantly, the roar of the squall softened, transformed into a continuous, heavy patter against the thick canopy of leaves. The temperature jumped three degrees; the air turned stagnant, thick with the sulfurous stench of anaerobic silt and decaying sea-grapes. The branches scraped against the aluminum gunwales with the screech of fingernails on chalkboard.

"Cut the power," Kasim commanded softly.

Aisyah pulled the mechanical disconnect pin. The hum of the motor died. The skiff slid forward on its own momentum, the hull brushing past thick, gnarled roots that dipped into the black water like spiders' legs.

"Satellite’s rising," Lin said. Her voice was barely a breath. "Bearing north-northwest. It’s sweeping the sector now."

Kasim leaned forward, reaching out with a bamboo setting pole he had pulled from the bilge. He braced the tip against the root of an old mangrove apple, checking their drift. The current here was counter-intuitive, split by the rising mud shoals into a dozen conflicting eddies that wanted to twist the skiff sideways and jam it broadside between two trunks.

Above them, through the gaps in the dripping leaves, the sky was a bruised, slate-colored purple. They couldn't see the satellite—hundreds of miles up in the cold vacuum, its cryogenic lenses mapping every heat differential on the strait's surface down to a fraction of an inch—but they felt it like an invisible weight pressing on the back of their necks. 

Every hot wire, every friction point on the propeller shaft, every lungful of thirty-seven-degree air Lin exhaled was a beacon waiting to be indexed by an algorithmic queue in a temperature-controlled server vault in Jurong.

"Motor housing is spiking," Aisyah whispered. Panic tightened the edges of her words. She was feeling the aluminum fins beneath the burlap. "The mud jammed the cooling channel before we killed it. The heat sink is bleeding into the mount."

"Burlap," Kasim said, his teeth clenched as he fought the pole against the suction of the clay bed. "Douse it."

Lin didn't hesitate. She plunged both hands into the dark, foul water over the side, scooping up handfuls of cold river-bottom sludge, slapping it directly onto the top of the casing. The stench was sickening—ancient rot and iron rust—but the wet muck hissed softly, swallowing the escaping heat before it could bloom upward into the gap between the branches.

For forty seconds, no one moved. The only sound was the dripping of rain from Lin's chin onto the lid of the cooler, and the thick, wet sucking sound of the tide draining beneath their keel.

The hull gave a sudden, grinding shudder.

Kasim froze, his weight pressed into the pole. The skiff had stopped. 

"We’re aground," Aisyah breathed.

"Not aground," Kasim said. He looked down over the low side. In the gloom, the dark form of a submerged tree trunk—a fallen nipah palm, long drowned and calcified by barnacles—lay wedged across the channel, six inches below the surface. The aluminum bow had ridden straight onto it, the weight of the battery pack pinning them down.

Lin checked the dial on her wrist. "Two minutes left in the transit window. If we thrash, the thermal sensors catch the water disturbance."

"We don't thrash," Kasim said. He laid the pole quietly across the thwarts. "We get out."

Without waiting for an answer, he swung his legs over the side. He sank instantly to his thighs in the thick, viscous mud, the cold muck filling his canvas trousers like lead shot. His feet slid on the slimy curve of the sunken log, searching for purchase among razor-sharp clusters of oyster shells. 

Aisyah followed him on the starboard side, slipping into the black water with a sharp intake of breath as the shells tore through the knee of her work trousers. 

Together, their shoulders braced against the aluminum bow plates, they pushed. 

The mud fought them with terrifying mechanical leverage. It wasn't just friction; it was a vacuum, holding the flat bottom of the skiff like a rubber suction cup. Kasim’s breath tore through his nostrils in ragged, muffled gasps. His bad shoulder—stiffened by years of deck-winch strain—burned as if someone were driving a hot awl through the socket. 

Beside him, Aisyah’s face was twisted in the dark, her neck cords standing out, her scarred fingers slipping against the wet metal.

"On three," Kasim hissed. "Lift. Don't push. Lift the nose."

Lin shifted her weight toward the stern, pulling the cooler against her belly to counter the balance, her eyes wide behind her rain-streaked lenses.

"One," Kasim grunted. "Two... heave."

They threw their spines into it. The bow rose an inch, two inches. The bottom scraped over the barnacled wood with a sound like tearing tin. The suction broke with a wet, hollow *thwip*, and the skiff shot forward into the deeper pool beyond the log.

Aisyah scrambled back over the gunwale, coughing up brackish water she had swallowed when her footing slipped. Kasim dragged himself up after her, his shins bleeding freely from shallow oyster cuts that stung with the sharp bite of sea salt.

Lin let out a long, trembling exhalation, tapping the crystal of her watch. "Window closed. The bird is over the Malacca approaches."

Kasim lay on the deck boards for three heartbeats, letting the cold rain wash the mud from his face. Then he sat up, wiping his eyes with the back of a bloody wrist.

"Aisyah. Clear the intake."

She nodded, her teeth chattering so hard she couldn't speak, and reached for the wire clearing rod under the seat.

Before her hand touched the tool, the darkness shattered.

Fifty yards behind them, back where the creek mouth opened into the bay, an engine revved—not the muffled growl of their electric drive, but the sharp, metallic scream of a twin-rotor interceptor skiff throttling up to plan speed. A blue halogen beam tore through the mangrove canopy, slicing between the gnarled branches like a broadsword, illuminating the rising steam of their own breath against the black leaves.

The patrol boat hadn't used the satellite. 

It was drifting on a passive acoustic hydrophone, listening for the scrape of aluminum against drowned wood—and it had just found it.
