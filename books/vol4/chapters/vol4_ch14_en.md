The safety harness bit into Lin’s ribs as the gantry lurched, the steel catwalk slanting under her boots like the deck of a foundering barge. Below, the hawser hummed with catastrophic tension, vibrating so violently it sprayed an aerosol of seawater off its braided strands.

"Jin!" Chen screamed, his knuckles white around the railing, his thumb clamped between his teeth until the skin split. "The truss is twisting! If the lower shoe goes, the whole tower rolls into the slipway!"

Lin didn't shout. Her breath came in shallow, ragged hitches, the copper taste of a panic attack coating her tongue. Her vision flared at the periphery—the telltale shimmering zigzag of an oncoming migraine brought on by forty straight hours staring through optical collimators. With her free hand, she pressed a blunt thumbnail deep into the tendon of her left wrist, an old habit from her youth in the suffocating aisles of her mother’s textile stockroom, grounding herself in localized pain to stop her vision from tunneling to black.

Sixty meters down, the dock responded.

A low, hydraulic thud reverberated through the hollow caissons. Uncle Tan had dropped the south ballast valve. Harbor water, black and thick with river silt, surged into the lower chambers. The dock's seaward edge sagged instantly, dipping three degrees into the swell.

At the same instant, a deafening hiss tore through the rain. Aisyah threw the diverted bypass. High-pressure steam from the auxiliary turbine slammed into the fire monitor lines, mixing with the raw saltwater feed. What erupted from the forward nozzles was not a clean jet, but a roaring, boiling blizzard of scalding fog and vaporized spray.

The wind caught the plume, rolling it directly into the cutter’s wheelhouse.

Through the whiteout, the cutter’s searchlights scattered into a blinding, featureless haze. Blind at the helm and caught off-balance as the dock’s iron truss dipped beneath the surging surf, the cutter’s pilot sheared hard to port to avoid grounding on the slipway sill. 

The tow-line slackened by an inch. It was all the physics needed.

With a shriek of tearing zinc, the greased grappling fluke lost its purchase on the submerged iron beam. The cable whipped free with the crack of a snapped bullwhip, slicing across the water and leaving a smoking furrow of white froth before sinking into the channel. 

The 65-meter crane snapped back against its remaining structural stops. The impact rattled Lin’s teeth in her skull; her head cracked against the transceiver mount, sending stars cascading across her eyes.

"Clear!" Jin’s voice rasped through the copper voice-pipe from the lower quay, thick and wet with his chronic bunker-oil cough. "Hawser slipped! Aisyah, kill the steam before you melt the pump gaskets!"

The roar died, replaced by the relentless drum of the monsoon rain hammering the rusted corrugated roofs of the shipyard.

Chen slumped onto the maintenance grating, his chest heaving. "The beam," he choked out, wiping stinging salt from his eyes. "The Alpine link. It’s completely dead. We lost the carriage lock."

Lin pulled herself up, ignoring the hot trickle of blood running from her hairline down into her left eyebrow. She checked the optical transceiver’s azimuth ring. The heavy brass gimbal was warped three millimeters out of true where the sheared pins had buckled the deck plates.

Before she could reach for the alignment wrench, the yard’s open-channel maritime receiver began to squawk. The audio did not come through the encrypted mesh; it blasted over the international VHF distress frequency, cold, sterile, and perfectly synthesized.

"Attention all vessels in the Singapore and Malacca Straits Traffic Separation Scheme. By decree of the Admiralty Division and Euro-Asian Maritime Finance, the facility designated Keppel-Batam Drydock Four is declared an abandoned maritime hazard and an illegal salvage operation. All hull identification transfers, cargo clearances, and auxiliary power off-takes originating from this coordinate are nullified under international maritime lien. Vessels attempting to berth or discharge cargo will be deemed co-conspirators in salvage theft and subject to immediate impoundment."

Chen stared at the speaker. "Salvage theft? They financed those docks thirty years ago. They were written off as scrap after the second harbor subsidence!"

"They don't care about the scrap," Lin said, her voice stripped of inflection. She wiped her forehead with the sleeve of her grease-stained jacket. "They care about the precedent. If an unbonded crew can bring a dead yard back onto the ledger without their debt covenants, their whole risk-pricing model collapses."

Jin Wei’s heavy boots rang out on the iron ladder below. He hauled himself up to the lower platform, chewing methodically on a scrap of dried bitter melon rind—a somatic defense against the sulfur vapor that perpetually burned his lungs. In his hand, he held a carbon-copy clipboard scribbled with pencil marks.

"Two of the grain freighters from the Mekong delta just altered course," Jin said without greeting. "They picked up the admiralty warning. Their captains are refusing to drop anchor in our basin. They’re turning back toward the commercial roads. If they don't discharge their rice into our grain elevators by morning, their crews won't get paid, and the settlement at Berth Nine runs out of meal in three days."

"What about our battery reserves?" Lin asked, leaning against the crane cab to steady her trembling knees.

"Down to twenty-two percent," Jin replied grimly. "The tidal turbine in the south channel took a submerged drift log twenty minutes ago. The sheer-pin broke on the rotor hub. We're running purely on the tugboat packs, and the reverse-osmosis pumps are drinking four hundred amps just to keep fresh water flowing to the clinic."

They were being starved out in real time. Not by shells or torpedoes, but by the quiet, mathematical closure of maritime clearing accounts.

Lin looked past the dripping steel girders toward the south. Geneva was obscured by five hundred miles of storm front and severed glass lines across the Indian Ocean basin. But the Pacific horizon was different.

"We don't route through Europe," Lin said suddenly.

Chen frowned. "What are you talking about? The validation relay is in the Alps. The whole clearing tree—"

"The clearing tree is a distributed mesh, not a cathedral," Lin interrupted, her mind racing past the throbbing ache behind her temples. "Who holds the bulk of the unrefined energy buffer in the southern hemisphere right now? It isn't the North Atlantic banks."

Chen’s eyes widened. "The Andean mineral syndicate. The lithium salt nodes in the Atacama."

"They’re choked on dry inventory," Lin said, pulling the laser transceiver yoke toward her chest. Her shoulder muscles burned with exhaustion. "The regional haulage syndicates locked their railheads two weeks ago because the sovereign clearinghouse refused to settle their fuel vouchers. They have forty thousand tons of refined battery-grade carbonate sitting in rail spurs under the desert sun, completely immobilized because they lack spare parts and desalinated process water for their refining loops."

"And we have three million gallons of fresh water sitting in the deep ballast tanks," Jin murmured, his eyes narrowing. "And twelve empty bulk drydocks with heavy machine lathes."

"It’s an unbonded bilateral swap," Lin said, her fingers already working the vernier dials on the transceiver's manual pedestal. "Usufruct exchange. We grant them repair slots and desalinated water certificates for their trans-Pacific bulk carriers; they assign direct title to two thousand tons of dry-cell lithium salt to the Batam regional commons. No commercial banks. No clearinghouses. No maritime mortgage liens."

"Lin, the beam path to the Pacific transponder tower is over nine miles across open water," Chen warned, checking the handheld optical power meter. "The humidity is ninety-eight percent. The fog from the monitors hasn't even cleared. You’ll get sixty percent scatter on the cyan spectrum. The packet loss will tear the transaction blocks to pieces."

"Then we tune it by hand," Lin said.

She bent over the eyepiece. There was no automated tracking; the servos had burned out when the gantry shifted. Lin rested her cheek against the cold, damp iron of the mounting bracket. She remembered the nights during her apprenticeship, sitting in windowless transit stations, calculating coordinate vectors on greaseproof paper to pass the time. She knew the drift of this tower: two millimeters south on the ebb tide, half a millimeter east with every ten knots of westerly squall.

She sighted the faint, pulsing red navigation marker on the distant horizon of the Indonesian headland—the mountaintop relay tower that bypassed the coastal fiber choke points.

"Chen. Call out the signal-to-noise ratio. Don't look at the ledger. Look at the raw carrier wave."

"Carrier acquired," Chen muttered, his voice taut. "Attenuation... seventy-two percent. It’s too noisy, Lin. The checksum is failing."

"Compensating for wind sheer," Lin whispered. Her breath fogged the brass scale. She eased the manual micrometric screw a fraction of a thread. "Now."

"Forty-eight percent scatter. Packet reception... twenty percent. Thirty percent. It's fragmenting!"

From down on the quay, Uncle Tan’s voice crackled through the comm-box: "Jin! The south caisson valve is jamming! Silt in the gate guides! The dock is settling another degree to the port! If you don't lock that beam now, the tilt will take the transceiver completely off the horizon!"

"Lin!" Jin barked.

"Quiet," she breathed.

She didn't fight the crane's movement. Instead of trying to hold the crosshairs rigid against the storm, she anticipated the crane’s rhythmic, ponderous sway against the surging tide. She dialed the azimuth past the center mark, letting the structural flex of sixty tons of iron swing the beam directly through the receiver’s aperture at the crest of the swell.

A high, clean chime cut through the static on Chen’s handheld terminal.

"Handshake confirmed!" Chen yelled, his voice cracking. "The Atacama node answered! Cryptographic verification received... they’re validating the local sortition tally from yesterday’s assembly!"

On the small screen, illuminated by the pale cyan glow of the optical carrier, the blocks aligned. A bilateral usufruct contract blossomed across the local air-gapped ledger: forty thousand liters of fresh potable water and two drydock maintenance reservations committed to the Pacific carrier *Lautaro*, in exchange for immediate physical transfer of eight hundred battery modules currently held in escrow at the offshore anchorage.

The admiralty lien had been outflanked without a single dollar clearing an offshore account.

"It took," Chen breathed, sinking back against the iron truss, his hands trembling violently. "The swap is registered on the southern mesh."

Lin let go of the aiming yoke. Her fingers were cramped into rigid, claw-like hooks, the skin around her nails raw and bleeding from the knurled brass knobs. She closed her eyes, letting the torrential rain wash the grime and blood from her face.

"Jin," she called down into the voice-pipe, her voice hoarse. "Signal the Mekong grain carriers. Tell them their bunker fuel and port dues are backed by physical lithium parity. Tell them to drop anchor in Berth Seven."

Silence lingered on the pipe for two seconds, followed by Jin’s gravelly laugh. "They’re already coming about, Lin. Their lead ship just fired her mooring thrusters."

Lin exhaled, her spine leaning back against the gantry rail, feeling the deep, rhythmic hum of the tidal current beginning to turn against the submerged caissons.

Then, the low-frequency warning horn on the harbor mouth buoys began to wail.

Chen shot upright, grabbing his binoculars and training them through the rain toward the outer channel.

"What is that?" Chen whispered, his skin going pale.

Lin snapped her eyes open and looked out.

The private tactical cutters hadn't retreated back to the commercial roadstead. They had fanned out into a screening line, and behind them, looming out of the black rain squall like an artificial island, moved a four-hundred-meter corporate heavy-lift vessel. Its deck was not laden with commercial containers, but stacked with eight decommissioned, concrete-filled bulk carrier hulls, swinging out on heavy industrial davits.

"They aren't trying to board us," Jin’s voice came up through the pipe, stripped of all humor, flat and cold with sudden dread. "They're scuttling. They’re dropping a wall of dead iron straight across our channel mouth."
