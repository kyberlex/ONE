The halogen blade sheared through the black leaves overhead, turning the drizzle into a curtain of falling needles.

"Down," Kasim rasped.

He didn't reach for a throttle. An engine rev would give their vector away to the hydrophone before the propeller could bite water. Instead, he shoved his calloused palm against the muddy bank, guiding the skiff’s nose deeper into the tangle of stilt roots. The sharp, vinegary smell of disturbed mangrove peat rose around them, thick enough to choke on.

Behind them, the twin-rotor interceptor surged into the creek mouth, its heavy hull pushing a bow wave that sloshed violently against the mudflats. The wash rocked the small aluminum skiff, lifting it inches, then dropping it into the silt with a sickening suck.

Aisyah worked on her knees in the bilge, her knuckles bloody from the oyster beds, sliding the wire clearing rod into the waterjet intake. Her fingers, burned raw years ago by switchboard flashovers, lacked sensation at the tips; she had to feel the jammed mangrove twigs through the vibration in her wrists. She twisted the steel wire once, twice, until a knot of fibrous root broke free with a wet pop.

"Clear," she whispered, her voice barely carrying past her cracked lips.

The searchlight swept twenty paces to their port side, incinerating the dark. It pinned a cluster of drowning pneumatophores in stark, blinding silver. A voice boomed over a marine hailer—flat, compressed, stripped of humanity by algorithmic amplification: *Vessel in unchartered shallows, cut your drives and prepare to be boarded under Admiralty salvage writ.*

Kasim didn't move. A drop of blood crawled from his temple, tracking through the river mud crusted along his jawline. He reached into his coat and produced an unlit cigarette—stale, bent nearly in half from three days crushed behind his compass case—and placed the dry paper between his lips, chewing on the bitter tobacco without lighting it. It was an old habit from his early days on the night tugs, something to keep his jaw from clenching until his molars cracked.

"They're drawing five feet," Kasim breathed into Lin’s ear. "We draw six inches. Hold the rail."

He dipped a single wooden sculling oar over the stern, feathering the blade so quietly that the dark water parted without a gurgle. He didn't fight the interceptor’s wake; he rode it. As the backwash bounced off the mangrove shelf, it carried them sideways through an archway of drowned roots into the deep dredged drainage flume of Drydock 4.

The searchlight wheeled away, scouring the empty mudbank where they had grounded three minutes before.

Ten minutes later, the aluminum hull bumped against the slime-slick timber fenders of Berth 9. 

The Keppel-Batam yard smelled of wet iron, diesel residue, and the sweet, decaying ozone of the massive battery banks working under load. Rain drummed on the corrugated iron overhangs sixty feet above. Uncle Tan stood waiting on the pontoon, his woolen watch-cap pulled down over ears swollen with gout. He didn't ask questions. He merely caught their bowline, threw a turn around an iron cleat with hands that moved like knotted oak branches, and reached down to pull Lin onto the staging planks.

"Jin Wei is inside the plate shop," Tan said, his voice a low rumble under the drumming downpour. "The mortgage receivers just dropped an automated admiralty lien on the harbor master's beacon. They’re giving the fleet six hours to slip cables or be impounded for scrap."

Lin couldn't stop her knees from knocking together. The cold of the Malacca Strait had soaked through her waterproof shell, settling deep into her marrow. Her migraine throbbed behind her left eyeball like a hot nail being tapped with a ball-peen hammer. But under her arm, wrapped in double-waxed canvas and tucked against her ribs, was the air-gapped slate containing the legal architecture they had spent five weeks drafting across three time zones.

"Assemble the delegates," she said, her teeth clicking against the words. "Not the captains. The delegates."

The plate shop was cavernous, lit only by low-voltage orange floodlights strung between overhead crane rails. The floor was a patchwork of rusted steel plates, worn smooth by eighty years of boilerplate transport. One hundred and one men and women—an odd parity drawn by sortition from the sixty-four cargo trampers, feeder barges, and coastal tugs currently riding out the financial freeze in the roads—stood in a loose circle around an iron layout table. 

There were no speeches. The mariners were tired. Their faces were gray from weeks of instant noodles and stale rainwater, their clothes stiff with grease and dried brine. 

Captain Jin Wei stood by the edge of the layout table, his posture crooked, leaning his weight off his left hip to ease the degenerating discs in his lower spine. He watched Lin approach with eyes rimmed in red. On the table lay an open brass telegraph dial and an unrolled Admiralty chart of the Riau Archipelago, pinned flat with two rusted iron rivets.

Lin dropped the waxed canvas package onto the chart. It hit the wood with a heavy, wet slap.

"The receivers think you’re squatters," Lin began. Her voice was cracked, raw from the salt spray, but in the enclosed steel belly of the plate shop, it carried like a hammer on an anvil. "They think because your owners folded their corporate shells in Monrovia and Majuro, your hulls are dead metal waiting for the cutting torch. They filed salvage claims under Singapore commercial jurisdiction."

A murmur moved through the circle. An older chief engineer from a bulk carrier spat into the drainage grate. "We have three days of flour left, Doctor. The bunker barge refused our credit cards this morning. They shut off the pipeline at the jetty."

"Because they are treating this anchorage as a private debt-recovery pen," Lin countered, pulling the slate from its wrapping. She keyed the manual rocker switch. A cyan schematic flickered to life, projected upward against the rusted flank of a suspended rudder stock. "This isn't a debt dispute anymore. This is a declared Commons of Refuge under the Law of the Sea."

Jin Wei leaned forward, squinting through the sulfurous glow. "The admiralty courts won't accept mutual aid as a defense against a valid maritime mortgage."

"They won't accept an excuse," Lin said. "They have to accept a sovereign humanitarian refuge. Under traditional sea law, an unflagged vessel in distress has an absolute right to seek shelter without seizure until life-safety is secured. We haven't just pooled our food; we’ve pooled the operational sovereignty of eighty-two hulls into an indivisible commons trust. The registry isn't tied to a flag state that can be bought by a hedge fund. It’s tied to the physical coordination ledger of this shipyard."

She brought her scarred palms flat down on the layout table. 

"Every kilowatt coming off the tidal turbines, every liter of fresh water from the reverse-osmosis skid, is logged as an immaterial public benefit under the Geneva sortition protocols. If they send armed private skiffs past the outer breakwater to seize these hulls, they aren't executing a repossession. Under international law, they are boarding an internationally declared humanitarian sanctuary during a systemic supply failure. The moment their private tactical teams cross the tidal sill, their corporate directors become personally liable for maritime piracy under universal jurisdiction."

Silence fell over the plate shop, broken only by the rhythmic *chug-thump* of the bilge pump down in Drydock 4.

A young deckhand, his forearms inked with crude home-needle swallows, shifted his weight. "That’s paper, Doctor. Paper doesn't stop an interceptor skiff with twin outboards and twelve men in Kevlar."

"Paper won't," Jin Wei said quietly, his voice cutting through the damp air. He straightened his back, a grim, hard pride surfacing through his exhaustion. "The steel does. Aisyah, are the high-pressure monitors plumbed to the ballast mains?"

Aisyah stepped forward from the shadows, her dripping coveralls leaving a trail of dark circles on the rusted floor plates. "Two thousand five hundred gallons a minute at ten atmospheres. If they come within fifty meters of the dock gates, we don't shoot them. We swamp their cockpits and wash their electronics into the channel. They won't keep a boat upright in that wash."

"And the sortition assembly?" Lin turned to Uncle Tan. 

The old man picked up the brass lottery drum from the floor, shaking it once. The ivory balls inside rattled with the dry sound of dice on slate. "One hundred and one members present. Quorum verified by physical mark."

"Then we don't ask for permission," Lin said, keying the final authorization on the slate with a thumb that trembled from cold. "We ratify the refuge charter. Seventy-five percent supermajority required. Raise your hands."

The vote wasn't sudden, nor was it unanimous in a burst of theatrical passion. It was slow, heavy, dragged out of men and women who had spent their entire working lives fearing the blacklists of the shipping syndicates. First Jin Wei’s calloused hand went up. Then Aisyah’s, her scarred fingers rigid. Then Tan. One by one, grease-stained arms lifted into the orange work-lights—engineers, cooks, second mates, ordinary seamen—until eighty-two hands stood rigid against the damp cavern of the shop.

Eighty-two out of one hundred and one. 

"Seventy-five percent exceeded," Tan announced, his breath fogging in the chill. "The Keppel-Batam Commons is in effect."

Lin exhaled, her knees finally giving way. She caught the edge of the layout table, her fingernails digging into the rusted timber. 

High above them, mounted on the 65-meter gantry crane tower, the cyan laser transceiver hummed to life. Bypassing the severed undersea cables and the jammed satellite links, the point-to-point beam sliced out across the dark waters of the Malacca Strait, locking onto the receiver tower on the Batam ridge, transmitting the cryptographic seal of the refuge treaty to the public ledger nodes in Geneva and Valparaíso.

Then, the high-voltage floodlights flickered twice and died.

Total, suffocating blackness dropped over the plate shop.

Outside in the harbor, the low, steady thrum of the tidal turbines cut out with a grinding metallic groan, replaced by the sudden, terrifying silence of an intentional grid drop. 

Through the salt-encrusted windows facing the channel, Kasim pointed toward the breakwater. 

A wall of red running lights—not two skiffs, but an entire salvage flotilla backed by an offshore ocean tug—had just crossed the outer buoy, running dark under complete electronic silence, their hydraulic winches already screaming as they dropped heavy steel dragging chains across the harbor entrance.
