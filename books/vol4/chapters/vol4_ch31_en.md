The tender’s gunwale slammed against the timber fenders of Berth 8 with a splintering thud, lifting on a three-meter monsoon swell that coated the lower gantry in yellowish foam. Rain struck the concrete deck like birdshot. Before the mooring warp had even bitten into the iron cleat, a deckhand dropped from the tender’s thwarts, clutching a bundle wrapped in oilcloth against his chest.

Behind him, Douglas scrambled over the rail, his face gray with salt-scum and exhaustion. "She’s not breathing right," Douglas shouted into the squall, his voice cracking against the roar of the surf. "The cabin flooded two hours out on the reef. She swallowed the bilge froth!"

Uncle Tan met them at the threshold of the machine annex, his heavy canvas coat slick and stiffened with grease. He didn't ask who the child belonged to or why an unregistered deckhand had his family stowed beneath the floorboards of an unclassed feeder boat. At seventy-one, Tan’s body moved with the sluggish, deliberate economy of an old diesel engine; his knuckles, thick and deformed by decades of damp heat and calcified joint pain, took the bundle with a firmness that surprised the shivering father.

"Inside," Tan rasped. "Out of the spray."

Aisyah came up the ladder from the drydock trench two rungs at a time, her boots dripping rust-colored slurry. Her palms were taped where the flash-burns had peeled back raw pink flesh three days earlier, the adhesive gummy and foul from seawater. She took one look at the bundle on the workbench and pulled the oilcloth clear.

The child was a girl, barely a month old, her skin the mottled lavender of a bruised plum. Her ribs snapped upward against her paper-thin chest with a violent, hollow click, sinking so deep beneath her sternum with every inhalation that her belly looked hollowed out by a trowel.

"Tracheal tugging," Aisyah said, the words clipped short as she leaned down, her ear an inch from the infant’s mouth. "Rattling deep in the bronchial branch. She’s choking on her own fluid."

"Concentrator," Tan said, already moving toward the back locker where the clinic supplies salvaged from the Jurong basin were kept under double canvas. He didn't look at the child's father, who had sunk to his knees by the scrap bin, his forehead pressed against the wet steel rim, shivering violently in his sodden coveralls. "Bring the four-liter bottle and the infant harness."

Aisyah threw open the wooden locker, hauling out the olive-drab plastic housing of the portable medical concentrator. She slammed the three-prong plug into the wall receptacle above the lathe bench and threw the toggle.

Nothing.

A dead click from the internal solenoid, followed by the dull, dying whine of an inverter cooling fan spinning down to zero.

"Main bus tripped," Aisyah spat, her voice tight. "The gantry crane’s optical sensor flooded during the last gust. The whole dock ring is isolated."

"The turbine feeder?" Tan asked, peeling back the child’s soaked cotton shirt. His blunt, scarred thumb pressed gently into the baby's heel; the white mark took nearly four seconds to fade back to sick purple. In his youth, running night runs on the unlit timber coasters between Pontianak and the Karimata Strait, he had watched three children die of croup in the foul, humid hold because the kerosene stoves had smoked them blind. The memory wasn't a story he told; it was a physical weight in the pit of his stomach, heavy as pig iron.

"Tidal turbine’s dumping directly into the deep bank," Aisyah said, her mind already tracking the copper runs through the wet floorplates. "If I throw the knife switch to force the tap, the voltage spike will fry the concentrator's motherboard in three milliseconds. We need clean, regulated DC, or the molecular sieve won't cycle."

"The tender," Tan said without looking up. He was tilting the infant’s chin back with two fingers, clearing a thick string of mucous and yellow fuel scum from her tongue with the corner of a clean rag. "The tug pack."

Aisyah was already out the door.

She dropped into the open hatch of the hybrid utility tender moored beneath the crane overhang. The air down here was suffocating—thirty-six degrees, thick with the stench of overheated electrolyte, ozone, and wet wood. In the bilge, sloshing six inches deep beneath the aluminum floor plates, was the sealed marine fuel cell they had pulled from a decommissioned fairway buoy two months ago. It was fed by a low-pressure bottle of boil-off hydrogen, its platinum catalyst stack designed to run continuously in salt fog without maintenance.

Her blistered fingers tore at the quick-disconnect terminal lugs. A sharp hiss of escaping pressure bit her forearm as she cracked the gas line regulator, checking the low-side gauge with a flick of her thumb. Two bars. Enough for three hours if the membrane didn't dry out.

She grabbed a pair of four-gauge jumper leads, clamped the teeth onto the output studs of the stack’s isolated buffer, and ran the heavy neoprene cables up the ladder, dragging them through the muck of the quay and through the annex door.

"Tan! Catch!"

The old man caught the lead with his left hand, wincing as the sudden load wrenched his inflamed wrist. Together, they didn't bother looking for the factory plug. Aisyah flipped the concentrator upside down on the timber bench, whipped an electrician’s flathead from her breast pocket, and cracked the plastic bottom case open with three brutal twists.

The interior was a tight maze of aluminum canisters filled with synthetic zeolite crystals and a miniature reciprocating piston pump.

"Bypass the onboard rectifier," Tan muttered, his breath coming in ragged whistles through his nostrils. He held the infant tucked in the crook of his right elbow, his body heat shielding her from the draft whistling through the corrugated iron siding. "Wire directly into the twelve-volt rail behind the smoothing capacitor. If you hit the logic board with negative polarity, the whole stack is scrap."

Aisyah peeled back the red insulation with her teeth, spitting copper strands and salt onto the floor. Her hands were shaking—not with fear, but with the cold tremors of sixty hours without real sleep and the sharp, hot throbbing in her burned palms. She touched the copper lead to the terminal lug behind the capacitor.

A tiny spark snapped. The small rotary compressor inside the machine coughed, shuddered against its rubber isolation mounts, and settled into a rhythmic, pneumatic thrum: *ch-ch-ch-ch-ch-ch.*

"Pressure’s climbing," Aisyah said, her eyes fixed on the analog flow indicator on the unit’s face. The steel float ball hovered, sank, then bobbed up to the two-liter mark.

Tan reached into his pocket and produced a clean length of clear silicone tubing he had cut from the reverse-osmosis test bench. He didn't have a neonatal mask. The adult nasal prongs were twice the size of the child's nostrils.

Taking his pocketknife—its blade ground down to a thin, flexible sliver from forty years of whetstone passes—he slit the dual prongs away entirely, leaving a single smooth silicone tube. He wrapped the tip in a scrap of sterile lint-free gauze, binding it with a loop of copper tie-wire so that only a diffuse, soft plume of oxygen could escape, preventing the high-velocity dry gas from tearing the infant’s fragile nasal tissue.

He brought the makeshift diffuser to within a half-inch of the baby’s face.

"Hold the bubbler," Tan commanded softly. "Keep the water level three fingers deep. If the gas dries out her lungs now, the fluid will turn to glue."

Aisyah held the plastic humidifier jar steady between her bandaged palms, feeling the cool, rhythmic vibration of the bubbles breaking through the distilled water. The sound in the shed narrowed until it was only that: the wet, steady ticking of the bubbler and the ragged, shallow gasps of the child.

For sixty seconds, nothing seemed to change. The child’s eyelids remained swollen shut, her chest heaving in that terrible, concave rhythm that signaled the final stages of muscular exhaustion.

Then, slowly, the blue shadow around her lips began to fray.

The dark, slate-gray hue clinging to her earlobes receded, replaced by the faint, mottled pink of returning capillary flow. The violent heave of her ribs slackened, settling into a longer, deeper rhythm. A single, thin wail broke from her throat—not strong, but clear and wet—before she turned her face into Tan’s greasy wool sleeve, her tiny, waxen fingers curling around the cuff of his button.

The father let out a long, shuddering sound that was half cough and half sob, his shoulders slumping against the scrap bin as if his spine had been pulled out through his collar.

"Keep the gas running," Tan said quietly, his voice dropping into the steady register of a man who had seen the sea give back life just often enough not to trust it. "Her temperature is low. We need dry wool and three hot water bottles against her flanks."

Aisyah didn't move. She kept her hands clamped around the humidifier jar, watching the tiny mist of condensation form and vanish on the infant’s upper lip with every breath. Her thumb, raw and throbbing where the tape had slipped, was pressed against the cold copper of the fuel cell lead. She could feel the faint, microscopic pulse of the current flowing through the wire—silent, steady, paid for by the slow, quiet decay of hydrogen across a sheet of carbon cloth.

Then, from the darkness outside the dock gate, a high, piercing warble tore through the rain.

It wasn't the storm.

Aisyah’s head snapped up. On the harbor wall, sixty meters above the boiling surf, the green line-of-sight laser transceiver on the crane tower flickered three times in rapid, frantic succession—the signal for an air-gap override.

The annex door swung inward on its iron hinges, letting in a curtain of horizontal spray.

Jin Wei stood in the threshold, his yellow oilskins dripping black water onto the sill. His hand was on the brass frame of the door, his knuckle white against the wet metal. Behind him, out beyond the concrete breakwater of Berth 7, three long, low silhouettes were cutting through the outer channel, running without navigation lights, their carbon-fiber hulls throwing twin knives of white spray through the dark.

"Jin?" Tan asked, not moving the child an inch from the warm circle of his chest.

"Apex tactical skiffs," Jin said, his voice level, flat, and hollowed out by wind. "They aren't hailing the harbor master. They've crossed the three-mile traffic separation line, and they have recovery rams mounted on their bows."
