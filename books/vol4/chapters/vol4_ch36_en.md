The rain hit the corrugated roof of Drydock 4 like a volley of lead shot, drumming so hard the iron ribs of the caisson vibrated against Jin Wei’s shoulder blades. Outside the caisson gate, across the churned slate water of the roadstead, three hundred thousand tons of steel were drifting toward the shoals without corporate tugs, their deck lights glowing like yellow teeth through the monsoon squall.

"They're coming in blind," Lin Zhang said. Her thumb traced a fresh bruise along her jawline where a swinging optical cable had clipped her two nights prior. Her radio headset gave off a dry, crackling hiss, fouled by lightning five miles south over the Karimun islands. "Singapore traffic control has wiped their transponder slots. On the commercial plot, those three ships do not exist. They are ghost hulls."

Hauer did not move. His knuckles, pale and dry, remained wedged inside his tailored oilskin coat. "The syndicate has already issued a remote emergency override to the fairway escort fleet," he said, his voice flat, scrubbed of malice, carrying only the terrifying calm of an actuary balancing an unavoidable write-off. "Those container vessels are encumbered assets under English admiralty law. If their crews attempt to breach the Batam shoals to discharge bonded freight into an unregistered commons, our private harbor tugs have standing contractual authority to intercept."

"Intercept?" Chen Rui looked up from the grease-stained structural table, his forearms bound in stiff canvas wraps against the ache of swollen tendons. He pushed his spectacles up his nose with the ball of his wrist. "The fairway is eight cables wide at low water, Hauer. You send four thousand horsepower into the flank of a laden feeder ship in this swell, and you’ll crack her fuel double-bottom. You'll dump three thousand barrels of heavy oil into the mangrove nurseries before midnight."

"Then her master should have respected the lien," Hauer said softly. "The market cannot absorb the precedent of seized tonnage dissolving into mutual aid."

Through the salt-streaked glass of the watchtower, three sharp crimson flares arced over the outer channel.

Apex Marine’s tactical escort cutters were already moving. They were twin-screw, reinforced-steel harbor workhorses, painted dead charcoal, usually hired to fend off sea pirates in the Phillip Channel. Now their bows threw twin walls of white foam as their diesel blocks roared against the rising ebb tide. Behind them lumbered the *Titan Barque*, a ninety-ton bollard-pull salvage tug under corporate charter, its flared ice-strengthened bow surging forward at twelve knots, aiming directly for the steering gear of the *Minerva Star*.

Jin Wei grabbed the copper handle of the assembly horn and pulled. The brass diaphragm bellowed across the flooded basin, a low, guttural shriek that rattled the loose galvanized plates above the machine shop.

"Aisyah!" Jin shouted into the hard-wired copper intercom. "Can the tidal turbines take the load?"

Her voice crackled back, breath ragged, fighting the howl of the salt spray down in the sea-chest chambers. "Submerged cells are at peak torque! The current’s giving us four knots clean! If you want the water monitors running at full head, trip the bypass on Berth 8 right now!"

"Trip it!" Jin roared.

At the edge of the decommissioned drydock pier, two retired municipal fire tugs and four salvaged shipyard utility craft—crewed by dockers in orange flotation vests patched with industrial duct tape—pushed out into the chop. They didn't carry steel guns or boarding pikes. Bolted to their bows were heavy industrial monitors salvaged from decommissioned chemical wharves: brass nozzles with three-inch bores, fed directly by the surge of the tidal battery banks.

A thousand yards out, the *Titan Barque* swung hard to port, her blunt snout carving through the swells. Her wheelhouse windows were barred with steel mesh to repel boarding hooks, her searchlights sweeping across the sea toward the lead wooden sampans that were struggling to lay navigational pilot lights for the incoming container giants.

Through the squall, the voice of the *Titan Barque*’s dispatcher boomed over the VHF channel sixteen, relayed by the harbor’s high-altitude optical repeater: *All unauthorized small craft in Sector Four, clear the fairway. Maintain safe berth or be subject to hydrodynamic fouling. Clear the fairway.*

"She's not slowing," Lin said, her hand clenching the edge of the radar console. Her knuckles were white, her eyes burning from three days without real rest. "The dispatcher in Singapore is running the throttle sequence via satellite uplink. It’s an automated intercept trajectory."

"Look at her deck," Uncle Tan said quietly. He had hobbled up the steep metal stairs from the sortition floor, his swollen knees clicking beneath his stained drill trousers. He was pointing a trembling, grease-blackened finger through the rain. "The wheelhouse isn't empty, Lin. Automation holds the wheel, but the flesh is still standing by the throttles."

On the bridge wings of the *Titan Barque*, three silhouettes in yellow rain gear stood clutching the salt-slick railings. They were not corporate mercenaries or syndicate lawyers. They were Batam mariners—men who had traded shifts at the repair docks, men whose cousins lived in the stilt villages of Pulau Buluh, whose wages were sent home in paper envelopes to buy rice and malaria medication.

Behind them, the massive black bow of the *Minerva Star* plowed through the dark, her deck officers leaning over her high forecastle, hammering a warning on her brass ship’s bell—a frantic, hollow clang that was swallowed by the storm.

"They're going to sheer her rudder," Chen Rui muttered, his fingers knotting into the frayed hem of his canvas jacket. "The *Titan* hits her at that angle, the container ship will ground herself on the western reef. Everything in the holds will break."

Jin Wei seized the radio transceiver from the bench. He didn't use the emergency channel. He didn't cite the international convention on salvage or quote the articles of the sortition charter. He switched the selector to the open local working frequency—the channel used by harbor deckhands to trade spare cylinder liners and warn of drifting palm logs.

"Matthias," Jin Wei spoke, his voice dropping an octave, rasping against the bunker fumes that had scarred his throat thirty years ago. "Matthias bin Kasim. I know you're at the winch console on that tug."

A breath of static answered him.

"You took your seamanship papers on the old *Riau Trader* with my brother," Jin said into the microphone. "Your daughter’s school fees were cleared through the clinic account in Berth 7 three months ago when the syndicate froze your payroll card. If you hit that ship, you’re not breaking a contract, Matthias. You’re ramming eighty thousand tons of poultry and grain meant for the basin, while your own village is boiling salted cassava."

On the radar sweep, the green blip of the *Titan Barque* continued to close the gap: four hundred yards, three hundred yards. Her wash was tossing the civilian pilot sampans like dry husks. In the watchtower, Hauer stepped forward, his eyes fixing on the digital readout of the vessel's telemetry on his personal tablet.

"The override has locked the governor," Hauer whispered, almost to himself, the certainty in his posture trembling for the first time. "The company pays their pensions. They won't forfeit their livelihoods."

"A pension is paper, Mr. Hauer," Uncle Tan said softly at his elbow. "You can't boil paper when the straits close."

Down on the lead utility launch, Aisyah’s crew unleashed the high-pressure monitors. Two massive plumes of sea foam leaped sixty yards through the dark, not striking the tug’s crew, but burying the wheelhouse windows in a thundering, blind cataract of green water. The blinding salt deluge shorted out the tug's forward optical cameras, stripping the remote algorithmic pilot in Singapore of its visual lock.

The tactical guidance light on Hauer’s screen flashed yellow, then dead gray. *Sensor telemetry degraded. Reverting to manual helm.*

For three seconds, nothing moved except the fury of the tide. The *Titan Barque*’s steel bow was fifty paces from the towering flank of the *Minerva Star*. A collision was a mathematical certainty.

Then, through the roar of the rain and the scream of the wind, came a sound like an iron mountain tearing itself open.

*CLANG-CLANG-CLANG.*

It was the manual brake band slipping on a five-ton windlass.

On the forecastle of the *Titan Barque*, two deckhands, their yellow slickers plastered to their backs by the deluge, lunged with heavy sledgehammers. They didn't touch the computer terminals or the encrypted bridge consoles. They struck the brass emergency release dogs on the starboard hawsepipe.

A cascade of rust and sparks shot thirty feet into the night as seven shots of forged-iron anchor chain roared out of the tug's locker.

The anchor struck the seabed twelve fathoms down, biting deep into the hard coral silt. The chain went taut with a hydraulic shriek that echoed off the steel flanks of the drydock. The *Titan Barque*’s bow violently jerked around to face the incoming swell, her stern slewing harmlessly away from the *Minerva Star*’s path, dragging her propellers through churned brown mud.

Behind her, on the second corporate interceptor, the *Apex Prowler*, the running lights suddenly blinked out. A heavy thud reverberated across the water as her crew dropped their port bower anchor directly into the fairway silt, killing their engines in dead silence.

Over the open channel, a single voice broke the squall. It was Matthias, breathing hard, his knuckles audible as he tapped the microphone casing twice.

"Batam control," the deckhand said, his accent thick with the salt of the islands. "Our windlass is fouled. Steering gear unresponsive. We are at anchor in the fairway, maintaining position for the safety of life at sea."

A long pause hung over the water, filled only by the hiss of rain on the dark roadstead.

"Requesting harbor pilot from Berth 7," Matthias added steadily. "Send a line. We are out of fuel."

Hauer stood motionless. The blue glow of his encrypted tablet flickered once, showing sixty-four systemic breach alerts across the Malacca tracking matrix, then the screen went dark as the local tower's laser link dropped beneath the weight of the downpour.

Jin Wei lowered the radio handset. His back throbbed with a dull, grinding fire, but as he stepped out onto the open platform of the watchtower, the smell of the salt spray was clean, cold, and entirely free of engine smoke.

Down in the basin, the massive silhouette of the *Minerva Star* slid past the caisson mouth, her wash rocking the iron gates with a slow, deliberate heave, her bow nudging into the waiting arms of the commons.

Behind Jin Wei, on the sorting floor below, the brass assembly drum began to turn again. But before the first wooden token could fall, a long, low horn blast echoed from the outer deep-water anchorage—not from the container ships, but from the gray line of industrial tankers anchored on the horizon, their deck lights flickering on, one by one, through the dark.
