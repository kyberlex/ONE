The voice-pipe over Berth Nine did not carry words, only the sharp, metallic crack of an iron crowbar striking plate steel, followed by a human scream cut short by a wet thud.

Jin Wei dropped the brass binoculars onto the plotting table. His third lumbar vertebra ground like a dry bearing as he straightened, the dull, familiar ache flaring through his right hip and down into the heel of his boot. Outside the wheelhouse windows, the sky hung the color of wet solder. The monsoon heat was thirty-five degrees, but inside the corrugated iron sheds of the lower berths, under ninety-eight percent humidity, the air sat like boiling soup. 

"Jin, stay on the bridge," Lin’s voice rasped through the acoustic tube from the gantry tower above. Her breath hitched, ragged with exhaustion. "The offshore heavy-lift is flooding its starboard ballast tanks. Chen says we have twenty minutes before they swing the first scuttle-hulk across the channel throat. If you go down there—"

"If Berth Nine goes up in smoke, we don’t have an inner basin to defend," Jin said. 

He didn’t take a sidearm; the covenant forbade lethal iron, and drawing a pistol in an enclosed compartment of desperate men was merely an invitation to die fast. Instead, he reached into his locker and pulled out a clean linen rag, wrapping it methodically around the calloused notch of his left thumb—an old, unconscious habit forged decades ago, when a snapped wire-rope winch on an unflagged coaster had peeled the flesh to the bone. Whenever chaos closed in, his fingers searched for that scar, reminding him that bone healed, but panic was fatal.

He shoved through the watertight door into the rain.

The yard smelled of hot iron, stagnant bilge, and sour human sweat. Down on the apron of Drydock Four, three hundred meters from the administrative offices, eleven private tactical contractors from the Apex security tender were trapped behind a perimeter of rusted cargo skids. Their fiberglass launch sat fifty yards out, dead in the water, its outboard fouled and smoking with plastic drift-netting. 

They were not advancing. They were tearing each other to pieces.

The contractor squad had been posted to hold the commercial fuel riser three days ago on seventy-two-hour corporate rations. Those rations had run out yesterday morning. Enclosed in black tactical overalls, heavy ceramic plate carriers, and ballistic helmets, their bodies had become personal furnaces. Two men were screaming at each other behind an overturned flatbed trailer, their carbines slung low, pointed at the deck between their boots.

"He told us six hours!" one screamed, his voice shredded, spitting white foam from cracked, blackened lips. "Six hours until the crew change! Look at the channel! Look at the crane ship, you idiot! They’re dropping bulkheads across the mouth! They aren't picking us up! They’re locking us in the pen!"

"Stand your post, Keller!" a second voice barked. It was their team lead, a man with a broken nose and the gray, gaunt hollows of advanced heat exhaustion carving out his cheeks. His sidearm remained holstered, but his right hand hovered an inch above the retention clip, trembling with neurological fatigue. "We have a contracted presence on Berth Nine until eighteen hundred hours. Stand your post or I log you for breach."

"Log me?" Keller lunged, slamming the butt of his unslung weapon into the leader’s chest plate. The leader stumbled back against the corroded iron bulkhead of the workshop, his boots slipping in a puddle of brown, stagnant rainwater. "With what ledger? Look at your comm-pad! There’s no carrier signal! The satellite link has been dead since dawn! There is no extraction!"

Four other contractors moved in, forming a ragged, sweating ring. Their eyes were wild, red-rimmed with salt-scour and fever. In the shadow of the workshop eaves, two more men lay on their sides in the dirt, their ballistic vests stripped off, vomiting clear bile onto the gravel. One of them had a bandaged leg wrapped in a filth-soaked pressure dressing, the skin around the edges swollen, purple, and weeping clear fluid.

Jin walked down the concrete ramp. 

He did not hurry. He did not sneak. His heavy steel-toed work boots struck the wet slag with measured, rhythmic clangs. Behind him, Uncle Tan wheeled a hand-dolly loaded with two fifty-liter blue polyethylene drums, condensation beading thickly against their chilled plastic skins. Beside the drums rested a canvas medical satchel.

Keller spun around, weapon snapping up toward Jin’s chest. "Hold! Stay where you are! Back up or I fire!"

Jin stopped five paces away. He kept his hands open, palms down at waist height. The bunker fuel cough caught in his throat, dry and rattling, and he waited until the spasm passed before speaking.

"Put the muzzle down, son," Jin said, his voice level, flat as an oiled sea. "You fire that inside this shed, the ricochet off that corrugated iron will kill the boy behind you before it touches me."

"We are authorized to secure Berth Nine," the squad leader wheezed from against the bulkhead, struggling to remain upright. His uniform was black with sweat across the spine, white crusts of evaporated sodium forming along his collar. "Apex Marine Tactical. Sovereign maritime security under... under Euro-Asian Maritime Finance lien order..."

His knees buckled. He slid three inches down the wet iron wall, catching himself on his knuckles.

"Your lien order doesn't pump fresh water," Jin said. He nodded toward Tan. "Open the valve, Tan."

The old docker didn't look at the rifles. His arthritic fingers worked the brass petcock on the lower drum. A stream of clear, cold water—pulled three hours ago from the reverse-osmosis plant and chilled through the tidal turbine’s intake heat exchanger—gushed into a five-liter galvanized pail. The sound of running water in the stifling, airless heat hit the contractors like a physical blow.

Keller’s jaw slackened. He swallowed, his dry throat clicking loudly.

"You’ve been drinking from the fire main," Jin said, looking past Keller toward the two men on the ground. "That water sits in dead-end black iron pipes under forty degrees of ambient heat. It’s full of rust, anaerobic bacteria, and heavy zinc. Drink another pint of it and your kidneys shut down before sundown."

"It's a trick," Keller whispered, though his rifle barrel dipped three inches toward the gravel. "They’re going to poison us, take our gear."

Jin walked straight past him. He didn't look back to see if Keller’s finger was on the trigger. He knelt beside the contractor lying on the gravel, whose calf was swollen to twice its normal size. Jin opened the satchel, pulling out a sterile bottle of saline and a scalpel wrapper.

Thirty years ago, adrift on an unpowered freighter in the Makassar Strait, Jin had watched three deckhands succumb to heat delirium. They had begun by screaming at the sky; within twelve hours, their skin had turned hot and dry, their minds wandering into childhood memories before their hearts simply arrested under the thickened slurry of their own dehydrated blood. He recognized the smell on this boy’s skin: the faint, sweet scent of systemic ketoacidosis.

"Cut the boot laces," Jin told Tan without looking up. "Tan, pour half a liter into a cup. Add two pinches from the paper packet. Sodium and glucose. Slowly. If he gulps it, he’ll aspirate."

The squad leader dragged himself forward, his boots dragging in the dirt. He dropped to one knee beside Jin. Up close, the man wasn't older than thirty-five, his eyes terrified not of the enemy, but of the realization that his life had been factored into a corporate loss spreadsheet as an acceptable rounding error.

"They aren't coming, are they?" the leader whispered.

"The heavy-lift out there has eight thousand tons of concrete ballast in its holds," Jin said quietly, slicing the rotten bandage away from the boy's leg with steady, economical strokes. A line of foul, yellowish fluid drained into the slag; the boy groaned, his body shuddering under a wave of shivering heat. "They are going to drop those hulls into the dredged gut. If you were on that jetty when the channel closed, you were meant to starve until our sortition council agreed to trade our battery banks for your medical evacuation."

The squad leader closed his eyes. A single tear cut a clean gray line through the grime on his cheek. "They gave us forty-eight hours of rations. Said the tugs would pull us off at high water."

"High water was forty minutes ago," Jin said. He took the steel cup from Tan and pressed it to the wounded boy's lips, supporting the back of his neck with one scarred hand. "Drink. Small sips. That’s it."

Keller stood frozen above them, his rifle hanging limp by his side. Behind him, three other contractors dropped their weapons onto the gravel with hollow, plastic clatters, dropping to their knees around the second water drum. One of them buried his face in his wet hands, his shoulders shaking with silent, dry heaves.

"Take your plates off," Jin told the squad leader. "All of you. Strip the Kevlar. You’re cooking your organs. Tan, get the stretchers from the machine shop. We move them into the turbine tunnel where the ventilation is running."

The squad leader unclipped his tactical vest. It fell into the mud with a heavy, dead slap. He reached down, took the cup from Jin’s hand, and held it to his wounded man's mouth himself.

"My name is Vane," the man rasped.

"Jin Wei," Jin replied. "Now help me lift him."

Before they could stand, a sound ripped through the basin—not an explosion, but the gargantuan, groaning screech of four-inch steel cables snapping under catastrophic tension. 

Jin snapped his head toward the channel mouth.

A mile out, the corporate heavy-lift had cut its forward davits. The first four-hundred-foot concrete-filled bulk hull tilted sideways, sliding down the angled slipway of the carrier's deck. It struck the water with a sound like a collapsing mountain, throwing a thirty-foot wall of white spray into the rain, before settling directly across the deep-water passage, its rusted spine breaking against the harbor bar.

The channel was shut. And from the gantry tower, Lin’s warning horn began a long, unbroken shriek.
