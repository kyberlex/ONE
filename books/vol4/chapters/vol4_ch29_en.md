The outboard engines shrieked like circular saws chewing through sheet zinc.

Jin Wei shoved the radio close to his mouth. "Lin, keep the laser locked on the Johor tower. Chen, lift the boy. Move!"

Rain fell in solid vertical ropes, flattening the greasy chop inside the inner slip. Chen Rui hitched the soaked canvas stretcher under Harun’s shoulders, his forearms corded and pale with strain, tendons snapping against his wrist bones with every heave. Aisyah took the diver’s legs. Harun groaned, teeth clicking against his split lip, but the boy had stopped fighting; he clung to the canvas like a child pulled from a well.

Jin turned his back to the gale, pressing his jaw against the wet concrete pillar of Sluice Three. Decades on commercial bilges had taught his teeth what his ears couldn't hear: three hulls, fiberglass-reinforced resin, twin two-hundred-horsepower outboards running wide open, planing over the submerged baffle nets.

"They're not aiming for the main lock," Jin said, spitting diesel-flavored rainwater onto the dock. "They're coming for Berth Nine. The blind spot beneath the gantry tracks."

"They're through the boom gap!" Lin’s voice broke through the speaker, chopped by static and the thrashing of the gale against the crane cab sixty meters up. "Three interceptors. Black hulls. Captain, they aren't deploying boarding ladders. They're carrying cargo cases."

Aisyah dragged Harun behind the safety of the reinforced pump-house doorway. She wiped salt from her lashes, her knuckles scarred white from an old battery flash that had permanently fused two fingernails. "Cargo? They breached an active lock to deliver freight?"

"Evidence," Jin said. The realization hit him not as a deduction, but as a cold weight in the pit of his stomach. "They need a legal trigger for live ammunition. Vance can't authorize lethal clearing on an unarmed maritime collective without an act of piracy."

Out in the black water, three low wedges of matte composite cleared the breakwater. Their running lights were taped over with dull mastic. They didn't hail. They didn't mount water cannon. The lead craft slammed against the rubber bumper of Berth Nine with a dull *thud*, its hull shuddering under the wash of its reversing props.

Four men vaulting the gunwale. No uniforms—heavy black oilskins, composite helmets, low-slung tactical webbing. They moved with the brisk, rhythmic economy of men working against a digital stopwatch. Two dropped to one knee with short-barreled carbines held low across their chests, covering the crane apron.

The third and fourth men hauled a long, olive-drab transit case onto the slippery concrete.

"Berth Nine maintenance bay," Jin whispered into the mic. "Lin, tell me the public feed is live."

"The optical link is trembling in the wind," Lin's voice came back, tight, her breath rattling with the jagged edge of a rising migraine. "Atmospheric scatter is taking half the cyan beam. But the harbor mesh has seventy-two independent receivers locked onto the tower. If I can hold the beam for twenty seconds, every cargo ship anchored in the roadstead sees the raw stream."

"Do not zoom," Jin commanded, keeping his voice dead flat, stripped of anger. "Wide field. Time stamp on the bottom margin. Let the water speak for itself."

On the apron below, the lead contractor barked a single syllable. His partner set down the transit case, punched the heavy latches, and flipped the lid open to the pelting downpour.

Inside, nestled in custom closed-cell foam, lay six Kalashnikov-pattern assault rifles, their steel receivers greased with heavy cosmoline, alongside two military satchel charges wrapped in commercial Malaysian fishing twine.

The contractor didn't check the chambers. He didn't chamber a round. He reached down, grabbed two rifles by their slings, and turned toward the padlocked auxiliary tool shed built into the base of the gantry tower—a shed registered in the port manifest to the shipyard’s local scrap-metal union.

"They're planting them in the workers' locker," Aisyah hissed, her hand gripping the rusted flange of the intake pipe until the skin over her joints went bloodless. "If the admiralty receivers find those inside the perimeter, UNCLOS Article One Hundred applies. Armed brigandage. They get a naval cordon and shoot-on-sight authorization within an hour."

"Stay down," Jin said. His lower back flared, the worn disc between his fourth and fifth vertebrae grinding with every short breath. He reached into his coat and felt the cold, notched edge of a brass pocket-watch case—not his father’s, but an old chronometer he’d bought off a bankrupt Greek second mate in Surabaya thirty years ago. He thumbed the dented rim twice to ground his racing pulse.

The contractor reached the shed door. He drew a battery-powered hydraulic spreader from his hip scabbard, shoved the jaws into the hasp, and squeezed the trigger. The hardened steel padlock sheared with a sharp, rifle-like *crack* that was swallowed instantly by the drumming rain.

He swung the corrugated sheet-metal door wide and reached back for the transit case.

"Lin," Jin said into the mic. "Flood the apron. Now."

Sixty meters above, Dr. Lin Zhang slammed the manual bypass switch for the main gantry lighting bank.

Three dozen four-thousand-watt industrial high-pressure sodium luminaires—rigged to the emergency battery bank salvaged from the hybrid tugs—roared to life in a blinding amber cascade. The entire length of Berth Nine, from the scum-crusted waterline to the crane tracks, was instantly illuminated in harsh, merciless relief. Every puddle became a mirror; every falling raindrop turned into a streak of hammered gold.

The contractor at the shed froze, his face caught square in the beam. His tactical visor was raised against the monsoon fog; his eyes shrank to pinpricks, blinded by the sudden deluge of lumens.

Directly above him, suspended from the underside of the gantry trolley, the shipyard’s public maritime-safety camera—a heavy, salt-pitted dome camera installed thirty years ago to monitor hull-plate stress during drydock flooding—pivoted smoothly on its brass worm gear.

A bright red indicator lamp on the camera’s aluminum housing blinked twice. Solid.

"Link confirmed," Lin’s voice cracked over the channel, trembling with exhausted triumph. "Cyan laser is penetrating the storm. The raw video is mirroring to the Hague Trust maritime registry, the Batam harbor master, and the automated bridge feeds of forty-eight commercial freighters sitting four miles off the breakwater."

On the dock, the two security men covering the apron swung their weapons toward the light tower, then hesitated. Their training was corporate, contractual, calibrated for shadows and legal ambiguity. In the glare of thirty thousand watts, framed against an open tool locker with automatic weapons held in bare hands, there was no ambiguous engagement.

"Drop the crates!" a voice boomed from the crane’s public hailing horn. It wasn't Jin's voice; it was the flat, recorded baritone of the harbor’s automated safety beacon, repeating the dock’s emergency flood protocol in English, Malay, and Mandarin.

The lead contractor cursed, his radio earpiece suddenly buzzing with furious traffic from the command skiff. He looked at the transit case at his boots, then looked up into the glaring eye of the safety camera.

Across the strait, on the bridge of an eighty-thousand-ton liquefied gas carrier anchored in the westbound lane, an automated navigational printer began chattering, spitting out low-resolution frames of the Batam gantry: two men in tactical gear, an open crate of assault weapons, and the clean, unaltered coordinates of Berth Nine stamped in the cryptographic ledger.

"Abort," the contractor muttered, the syllable caught plainly by the directional audio sensor mounted above the shed. "Package is compromised. Back to the boats. Back to the boats!"

They didn't bother locking the transit case. They kicked the rifles across the grease-filmed concrete, grabbed their empty spreader, and sprinted for the gunwales.

Aisyah leaned forward, her chest heaving against the rain-chilled bulkhead. "Captain, the fire monitors—we can pin them against the seawall before their engines catch."

"No," Jin said. His lungs burned with the cold vapor, his cough coming hard and deep, rattling his ribs. "Let them run. We don't need their bodies. We have their serial numbers."

The three skiffs slammed their throttles forward, churning the dark water of Sluice Three into a violent, frothing froth of diesel exhaust and white foam. Within thirty seconds, their hulls had vanished back into the black sheets of the monsoon, fleeing out toward international water where their transponders could hide among the container traffic.

Jin stepped out onto the wet apron. The rain drummed relentlessly on the corrugated iron of the tool shed, washing away the grease the contractors had left on the floorplates. The transit case lay on its side, the cold foam soaking up seawater.

Behind him, Chen Rui stepped into the light, his work boots squelching in the puddles. Harun was wrapped in gray wool inside the dry pump room, sleeping the heavy, dead sleep of hypothermia recovery.

"They'll claim it was a rogue contractor," Chen said, his voice flat with fatigue. "They'll say the weapons were ours and their security team was interdicting them."

"The ledger has the optical burst from before they hit the bumper," Jin replied, kneeling down beside the spilled case. He didn't touch the cold steel. He simply watched the rain pool in the rifle sights. "Every second is signed by five nodes on two shores. You cannot forge an ocean."

The radio at his shoulder crackled again.

It wasn't Lin this time.

The voice was rough, choked with salt and panic, coming over the short-range VHF emergency frequency from the grounded container vessel three hundred yards out on the mud spit.

"Batam Commons... this is the third mate on the *Nordic Sentry*," the voice gasped, competing with the metallic groaning of tortured steel. "The current... the tide turned four knots early. The hull is splitting at hold four. Our master abandoned ship on the launch... we have four hundred tons of bunker fuel over the lower bulkhead, and the plates are tearing."

Jin stood up slowly, feeling every year of his life in the hinge of his knees, and looked out into the howling dark.
