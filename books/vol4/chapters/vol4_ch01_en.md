The cyan beam hissed through the deluge, boiling rain into a needle-thin tracer of steam that vanished eight nautical miles out into the black throat of the Philip Channel. 

Sixty meters below, the wooden timber boom of Berth 9 did not break; it detonated. Splintered teak and yellow mooring drums shot across the grease-slicked water as the first Apex interceptor punched through the outer slip. Twin high-displacement outboards shrieked in reverse, throwing up a rooster tail of white froth that caught the glare of halogen searchlights. The beam swept across the rusted flank of Graving Dock 3, catching the weeping steel bulkheads and blinding Jin Wei where he stood behind the storm bollards.

Jin didn’t blink. He couldn’t. The salt mist stung his retinas, but the ache in his lower spine was worse—a dull, grinding nerve pinch born of thirty years spent pacing vibrating engine-room deckplates. He spat a mouthful of bile and bunker-vapor phlegm into the scum line at his boots.

“Hold the line!” Jin bellowed against the squall. His voice was ragged, rasping over the old chemical burn scarring his collarbone. “Do not run! If you scatter into the yard, their optics pick you apart!”

Behind him, huddled beneath the overhang of the disused plate-bending shop, four hundred mariners stirred like a herd of cattle scenting an approaching fire. Men and women from six flagged registries, their shore passes revoked forty seconds ago when the Singapore digital clearinghouse snapped shut. Their phones were dark slabs of useless glass; their maritime identity chips flashed red failure codes against the dead regional network. Across the strait, the automated container terminals of Jurong and Pasir Panjang looked like an abandoned necropolis, five hundred gantry towers frozen in mid-stride, container spreaders hanging paralyzed over dark container hulls.

The digital world had suffered a stroke. The physical world was arriving by sea.

Up in the crane gantry cab, Lin Zhang felt the vibration through the rusted soles of her sneakers before she heard the report. Her left eye throbbed behind her temple—the cold, blinding spike of a chronic migraine brought on by thirty-six hours staring at unbuffered routing logic. Below her, the second Apex skiff cleared the broken boom, a carbon-fiber wedge riding low, its crew clad in dull gray flotation armor, holding boat hooks tipped with pneumatic grappling claws.

“Lin!” Aisyah shouted, bracing her boots against the crane’s wet lattice frame. The high-voltage flash burns across the tips of her fingers were slick with grease as she jammed a mechanical lock-pin into the optical gimbal. “Laser signal dropped thirty percent! The rain’s scattering the aperture!”

“Don’t let it drift!” Lin screamed back. Her fingers trembled against the cold aluminum casing of the interface board. She didn’t have a computer terminal; the master controllers were dead. She had only raw signal-bias screws and an analog micro-ammeter salvaged from an auxiliary generator panel. “If the beam slips off the offshore buoy, the handshake dies! Batam won’t see the ledger!”

Through the viewfinder, the light across the strait was a shivering pinpoint. The tropical squall was thick as wool, each drop of rain an optic lens fracturing their lifeline to the independent nodes down south. Lin’s lungs seized—not from the wind, but from the tight, sickening knot of panic that had chased her out of the corporate data corridors. The memory of drone camera clusters descending on her university lab rose in her throat like sour milk. She forced her knuckles flat against the gimbal plate, manually compensating for the sixty-knot gusts rocking the crane.

On the quay, Chen Rui was already at the sea-chest intake. His forearms, wrapped tight in tension bandages to quiet the chronic tendonitis that turned his grip to ash, hauled on the iron handwheel of the auxiliary ballast valve.

“Jin!” Rui yelled, his face slick with greasy rainwater. “The tidal surge is maxing the sump! If I don't vent the turbine housing, the back-pressure will crack the copper windings!”

“Venting wastes the head!” Uncle Tan emerged from the shadow of the winch house, his oilskin poncho flapping like a torn sail. His knees moved with the stiff, hitching gait of advanced rheumatoid arthritis, but his hands—ancient, calloused, scarred by forty years of hauling nylon hawser—were steady as a vice. He shoved a heavy wooden flemish-flake mat under the relief valve stem. “Damp the hammer, Rui! Don’t dump the load, transfer it!”

The first tactical skiff was fifty yards out, cutting through the chop toward the low concrete ledge of the drydock apron. A loudspeaker crackled from its coach roof, dry and synthesized, devoid of human breath: 

*“Berth Nine unauthorized assembly. By order of the Admiralty Receiver and Maritime Commercial Syndicate, you are occupying restricted industrial infrastructure. Disperse into the designated processing pens on Pier Twelve. Non-compliance constitutes piracy under the revised Straits Navigation Accord.”*

“Piracy,” Jin muttered through clenched teeth. “They steal the ships out from under our bunks, starve us on anchor for four months without pay, and call us the thieves.”

He swung his arm down toward the dock apron. “Aisyah! Give them the salt!”

From the mid-deck platform of the gantry, thirty feet above the water line, Aisyah slammed her heel against the counterweight release of the shipyard’s dual-barrel high-pressure monitors. 

There were no rifles on Berth 9. The covenant of the assembly was ironclad: no lead, no cordite, no blood drawn that could not be washed clean by seawater. But kinetic mass did not care about philosophy.

Twin bronze monitors—reclaimed from a decommissioned steam tug and fed directly by the roaring, turbine-pressurized sea sump—kicked violently in their trunnions. With a deafening, percussive *whump*, two solid columns of green harbor water, five inches thick and under fifteen atmospheres of raw hydraulic pressure, punched through the squall.

The jet struck the lead interceptor square across the foredeck.

The sound was like an oak tree snapping in a gale. The carbon-composite windscreen of the skiff imploded into frosted sugar. The two tactical operators standing on the forward casting platform were knocked clean off their feet, thrown violently backward into the cockpit well as thousands of gallons of water swamped the vessel in a matter of seconds. The skiff’s nose dug into the chop, its twin outboards lifting out of the brine with a high-pitched, screaming whine as the props churned thin air before the automatic bilge sensors choked on harbor mud.

The second boat swerved hard to starboard to avoid the deluge, its searchlight beam swinging wild into the clouds. The pilot cut the throttle, struggling to keep the craft upright in the boiling cross-swell kicked up by the monitors.

“Drop the wedge!” Jin shouted.

Chen Rui hauled the tripline on the overhead crane track. A four-ton iron gantry drop-wedge—an obsolete casting used fifty years ago to brake rolling shipyard cranes—slid off the grease-rails and fell seventy feet straight down into the slipway mouth.

The impact shook the concrete quay beneath Jin’s boots, sending a three-story wall of brown foam over the dock edge. The iron wedge settled deep into the harbor silt, its rusted teeth jutting three feet above the water line, sealing the inner basin against any hull drawing more than six feet of water.

The swamped lead skiff, dead in the water and listing ten degrees to port, drifted back out toward the channel on the receding ebb tide. Its crew was already scrambling with manual bailing buckets, their weapons untouched in their tactical racks, their mission drowned under the brutal, unyielding weight of the sea itself.

A ragged, tentative cheer rose from the four hundred stranded mariners behind the plate shop.

“Save your breath!” Jin snapped, turning on them with a glare that cut through the darkness. “They didn’t come to kill us! They came to see if we’d roll over! They know the route now!”

High above, Lin Zhang didn’t join the cheer. Her hands were cramping around the cold brass adjusting screws. Her eyes were burning, tears running down her cheeks to mix with the rain.

“Jin!” she shouted down the gantry ladder, her voice thin against the wind. “The cyan link—it’s holding, but Batam is reporting packet loss! The terminal clearinghouses aren't just frozen—they’re being overwritten! Aegis-Omni isn’t waiting for a court order. They’re running a total sovereign registry purge!”

Jin looked out across the channel. 

The lights of Singapore were not coming back on. In fact, a deeper darkness was spreading. Out past the barrier islands, the running lights of three massive, darkened shapes were turning slowly into the southern fairway. Not tactical skiffs. Not coastal patrol.

Heavy salvage cutters. Admiralty tugs under commercial lien, each one carrying deep-sea cutting gear and heavy diesel generation, coming to strip Berth 9 down to its bare iron ribs before the morning tide could turn.

The first skirmish was over. The physical siege of the strait had just begun.
