The silt churned black in the shallows where the water monitors could not reach.

Chen Rui tasted old iron and salt. The spray driving off the drydock pontoon carried the stench of decayed mangrove peat and churned bunker residue. Thirty feet out in the gray chop, just beneath the surface scum, a twin track of frothing bubbles crawled steadily toward the submerged tidal power riser.

"Umbilical!" Chen screamed into the wind, the sound torn from his mouth before it could travel ten paces. He hooked his elbow through the safety stanchion to keep from skittering across the algae-greased plating. "The crawler is tracking the casing!"

Aisyah binti Rahman was already moving. She did not waste breath shouting back. Her dive booties found purchase on the rusted diamond-tread of the pontoon deck, her center of gravity low, thighs burning from sixteen hours of salt-water diving. There were no small arms on the pontoon; the yard’s defense covenant barred firearms from the berths. What she had was a three-pronged salvage grapnel hooked to a spool of three-quarter-inch galvanized steel cable, its tines pitted and scarred from years of dredging fouled mooring chains out of the fairway.

High above them, sixty-five meters up in the drafty glass perch of the gantry cab, Captain Jin Wei leaned his forehead against the vibrating window. Every shudder of the auxiliary hoist sent a dull, familiar jolt through the lower three vertebrae of his spine—a chronic, grinding ache earned across four decades of standing watch on vibrating engine room deckplates. Through the misted glass, the crawler looked like a flattened iron beetle dragging a thick umbilical of hydraulic line and high-voltage feed through the mud.

Out in the deep gutter of the channel, beyond the thrashing curtain of the fire monitors, the second tactical skiff wallowed with its throttles idling. Vance had stationed his craft right at the drop-off, feeding the tether off a snub-nosed deck drum. His men were not pirates; they were maritime enforcement contractors working off a court-sanctioned admiralty impound order, moving with mechanical, disciplined precision. If that submerged crawler clamped its magnetic jaws onto the yard’s main bus conduit, the cutting charge inside its belly would sever Node 4’s power supply before the ledger could broadcast past the island chain.

"Line’s three yards off the riser collar!" Chen bellowed up toward the cab, his voice cracked from bunker smoke and raw salt air.

Jin’s calloused thumb hovered over the master hoist lever. He did not pull it. Dropping the crane’s salvage block into the shallows blindly would risk crushing their own tidal feed line. "Hold your ground, Chen!" Jin’s voice rattled down through the brass acoustic horn mounted to the pontoon frame. "Wait for the turn!"

At the very summit of the gantry tower, exposed to the full fury of the squall, Dr. Lin Zhang felt neither the height nor the rain. A sharp, rhythmic migraine pounded behind her right eye—the familiar, sickening pressure brought on by forty-eight hours of stare-down with terminal screens and cold coffee. Her fingers, stripped of her oilcloth mittens so she could work the thumbwheels, were numb, white at the nails, and slick with freezing rainwater.

The optical transceiver assembly was shuddering inside its gimbal mount. Clamped to the handrail beside her, the small field diagnostic unit showed an optical carrier attenuation jumping erratically: sixty-four percent, seventy-two percent, eighty-one percent. The monsoon downpour was acting like an endless sheet of frosted glass, each suspended droplet fracturing the cyan laser into useless halos of scattered light.

She did not seek solace in memory. She did not whisper a prayer. Her competence had not been handed down by ancestors; it had been hammered into her through three grueling post-doctoral winters in the unheated basement laboratories of the Shanghai Marine Institute. She knew the physical perversity of salt-pitted brass. She knew that on a salvage mount exposed to tropical humidity, the vernier lead screw suffered four full degrees of dead slack on the clockwise throw before the worm gear finally bit into the elevation teeth.

*Take up the lash,* she told herself, teeth chattering so hard they ached in her jaw. *Feel the thread.*

She cradled the knurled collar with her palm, bracing her wrist against the transceiver frame. Her thumb dialed the micrometer drum counter-clockwise to clear the salt crust, then rolled it back three millimeters until she felt the hard, mechanical bite of metal against metal.

A tiny green diode on the housing flickered. Attenuation dropped to fifty-one percent. The carrier locked.

"Lin!" Jin’s voice boomed through the acoustic line from the cab below. "The bottom crawler is five feet from the flange! I cannot hold the drop clear of our own conduit!"

Lin did not answer. Dialogue under tension was wasted lung capacity. She leaned into the eyepiece of the optical sight, her cheek resting against the cold, anodized aluminum tube. Through the crosshairs, five miles out across the churning, black-and-silver expanse of the Singapore Strait, the silhouette of the sister transceiver atop the decommissioned grain silo at Batam was an indistinguishable smudge behind the sheets of rain. Beyond it, tracking silently across the low horizon, was an open-access environmental monitoring satellite, its polar orbit passing directly through their narrow transmission window.

They had ninety seconds before the orbital arc dipped below the line of the southern islands.

Down on the pontoon, Aisyah swung the heavy salvage grapnel twice, using the momentum of her shoulders, her boots planted wide on the slick steel. Chen Rui threw his full weight against the friction brake of the manual winch, his forearm tendons bulging against his skin with a vicious, fiery cramp.

"Drop it!" Chen yelled.

Aisyah released the sling. The three-pronged iron head flew ten feet outward, punching through the muddy froth of the shallows. It sank instantly into the mangrove silt.

"Reel!" she gasped, dropping to her knees on the pontoon deck, grabbing the winch handle beside Chen.

Both of them leaned in, their chests heaving in ragged, simultaneous bursts. The bronze pawls clattered furiously—*clack-clack-clack-clack*—as the drum took up the slack. The steel cable came out of the surf dripping gray slime, slicing through the brown foam until the tines snagged something hard and unyielding.

The winch violently kicked back. The steel handle struck Chen across the wrist with a hollow crack that drove the air from his lungs, but he dropped his hip against the spoke, locking it with his ribs.

Out in the mud, thirty feet off the pontoon, the water erupted. The crawler’s rubber-jacketed control umbilical pulled taut as an iron rod, hauled sideways out of the mud by the grapnel's tines. The crawler slewed violently ninety degrees away from the power conduit, its magnetic treads spinning uselessly against the churned silt.

Out in the channel, the winch on the stern of Vance’s skiff screamed as the sudden dead-load stalled its electric motor. The tactical craft’s stern was yanked three feet down into the following swell, seawater sloshing across its low transom.

"Umbilical fouled!" Chen shouted through clenched teeth, his face pressed against the cold winch frame. "Jin! Take the line!"

In the cab, Jin Wei shoved the main hoist lever all the way to the forward stop.

The massive, five-ton cast-iron counterweight block dropped from the gantry’s auxiliary hook. It fell sixty feet through the howling monsoon, striking the mudflat precisely where the taut tether broke the surface. The impact was not an explosion; it was an earth-shaking, hydraulic thud that sent a geyser of black mangrove mud and severed hydraulic fluid thirty feet into the rain.

The severed umbilical whipped through the air like a broken guitar string, splashing dead into the shallows.

Out in the channel, the deck winch on Vance’s skiff sheared its mount with a metallic groan. Vance leaned over his coaming, water pouring from the rim of his helmet, watching the dead telemetry screen on his tactical console go completely flat. He did not curse. His shoulders dropped half an inch. He was a professional; the material reality of a broken umbilical and a crushed drone was an absolute boundary. He keyed his radio, his voice flat and steady: "Recovery failed. Stand off to five hundred yards. Maintain observation."

Aloft on the catwalk, Lin Zhang did not see the crawler die.

Her entire universe had narrowed to the three-inch dial of the optical receiver and the tiny vernier screw under her bleeding thumb. The cyan laser cut through the thinning fringe of the squall, striking the mirror array of the Batam silo and refracting upward into the low-altitude sky.

On her field terminal, the transmission progress bar flashed from amber to blinding, solid green.

*Packet stream verified.*

The debt ledger—hundreds of thousands of encrypted usufruct titles, mutual-credit settlements, and maritime mortgage erasures recorded from two dozen port councils across the archipelago—surged into the sky at the speed of light. The multi-hop relay grabbed the passing environmental orbiter, pinging the burst across seven decentralized ground stations from Yokohama to Rotterdam in less than two-fifths of a second.

On Vance’s bridge console, the automated admiralty arrest alerts suddenly vanished, replaced by standard, non-encumbered transit clearings verified by the regional commons node.

Lin stepped back from the transceiver, her chest hitching as the cold air finally caught in her throat. She pulled her trembling hands into the sleeves of her grease-slicked jacket, her vision swimming with blue afterimages.

Then, from the deep channel beyond the breakwater, a sound rolled across the anchorage that froze the marrow in Jin Wei's spine.

It was not the high whine of a mercenary outboard.

It was the deep, thunderous bellow of an automated air horn—five short, continuous blasts signaling immediate, unavoidable danger.

Emerging from the blinding squall at fourteen knots, completely blacked out, its satellite-steered collision-avoidance system frozen by the emergency network reset, a three-hundred-meter automated container ship was plowing dead toward the mouth of the decommissioned drydock, its towering bow wave already surging toward the fragile pontoons.
