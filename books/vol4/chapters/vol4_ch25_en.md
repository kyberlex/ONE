The rain hammered the reinforced glass of the gantry cab in rhythmic, deafening sheets, but the steady cyan line held. Out across the churning chop of the strait, the relay of nine merchant hulls remained pinned in place, an ad-hoc breakwater of rusting iron and stubborn mass shielding the subsea trench. 

Then, five miles north, the mainland died.

It did not dim. It was not the gradual brownout of a strained transformer bank. In a single, absolute heartbeat, the glittering curtain of high-rise towers, automated container cranes, and coastal refineries along the littoral rim snapped into total blackness. 

Inside the gantry cab, the sudden void was so vast it felt like a physical pressure drop. The horizon simply ceased to exist, swallowed whole by the tropical squall.

Jin Wei pressed two fingers against the hollow of his collarbone, where the old chemical scar throbbed against the humid heat. He shifted a dried clove from one side of his jaw to the other—a habit picked up forty years ago shoveling raw bituminous coal in the unventilated holds of river lighters, the sharp, bitter oil the only thing that kept the stomach from turning inside out when the bilge water rose. 

"They dropped the mainland feeders," Lin said. Her voice was flat, raspy with exhaustion. The blue terminal glow reflected off the dark circles beneath her eyes, where days of broken sleep had etched deep, gray bruises into the skin. "Not a system fault. They tripped the high-voltage interties at the regional switching station. Deliberate sector isolation."

"They're suffocating the shore," Uncle Tan murmured from the steel hatchway. He stood with both hands resting on the handrail, rubbing camphor oil into the swollen knuckles of his right hand with his thumb. The sharp scent of wintergreen and menthol cut through the cab’s heavy stink of damp wool and ozone. "They couldn't cut our optical trunk, so they are starving the villages to make an example."

Across the mudflats and mangrove estuaries flanking the naval channel lay thirty stilt *kampungs*—communities of drift-net fishermen, crab farmers, and repair dockers who lived on the margins of the shipping lanes. The corporate grid had always treated them as an afterthought, charging triple the commercial tariff through prepaid smart meters. Now, with the regional circuit open, their small world had plunged into dark water.

"The nursery aerators," Aisyah said, stepping into the cab. She peeled off her neoprene diving hood, her dark hair plastered to her skull with brine and sweat. Her thumb grazed the white, calloused ridge on her index finger—an old arc-flash burn from a shore-power junction box that had never healed smooth. "Tanjung Uma has twelve hundred tons of tiger grouper fry in the nursery basins. Without oxygenation pumps, the fingerlings drown in the silt within forty minutes."

"And the clinic at Teluk Sengat," Rui added without looking up from the structural stress monitor. His forearms were wrapped in tight elastic braces to ease the tendonitis flared by forty-eight hours at the manual helm. "Their cold-chain refrigerator runs on the coastal spur. If the insulin spoils, three hundred people are stranded until the monsoon breaks."

Jin looked out into the rain. The consortium’s calculation was bone-chillingly simple and utterly rational from a ledger sheet: by severing the entire coastal periphery, they created a humanitarian crisis on the node's doorstep. Either the independent dockers would abandon their sovereign mesh to salvage their families' livelihoods, or the fishing villages would turn against the shipyard in sheer, desperate panic.

"We have the tidal surge," Jin said. His voice was low, gravelly from bunker vapor. "What is our storage state?"

"The tug battery banks are sitting near full capacity," Rui said, tapping a mechanical indicator dial on the bulkhead. The brass needle trembled just below the red line. "The twin turbines in the deep channel are biting hard into the four-knot ebb. But our step-up transformers weren't built to backfeed the entire southern district. If we tie into the regional trunk, we bleed our own operational margin. If the private security skiffs return, we won't have the current to power the seawater monitors or swing the container drop-blocks."

"We call the panel," Uncle Tan said softly. "We don't make this choice in the dark."

The neighborhood sortition council assembled twelve minutes later on the covered deck of Berth 8, huddled beneath the overhang of an overturned barge to escape the driving rain. Fifteen souls, chosen by the brass lottery drum at dawn: five deckhands, four net-menders, three shore mechanics, two night-shift cooks, and an unlicensed diesel fitter from the reclamation fleet. 

There was no rostrum. No microphone. Only the deafening roar of rainwater pouring off the barge's corrugated iron roof into the dock basin.

Aisyah laid the line schematic flat on a wooden cable drum, weighing down the corners with rusted coupling nuts. "The consortium's automated substation on the jetty is locked down from the mainland. We can't access their distribution controls. But the old rural spur—the heavy copper line that supplied the decommissioned cold-storage plant twenty years ago—still ties directly into our transformer bank."

"It's corroded," the diesel fitter said, wiping rain from his chin with a calloused forearm. "The insulation on those mangrove poles has taken twenty monsoons of salt spray. We run full current down that line, we lose a fifth of it to heat and leakage before it even reaches the first village."

"Then we push through the leakage," Uncle Tan said. He looked at the fifteen faces around the drum. "If we hoard the battery banks to keep our searchlights bright, we are just another warlord's bunker sitting in the swamp. If the fish die tonight, the villages starve next month. Then what are we defending?"

The vote took less than two minutes. No speeches. No rhetoric. Fifteen hands lifted into the humid, salty air. Unanimous.

"Aisyah, Rui—take the breaker crew," Jin ordered, his voice cutting through the drumming downpour. "Manual engagement. Keep the fire hoses pressurized on the cooling jackets. If the transformer core begins to whistle, you trip it manually. We don't burn the yard to warm the coast."

They moved with the grim, hurried efficiency of people who had spent their lives around dangerous machinery. On the lower machinery deck beneath Drydock 4, the air was thick with the heat of the massive marine battery modules. Twelve tons of lithium-iron cells, salvaged from the guts of dead hybrid tugs, rested in welded steel cradles, their thick, copper busbars sweating condensation in the swampy humidity.

Aisyah stood before the primary intertie cabinet, an insulated fiberglass rod gripped tightly in her calloused hands. Beside her, Rui checked the manual override levers on the oil-cooled step-up transformer. 

"The line impedance is going to kick back like a mule," Rui warned, his hand poised over the mechanical trip latch. "The moment we bridge into the rural spur, the magnetic drag on the tidal turbines is going to double."

"Let it bite," Aisyah said through gritted teeth. 

She slammed the heavy fiberglass hook into the overhead disconnect switch and pulled down with her entire body weight.

A crack like a dry timber snapping echoed through the vault. A blinding violet arc spat across the contacts, illuminating the wet concrete walls in stark, violent relief. The smell of scorched air and transformer oil washed over them in a suffocating wave.

Deep beneath the drydock floor, the subsea cable began to hum. It was not a mechanical rattle, but a deep, vibrating resonance that rose through the soles of their boots—a sound felt in the teeth and the marrow of the bone. Out in the black depths of the channel, the massive twin tidal turbines met the staggering electrical load of thirty coastal settlements. The steel mooring chains groaned, tightening like violin strings against the four-knot current as the water fought to spin the loaded blades.

In the gantry cab high above, Jin held his breath.

For five agonizing seconds, the coastline remained an unbroken void of ink and rain.

Then, across the mangrove flats of Teluk Sengat, two miles out, a cluster of yellow sodium lamps flickered, died, and flared back to brilliant, steady life. 

A cheer, thin and distant against the roar of the squall, carried across the water.

Half a mile further south, along the stilt platforms of Kampung Pasir Putih, another knot of lights broke through the darkness. Then another. And another. Like a string of pearls catching fire in the storm, the thirty villages ignited along the littoral fringe. Out in the shallow estuaries, the water erupted into white spray as thousands of paddlewheel aerators ground into motion, churning life-giving oxygen into the suffocating nursery ponds. 

The cold-chain chillers at the clinic hummed. The harbor beacons along the fishing slips turned green.

Jin leaned his forehead against the damp glass of the cab, the dried clove resting bitter against his tongue, his chest loosening for the first time in eighteen hours.

Then, the collision-warning alarm on the gantry console let out a single, piercing shriek.

Lin's hands flew across the optical terminal. "Jin," she said, her voice dropping into a razor-thin whisper. "Look at the channel mouth."

Jin raised his binoculars into the rain.

Three black-hulled tactical interceptors, running entirely dark with their automated identification systems killed and their radar profiles minimized beneath wave-skimming hydrofoils, had just rounded the southern spit. They weren't heading for the merchant blockade.

They were running at thirty-five knots straight toward the unshielded shore riser where Node 4’s rural power spur entered the mangrove mudflats.
