The hull of the hydrofoil groaned as Aisyah brought it alongside Berth 8. The Keppel-Batam drydock smelled of wet rust, anaerobic silt, and the bitter sulfur of dying lead-acid backup banks. High overhead, the sixty-meter crane stood against the bruised purple of the pre-dawn squall, its cyan optical transceiver blinking in rhythm like a tired pulse.

Jin dragged himself over the gunwale, his lower back flaring with a white-hot spike that made him catch his breath against the slick iron ladder. Decades of standing watch over thrumming diesel bedplates had ground the cushions between his vertebrae to gristle. Behind him, Lin clutched the waterproof courier satchel against her ribs, her knuckles gray. She kept her jaw locked to stop the trembling; whenever adrenaline receded, the damp heat of the strait made her teeth chatter as though she were standing in freezing spray. She had spent three hours chewing dry cloves from a crushed packet in her pocket, a nervous habit carried over from graduate school to keep the bile down when the screen-flicker triggered her migraines.

Uncle Tan stood at the head of the slipway, flanked by forty mariners, grease-monkeys, and container lashers. The rain had paused, leaving the air so thick with vapor that every breath felt half-drowned. A single low-draw sodium work lamp hung from an exposed conduit, casting long, jaundiced shadows across the pitted concrete.

"The optical feed from Johor dropped five minutes ago," Tan said without preamble. His voice was a rasp, worn thin by six decades of coastal smoke and salt damp that had swollen his knuckles until they resembled knotted driftwood. "The harbor master at Jurong has declared an emergency navigation cordon. Apex security launches are already laying acoustic hydrophones along the fairway to catch electric propeller whine. We have maybe thirty minutes before they drop the steel anti-debris nets across the eastern passage."

"And Geneva?" Lin asked. Her voice was flat, parched.

"The cantonal police have locked the gates," Chen Rui said, stepping out from the shadow of a rusted gantry strut. His forearms were bound in blue athletic tape to brace his inflamed tendons, his work shirt darkened with sweat. "They cut the thermal district pipes to the delegate wing at midnight. The Alpine delegates are sleeping in their wool survival coats. The clearinghouse wants the assembly dead of exposure before the first gavel drops."

Jin wiped grease and rain from his brow. "Then we don’t wait for daylight. Where is the drum?"

Under the Covenant, there were no appointments. No veteran master could declare himself ambassador; no engineer could claim the right by virtue of intellectual sweat. Power was an unexploded mine; to let any hand reach for it willingly was to invite the old rot back into the bilges.

Tan turned and led them toward the center of Berth 8, where a three-legged wooden surveyor’s tripod supported a battered brass cylinder. The cylinder had once been the mechanical case of a triple-expansion engine telegraph, salvaged from an ore carrier scrapped twenty years ago in Chittagong. Its polished face had been drilled out, replaced with a hinged wire-mesh hatch secured by a spring-loaded cotter pin.

Inside lay one hundred and one turned hardwood tokens, each the size of a nutmeg, smoothed by the calloused palms of dockers from Surabaya to Belawan. One hundred and one registered mariners of the newly ratified strait commons.

"We need two to take the seat," Tan said softly, his gnarled hand resting on the crank handle. "One deck, one technical. That was the rule agreed upon under the crane yesterday. Two to speak for the southern water, or no one goes."

Jin looked at the drum. In his youth, before the shipyards had automated the slips, his mother had mended hemp trawling nets on the muddy beaches of Hainan, always leaving a deliberate irregularity in the knotting so the sea gods wouldn’t think the net was an act of human arrogance. Jin felt that same ancient dread now, prickling along the skin of his neck where an old chemical burn from an engine backfire had left a patch of hairless, white scar tissue.

"Spin it," Jin said.

Tan took the iron handle. The gears inside the telegraph casing clicked with a hollow, rhythmic rattle that echoed off the high corrugated walls of the drydock. The wooden tokens tumbled within the cage, clacking against the perforated brass like dry bones.

Nobody spoke. Around the perimeter, five men holding brass fire nozzles hooked to high-pressure saltwater mains kept their eyes pinned on the black water beyond the sill. If the Apex patrol skiffs came within range, their only defense was the sheer weight of sea-water ballast, enough to swamp an interceptor’s cockpit without firing a single lead slug.

Tan stopped the handle. He did not look into the drum. He reached in with two fingers, fished blindly into the mass of polished wood, and drew out a token.

He held it under the dull sodium lamp. Carved into the grain was an anchor crossed with a monkey’s fist knot—the seal of the deck watch.

"Number forty-four," Tan read.

Aisyah stepped forward, unfurling the grease-stained ledger from the drydock bench. Her thumb, scarred across the cuticle from a high-voltage battery flash, tracked down the ink-drawn column. She stopped, her eyes lifting to meet Jin’s.

"Jin Wei," she said, her voice dropping an octave. "Master mariner. Unattached."

Jin closed his eyes. A sudden, deep weariness settled onto his shoulders, heavier than the hydraulic winches he had spent his life rigging. He had wanted nothing more than to take a bunk in the abandoned machine shop, sleep for twenty-four hours, and let the younger engineers patch the turbine seals. Geneva was six thousand miles of frozen rock, concrete corridors, and men who fought with legal liens instead of tow lines. He hated cold air; it made his cracked collarbone ache until his teeth throbbed.

"The drum doesn't bargain," Jin muttered, exhaling a long breath that smelled of stale tobacco and bile. "Draw the engine seat."

Tan turned the crank again. Three full revolutions. The brass gears whined on dry bearings.

The old man reached his hand into the mesh, his fingers searching among the wood. He brought out the second sphere. It was marked with three concentric rings—the sign of the electrical and transmission tenders.

"Number seventy-one," Tan said.

Aisyah ran her finger down the second ledger page. The silence in the drydock grew thick enough to hear the rhythmic drip of condensation falling sixty feet from the gantry beams into the drainage sump.

She didn't look up. "Lin Zhang. Signals and systems architecture."

Lin flinched as though struck across the mouth. She took an involuntary step back, her heel catching against an iron cable ring bolted to the floor. "No. I'm a compiler, Uncle. I run the air-gapped packet switches. I don't... I can't sit in a room with five hundred lawyers. If they put a biometric camera on me, the clearinghouse flag—"

"The Covenant drew your number, daughter," Tan said. His voice was not unkind, but it was as unyielding as the granite blocks lining the drydock floor. "The assembly is not an embassy of the clever. It is a jury of the exhausted. If we send diplomats, they will buy them with port concessions before the third reading. If we send you, they will have to explain to a woman who has worked thirty-six hours without sleep why her people should freeze in the dark."

Lin looked down at her hands. The nails were bitten raw, her skin sallow from weeks beneath flickering low-voltage diodes. She looked at Jin, whose face had settled into the grim, vacant mask of a captain preparing to ride out a typhoon with a fouled propeller.

"We have to cross the Arabian Sea before the monsoons turn," Jin said simply, looking at her. "The electric glider doesn't have cabin heat. Pack your wool."

Before Lin could speak, the warning horn atop the gantry tower gave two sharp, truncated blasts.

Chen Rui dropped his wrench into a bucket. "Apex launch entering the channel. They’re running blacked out, three hundred yards off the beacon. They aren't boarding yet—they’re dropping bottom-moored acoustic gates to bottle the fairway."

"The glider is charged to seventy percent," Aisyah said, already swinging down into the maintenance well where the twin-hulled composite craft rested on its pneumatic transport rollers. "We don't get eighty. We take the tidal outflow now, or we sit in this mud until the receivers come with the admiralty marshals at daybreak."

Tan reached into his canvas smock and pulled out a small, heavy object wrapped in oilcloth. He pressed it into Jin’s thick palm.

It was not an amulet or an heirloom. It was an ordinary three-inch stainless-steel pipe coupling, its threads freshly cut, greasy and cold to the touch.

"If the Swiss heating is cut," the old man said, his knuckles grazing Jin's coat, "tell the people in the hall that the water only flows when the pipe is clear of middle-men. Show them the iron. Remind them what holds a ship off the rocks."

Jin slipped the fitting into his mackinaw pocket. The weight pulled reassuringly against his hip. "Lin. The satchel."

Lin pulled the strap tighter across her chest, securing the air-gapped drive containing the Pacific signatures against her sternum. Her panic hadn't vanished, but the drum had spoken, and in the strange logic of the commons, the burden felt lighter now that it was no longer a choice.

They scrambled down the ladder into the narrow cockpit of the sea-glider. The craft was stripped bare—no bunks, no galley, only two carbon-fiber bucket seats bolted directly over the battery housings and an aluminum control yoke.

Aisyah slammed the canopy shut from the outside. Her face, framed by the spray-pitted polycarbonate, was serious and pale. She tapped the glass twice with her knuckles, then yanked the manual release on the drydock floodgate.

A wall of black, churned sea water rushed under the hull, lifting the glider from its cradles with a violent, shuddering heave. Jin’s fingers found the propulsion toggle. The electric drives spooled up with an ear-splitting, sub-harmonic whine, vibrating straight through his tailbone.

Through the forward glass, the dark mouth of the slipway opened onto the strait. Across the channel, two sweeping halogen searchlights from the tactical patrol boats crisscrossed the black swells, searching for the wake of a vessel that was already dropping from the cranes into the dark.
