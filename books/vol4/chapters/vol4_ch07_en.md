The room seemed to lose its air. 

Jin did not speak. His left hand rose slowly to his collarbone, fingers digging through his sweat-stiffened shirt into the rigid knot of his burn scar. The diesel bulb overhead swayed in the draft off the slipway, throwing his shadow in jagged lunges across the blueprint-strewn table.

"Say that again," Rui murmured. His voice had gone thin, the mechanical pencil snapping between his calloused fingers.

"The master key issued when the port authority liquidated your command of the *Bintang Laut*," Lin said, her breath catching as she angled the lens closer to the thermal paper. "They never retired the certificate. When you surrendered your physical token at the receiver's desk in Batam three years ago, they logged it as decommissioned. But in the root directory, the signature remained valid. Every algorithmic foreclosure across the strait over the past eighteen days has been stamped with your digital hand, Jin."

Before Jin could draw breath, a flat, metallic thrum vibrated up through the zinc floorboards. It was not the gentle pulse of the tidal turbines churning in the deep channel. It was a rhythmic, cavitation-heavy chop—twin inboard diesels running muffled without running lights, cutting through the outer breakwater at speed.

Aisyah kicked the door open. Her sleeveless coveralls were slick with rain and black grease, her dive hood pushed back onto her shoulders.

"Skiffs," she said. Two words, snapped like cold iron. "Three of them. Passing the northern shoals. No transponders."

Jin dropped the magnifying glass. The shock of the forged signature vanished into the cold, mechanical calculus of survival. "Berth Nine?"

"Heading straight for the crane tracks," Aisyah said, already swinging back into the gloom of the exterior gantry. "They want the battery packs. If they drown our sub-station, the gantry laser drops dead."

"Tan!" Jin roared into the stairwell. 

The old bosun was already moving among the braziers below, his voice low and firm, herding the sixty-odd refugee sailors away from the open wharf and into the deep concrete belly of the machine shop. There was no panic, only the swift, shuffling urgency of men who had spent their lives listening for the sound of breached bulkheads.

"Rui, the valve deck!" Jin shouted, taking the stairs three at a time. The degenerative ache in his spine flared with each impact, a familiar white-hot spike that he ground down beneath his teeth. "Lin, kill every exterior floodlight! Go dark on the water!"

The shipyard plunged into blackness. 

Through the driving tropical rain, the strait was an oily expanse of shifting shadows. Jin hit the wet gravel of Berth 9, boots sliding on the algae-filmed concrete. The heat index sat suffocatingly high even at midnight, the air thick as steam, smelling of salt mud and burning electrical insulation.

Ahead, cutting through the chop with predatory stillness, three rigid-hulled inflatable interceptors crested the wake of the channel. Their low-slung, matte-black composite hulls sat deep in the water, carrying six men each in tactical flotation armor. Jin caught the dull glint of carbon-fiber boarding ladders slung across their thwarts and pneumatic grappling guns resting on their bows. No blue flags. No sovereign coast guard insignia. Apex Marine Tactical.

They were not here to serve paperwork. They were here to disable the node’s independent power before dawn.

"Aisyah! Are the intake lines clear?" Jin barked, reaching the pump manifold beneath the crane's forward pylon.

"Silt filter’s half-choked from the tide change!" her voice crackled over the hardwired speaker near the valve cluster. "I’ve got the bypass open, but the salt crust is jamming the secondary gate!"

"Take the sledge to the bypass ring!" Rui grunted, throwing his full weight against a three-foot bronze spanner on the sea-cock valve. The bilateral strain in his forearms made his sinews tremble violently, his skin slick with sweat and brine. "Move! They’re inside two hundred yards!"

The lead interceptor throttled up. The low rumble became an aggressive, throaty snarl as its twin props bit into the shallow basin. A narrow, high-intensity spotlight punched out from its console, spearing across the rusted hull of the slipway, hunting for the main electrical umbilical connecting the tidal turbine bank to the shore manifold.

The beam struck Jin full in the chest. 

*Crack.* 

A pneumatic grapple arched upward through the squall, its four-pronged titanium hook clattering violently over the lower catwalk railing of Berth 9. The heavy Kevlar climbing line snapped taut against the pier edge as the lead skiff reversed thrusters to hold station against the stone quay.

"Rui! Turn it!" Jin yelled.

With a ragged cry, Rui slammed the bronze wheel past its corroded stop. Inside the cast-iron housing beneath their feet, four hundred gallons of pressurized seawater hit the line with a shudder that jarred Jin’s teeth. 

Jin lunged for the dual-grip tiller of the primary salvage monitor mounted to the dock edge—a heavy brass cannon salvaged from an ocean-going firefighting tug, its swivel joint stiff with aged tallow. He dropped his shoulder into the counterweight, dragging the nozzle downward until the sights lined with the black rubber sponson of the lead boat.

"Brace!" Jin bellowed.

He threw the quarter-turn lever.

The sea roared back through the pipe. A solid column of green ocean water, six inches thick and pressurized to bursting, erupted from the nozzle. The recoil slammed into Jin’s chest like an iron slab, driving the heels of his boots back two inches into the rain-slick planks.

The jet struck the water three yards short of the lead interceptor, throwing up an blinding wall of spray that completely swallowed the boat’s spotlight. Jin adjusted his footing, driving his hips forward, and elevated the iron barrel.

The high-pressure torrent slammed directly into the skiff’s bow. 

Under the crushing kinetic impact of twelve tons of moving water per minute, the light composite craft didn't just swerve—its bow was forced violently down into the chop. The boarding ladder shattered like dry bamboo. Two mercenaries on the foredeck were swept clean off their feet, thrown across the center console into the engine well by the sheer hydraulic mass.

The gunboat’s engine shrieked as its propellers broke surface, churning air, before the boat slid sideways, taking hundreds of gallons over its port gunwale.

"Second boat on the jetty flank!" Lin’s voice echoed down from the crane gantry over the sound-powered telephone horn. "They're attempting a cut on the sub-surface cable!"

Jin swung the monitor. The brass collar groaned, protesting the lateral torque. The jet carved a boiling trench across the basin water, slicing through the midnight drizzle until it caught the second craft broadside. 

The jet sheared off the skiff’s outboard cowling with a metallic crunch, crushing the composite shroud into the cooling fan. White steam erupted as salt water flooded the hot cylinder block. The craft lurched violently, dead in the water, wallowing heavily as its self-bailing scuppers choked on river debris.

From the deck of the third skiff, twenty yards back, an operative raised a compressed-gas launcher. A blinding canister arced through the dark, striking the gantry crane’s iron stanchion and bursting into a dense, chemical aerosol curtain of gray tear gas.

The wind from the sea caught the cloud, rolling it directly into the pump bay.

Jin pulled his collar over his mouth, coughing violently. His lungs burned, the old bunker-fuel damage flaring into an agonized spasm that forced him to his knees. Rui fell beside him, retching onto the iron grating, his eyes streaming with tears.

"Aisyah..." Jin wheezed, his vision blurring.

Above them, the high-pitched whine of the gantry’s auxiliary hoist screeched into life.

Aisyah hadn't abandoned the crane controls. From her perch forty feet above the waterline, she threw the manual brake on the gantry’s empty spreader bar. 

Six tons of structural steel dropped straight down through the darkness.

It did not hit the skiff; it plummeted into the water four feet off its starboard beam. The displaced tidal surge slammed against the third craft with the force of a rogue wave, flipping the light interceptor onto its beam ends. The crew was hurled into the churning, oily blackness of the slipway basin.

The surviving first boat, half-swamped and its console smashed by water pressure, scrambled to engage its bow thrusters, desperately hauling its sodden comrades out of the chop. Its searchlight was dead, its electronics ruined by the sheer volume of salt brine that had drowned its open cockpits. 

Without firing a single shot, the remaining tactical craft backed frantically into the fairway, trailing oily foam and severed lines, its sirens dead, its crew shivering and defeated by the pure, unyielding mass of the sea.

Silence fell over Berth 9, broken only by the hiss of the receding wake against the stone wall and the ragged, gasping breaths of the three people on the valve platform.

Rui sat with his head between his knees, spitting clear saliva, his forearms trembling uncontrollably. Lin appeared at the bottom of the ladder, carrying a lantern shielded with a piece of cardboard, her face smudged with soot.

"They're clearing the channel," she whispered, her voice shaking. "They won't try the basin again tonight."

Jin pulled himself upright by the monitor's brass handle. His uniform was soaked through, cold mud caked across his shins. He looked down at the dark water where the skiffs had retreated.

Something bobbed against the wooden fender piles.

It was not a man, and it was not a shattered spar. It was a heavy, waterproof flight case that had been thrown from the swamped command boat, caught in the eddy behind the jetty.

Rui leaned over the edge with a boat hook, dragging the dripping plastic case onto the dock. The latches were sealed with red customs tape.

Jin dropped to one knee and thumbed the release.

Inside, resting on die-cut foam, was a commercial satellite terminal wired to a ruggedized field tablet. The screen was still illuminated under its tempered glass, displaying an active admiralty recovery ledger that updated every three seconds.

At the top of the queue, glowing amber in the darkness, was the registration of the Keppel-Batam shipyard itself.

Beneath it was the order authorization timestamped forty minutes ago, signed by the same hijacked credential. But beneath Jin’s forged name, a new field had populated, linking the Batam receiver’s terminal to an offshore operating account in Singapore.

The name on the beneficiary line made Jin’s breath catch in his throat.

It was not Euro-Asian Maritime Finance. 

It was the personal pension trust of Captain Jin Wei's own missing chief engineer—the one man who had gone down with the *Bintang Laut* three years ago.
