The halogen beam caught them like deer against salt-crusted timber. 

The light was so intense it turned the falling sleet into a curtain of blinding needles, bleaching the blue paint of the sea-glider down to the raw primer. Jin Wei did not flinch, though the glare stabbed into his optic nerves, instantly waking the dull, rhythmic throb behind his temples. Decades under the flickering fluorescent tubes of container engine rooms had left his eyes intolerant of sudden white light; he simply dropped his chin into the salt-stained collar of his oilskin and shifted his weight off his compressed lumbar discs.

Out on the ice shelf, the cantonal hydrofoil’s twin diesels rumbled at low idle, a wet, churning vibration that traveled up through the frozen soles of Jin’s sea boots. The craft was an armored wedge of aluminum and zinc, its bow sheathed in sacrificial steel to crack the surface rime.

On the foredeck, a figure in a heavy navy-blue storm parka raised a handheld megaphone. His breath curled into the halogen cone, thick as sheep’s wool.

“Slipway three is municipal reserve!” the voice called, shorn of electronic resonance by the freezing air. The French was flat, nasal, carrying the dry, unhurried cadence of a civil magistrate whose pension did not depend on bravado. “Curfew commenced at midnight under the cantonal emergency maritime decree. Power down your auxiliary turbines and identify your flag registry.”

Beside Jin, Lin Zhang’s fingers tightened on the strap of her canvas satchel until the knuckle bones showed white through her skin. Inside the padded nylon envelope against her ribs rested two solid-state ledger cores, still radiating a faint, residual warmth from their forty-hour run across the Mediterranean. If the Swiss maritime bailiffs impounded that satchel under the federal receivership writ, the Malacca drydocks were gone before breakfast. Three thousand seafarers would wake up in Batam to find their berths welded shut and their digital food scrip voided by the clearinghouses.

“Marcus,” Lin whispered, her voice barely carrying over the hydrofoil’s diesel clatter. “If they board, they’ll run the biometric hash against the Singapore admiralty warrants.”

“They aren’t boarding,” Marcus said. 

He didn’t reach for a weapon. He had none, and wouldn't have drawn one if he did. Instead, he reached into the pocket of his greasy mackinaw and pulled out a rectangular slip of yellow linen paper, folded into four stiff creases. He stepped away from the glider’s sponson, his steel-toed work boots crunching deliberately across the crust of frozen slush toward the end of the timber pier.

“Officer Baudin!” Marcus shouted. His voice was gravelly, worn rough by thirty years of Detroit foundry dust and river wind, but it carried an effortless, heavy authority that made the searchlight twitch slightly off Jin’s face.

The man on the hydrofoil lowered the horn. “Who’s that on the slip?”

“Marcus Cole. We met in November at the cantonal flood mediation board.” Marcus didn’t stop until his boots were two inches from the edge of the iced-over stringer. He held the yellow linen sheet out against the glare. “You’re idling forty meters past your territorial sector line, Henri. Under the 1888 Lac Léman Littoral Usufruct Accord, these timber slips belong to the Versoix Net-Menders’ Cooperative. Federal emergency curfews don’t apply to guild waters without a signature from the Grand Council of Geneva. You have that signature on your clipboard tonight?”

A long, agonizing silence hung over the ice, filled only by the rhythmic *chug-slosh* of the hydrofoil’s exhaust.

Jin watched the officer on the deck. He recognized the type: not a private security contractor itching for a bonus, but a tired fifty-year-old municipal harbor master with an arthritic knee and two years left until retirement, stuck running winter patrols because the federal banking commission had put the cantons on high-alert status. Baudin didn't want a riot on the Versoix slips. He wanted a cup of chicory and a warm radiator.

Baudin leaned over the rail, squinting through the falling sleet at the yellow sheet. He knew the paper. Every water cop between Lausanne and Geneva knew the antique linen stock of the local fishing guilds—legal landmines left behind by nineteenth-century socialist weavers that modern corporate lawyers spent millions trying to invalidate and always failed.

“The harbor master’s office is locked down until six, Marcus,” Baudin called back, his tone shifting from bureaucratic threat to weary irritation. “The private bailiffs from Zurich arrived on the night train. They’ve got five squads of private port marshals at the commercial docks. If that pontoon craft moves an inch into the federal channel, I have to impound it.”

“It’s not moving,” Marcus said. “The batteries are dead anyway. We’re securing the lines to the guild bollards and going inside to dry our socks.”

Baudin spat over the side into the black water churning around his props. “Two hours until dawn, Marcus. At seven, the commercial receivers file the universal foreclosure order at the Palais. After that, guild paper won’t buy you a loaf of rye.”

The hydrofoil’s engines revved with a heavy, throaty growl. The helm came hard over, the steel prow carving a wide arc through the broken surface slush, and the white cone of the searchlight swung away, sweeping across the dark, undulating surface of the lake toward the distant amber streetlamps of the quayside.

The darkness collapsed back over them, cold and suffocating.

“Come on,” Giulia muttered, her hand gripping Lin’s elbow. “The wind is turning north. The lake effect will freeze the rudder linkages if we stay on the ice.”

Marcus grabbed the glider’s bow painter, looping the thick braided nylon around an iron cleat embedded in the mossy oak pilings. With a practiced heave, he threw open the low cedar sliding door of the nearest boat-shed.

A wave of heat rolled out into the sub-zero night.

It wasn't the sterile, conditioned heat of an office tower; it was thick, living air that smelled of wet loden wool, curing pine tar, woodsmoke from three drum stoves, and the sharp, mineral tang of unwashed human sweat. 

Jin Wei stepped over the raised wooden threshold, his knees trembling slightly as the sudden warmth hit his frozen joints. 

The cavernous interior of the shed stretched fifty yards into the gloom, lit only by suspended storm lanterns and the orange glow of the iron stoves. Suspended from the massive timber rafters overhead were four antique wooden lake skiffs, their varnished ribs glowing like the skeletons of great fish. Beneath them, seated shoulder-to-shoulder on rough-cut pine benches and inverted crates, were hundreds of people.

They were dockers from Rotterdam with split lips and salt-rimed coats; rail engineers from the Ruhr with oil ground permanently beneath their fingernails; high-altitude line-workers from the Valais wearing wool balaclavas pushed up over their foreheads; and mariners in stiff blue dungarees who had managed to slip off the container ships before the Singapore bailiffs seized the gangways.

No one was cheering. No one was waving banners. They were chewing hard tack, scraping frost from their spectacle lenses, and checking mechanical pocket watches by lantern light.

At the far end of the shed, standing over a carpenter’s workbench cleared of wood-shavings, stood a woman in an oil-darkened leather flight jacket. Her skin was the deep, burnished bronze of the high Karoo, her hair cropped close to the scalp, and her right forearm bore the pale, puckered track of an arc-welder’s flash burn. 

Nia Ndlovu looked up as the sliding door thudded shut.

For five long seconds, the room fell dead silent. The only sound was the crackle of pine knots in the woodstove and the dry, hacking cough that Jin Wei couldn't hold back.

Nia crossed the sawdust-strewn floor, her stride unhurried, grounded by the heavy steel caps of her boots. When she reached Jin, she didn't offer a polite European handshake. She laid both palms flat against his upper arms, her calloused fingers digging into the wet wool of his pea coat with the fierce, anchoring grip of someone who had pulled men out of flooded mine shafts.

“You brought the strait,” she said quietly. Her voice was deep, textured like crushed shale.

Jin swallowed the metallic taste of his cough. “We lost three feeder tugs at the Horsburgh light. The mortgage receivers ran wire nets across the channel.” He reached back, gently taking Lin’s satchel and laying it on the nearest bench. “But the ledger roots are whole. Thirty-two hulls verified by their crews.”

Nia nodded slowly, her dark eyes reflecting the lantern flame. She looked past Jin to Marcus, then to Giulia.

Four people. Four continents. 

Marcus, who had kept the cold-forges of the Detroit rail loop running through two federal bankruptcies without firing a single round. Giulia, who had held the Alpine penstocks against the energy trusts using nothing but mechanical weir locks and cantonal water statutes. Jin Wei, whose battered bulkheads had turned the Malacca traffic lane into an open commons. And Nia, who had unified twelve thousand miles of sub-Saharan solar corridors across four closed borders by moving high-voltage inverters on the backs of mules.

They did not speak of victory. They had seen too many assembly organizers disappear into federal holding cells to believe in easy dawns.

“The sortition parity,” Giulia said, pulling off her wool gloves to reveal fingers stiff with frost. “What is the count?”

Nia gestured toward the long benches. “Three hundred and one selected delegates arrived before the cantonal police blocked the rail head. With the four of us standing together on this floor, we are three hundred and five.”

The odd parity. The Class Zero invariant, preserved across ten thousand miles of winter sea and cordoned track.

Lin stepped to the carpenter’s bench, unzipping her canvas satchel. She did not plug the cores into any network; there was no internet here, no cellular signal penetrating the zinc-lined roof panels. She pulled out a small, wind-up micro-projector—a mechanical device built by the Valais precision co-ops, driven by a brass clockwork spring.

She set it down on an overturned spruce barrel. The brass gear buzzed, a tiny shutter clicked open, and a sharp beam of amber light cast a map across the rough pine wall.

It was not a map of nations. It was a map of physical lifelines: the high-voltage catenaries of the Alps, the freight arteries of the Midwest, the Malacca shipping channels, the Sahelian solar routes—all converging like drawn veins upon a single, low gray building on the hill above them.

The Palais des Nations.

“Seven o'clock,” Lin said, her voice steady now, stripped of fatigue. “The Euro-Asian Maritime Finance syndicate and the Zurich clearinghouses have convened an extraordinary closed session in the council chamber. They intend to pass the Global Foreclosure Protocol under emergency receivership rules. If they sign it, the private bailiffs have statutory authority to cut power to every node that hasn't cleared commercial debt by noon.”

Marcus walked over to the wall, his heavy hand resting on the pine timber just beside the illuminated outline of the Palais. 

“The gates are locked,” Marcus said. “The Swiss federal police have placed concrete anti-ram blocks at the Pregny gate, and the private marshals have welded the turnstiles.”

Giulia stepped forward, adjusting her spectacles. From inside her wool tunic, she withdrew a folded sheet of vellum, yellowed and fragile along the margins, covered in faded blue draftsman’s ink.

“The gates are locked,” Giulia agreed, her voice flat and resolute. “The surface belongs to their bailiffs. But the Palais was built in 1930 over the old drainage system of the Ariana park estate. The lake overflow conduit runs from the timber slipway directly beneath our feet, straight up the hill into the central utility plenum under the Assembly floor.”

Jin Wei looked down at the floorboards beneath his boots, hearing the subterranean rush of lake water pushing through the ancient stone culverts below.

“The conduit is sixty centimeters wide,” Jin said, his seafarer’s eye immediately measuring the volume. “And the current is fighting the lake ice.”

Nia unbuttoned her flight coat, revealing her work harness and a coil of braided retrieval rope slung over her shoulder.

“We didn't cross four oceans to look at the front door,” she said.

The clockwork projector clicked, its brass spring winding down to its final, stubborn revolution. Outside, across the frozen surface of Lake Léman, the first gray light of dawn was beginning to bleed through the falling snow, and from the direction of the city, the low, mechanical rumble of the bailiffs’ diesel convoys began to shake the earth.
