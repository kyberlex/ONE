The groan of Berth 9 did not stop. It deepened, rattling the calcium-crusted iron railings until the rust fell in gray snow. On the seawall apron, four hundred refugees shrank back against the concrete parapet, their breath misting in the torrential damp as the canted freighter’s bow hung over them like a cliff face waiting to drop.

Inside the caisson control trench, the drydock ballast lines stuttered.

"Conduit three is dead," Lin shouted from the pump well, her voice fighting the roar of the bilge discharge. Her skin was the color of curdled tallow, dark hollows bruised beneath her eyes from three sleepless rotations. "The foundation shift sheared the conduit trench outside the sluice gate. Telemetry to the seaward ballast valves is gone. If we can’t balance the starboard tanks before the tidal rip peaks, the keel blocks will roll."

Captain Jin Wei didn’t waste words on panic. Decades in the lower flats of rust-bucket bulkers had taught him that steel gave three distinct warnings before it killed you: first a hum, then a tick, then a silence. The ship was still ticking.

"Aisyah," Jin barked.

Aisyah binti Rahman was already hauling the vulcanized drysuits from the salt-stiffened lockers behind the workshop. Her fingers, scarred at the pads where high-voltage arcing had left white calluses, moved with furious efficiency across the brass inflator valves. Before she had ever touched an ROV tether, she had spent her adolescence free-diving for spiny lobsters off the coral shelves of Pulau Semakau; she gauged the channel's rip not by navigational buoys, but by the lateral torque pulling at the tendon behind her left knee.

"Four knots out there, Captain," she said, pulling a neoprene hood over her cropped hair. Her voice was flat, level, stripped of youth. "Current’s turning against the monsoon runoff. It’s washing machine water down by the sill."

"We go off the outer caisson catwalk," Jin said, dragging a heavy full-face mask over his neck. His lumbar spine flared with familiar, grinding pain—two crushed discs from an exploded steam line thirty years back that made bending feel like chewing glass. As a boy, before he ever went to sea, he had learned to calm the terror of dark water by humming sea shanties in four-four time, matching his lungs to the thrum of a diesel exhaust. He hummed now, low in his throat, a harsh, gravelly drone. "Conduit raceway is three meters below the mudline. We have twenty minutes of slack window before the strait floods."

Two apprentice divers, boys from the Batam boatyards who had never worked deeper than a harbor mooring, stood frozen by the dive staging rack, their eyes wide and white against their grease-smeared faces.

"Tether to me," Jin told them, his tone dropping into the mechanical cadence of survival. "Keep your fins off the gravel. If you get pinned against the intake grate, don't kick. Dump your suit gas. Drop to the mud. Roll left. Understand?"

The boys swallowed and nodded, their hands trembling as they buckled their lead-shot weight belts.

They dropped from the caisson's low service platform directly into the black throat of the channel.

The water didn't feel like liquid; it felt like a moving wall of cold wet cement. The Singapore Strait was emptying east to west, funneled between the granite riprap of the breakwater and the shipyard’s massive concrete caisson. At four knots, current ceased to be a drift—it became a hydraulic ram. It snatched Jin’s rubber-suited shoulders, slamming him hard against the guide wire before his buoyancy compensator could bite.

Silt blinded him instantly. The tropical sun, already suffocated by monsoon cloud, vanished beneath ten feet of churned, muddy brine laden with harbor runoff, decomposing mangrove pulp, and microscopic diesel droplets.

Through the bone-conduction comms clamped behind his ear, Lin’s voice crackled, distorted by the static of water: *"Jin... starboard ballast pressure rising... the cant is three point five degrees. You have twelve minutes before the crane tracks buckle."*

"On the guide," Jin grunted into his oral-nasal cup. "Aisyah. Left pin."

"Got it," her voice came back, tight, rhythmic with heavy breathing.

Their dive lamps cut yellow, cone-shaped tunnels through the murk, illuminating swarms of suspended silt that looked like driving sleet. Jin pulled himself hand-over-hand down the anchor chain. The cold seeped through his suit, biting his scarred collarbone where an old steam-scald had left the tissue thin and numb. Behind him, the two apprentice divers kicked frantically, their fins thrashing against the current, burning through their air at twice the safe consumption rate.

"Stop kicking!" Jin rasped, his voice raw from bunker cough. "Arm-haul only. Save your lung."

They reached the concrete sill twenty feet down. Here, the current compressed, howling through the gap beneath the drydock gate like an underwater gale. The roar was deafening—a deep, subsonic vibration that rattled Jin’s teeth inside his gums.

There lay the raceway: a rectangular trough cast into the granite foundation, meant to protect the armored umbilical that carried the drydock’s pulse.

It was smashed.

A ten-ton concrete scour-block, dislodged by the listing freighter’s shuddering keel blocks above, had toppled directly across the steel conduit tray. The three-inch armored casing—sheathing two hundred strands of pure glass—was crushed flat beneath forty cubic feet of mossy aggregate. The outer steel braiding had peeled open like an overcooked sausage, its broken fiber cores trailing in the current like pale, severed horsehair.

"Crushed," Aisyah said, dropping to her knees on the slime-covered ledge. The current caught her dive cylinder, trying to flip her over the drop-off into the sixty-foot channel trench, but she jammed her heavy brass pry-bar into an expansion seam and held. "Zero continuity. Casing pinched clear to the copper sheath."

"Jack," Jin commanded, pointing his beam at the narrow crevice beneath the scour-block.

The first apprentice fumbled with the canvas tool bag. A brass-bodied hydraulic spread-jack slipped from his neoprene glove, tumbling toward the abyss. Jin’s hand shot out—instinct older than his pain—and snagged the high-pressure hose by two fingers, dragging the tool back with a jolt that sent an electric spike through his injured lower back. He didn't curse. Cursing cost air.

"Seat it," Jin ordered, thrusting the tool into Aisyah’s hands. "Under the rebar. Lift two inches. That’s all we need to pull the slack loop."

Aisyah wedged the flat jaws of the spreader into the narrow crack beneath the granite mass. She began pumping the manual lever. *Clack. Clack. Clack.*

The tool groaned under the strain, hydraulic fluid weeping past its salt-scored seals. Slowly, agonizingly, the concrete slab shifted, peeling up an inch from the steel trough. A plume of black, foul-smelling anaerobic mud puffed out, carried instantly away by the four-knot current.

"Slack coming!" Jin yelled, gesturing to the second apprentice. "Pull the green sleeve! Don't kink it!"

The boy reached into the recess, his fingers clumsy with fear. He hauled out the yellow emergency splice enclosure—an air-gapped, gel-filled junction box designed to be bridged underwater using mechanical splice blocks. But the cable had torn short. The factory terminal block was mangled, brass pins sheared off flush against the polyurethane boot.

"No connector," Aisyah panted, her breathing ragged in the mask. "We have to core-splice. Cold-cure resin."

Jin looked at his wrist gauge. Seven minutes of slack water left. When the tide turned fully, the reverse surge from the deep-water shipping channel would sweep this ledge with five knots of unyielding hydrodynamic force. No human grip could hold a pry-bar in that.

"Open the gel cassette," Jin said.

His fingers were thick, stiffened by age and salt, but inside the rubber diving mitts, the muscle memory remained. He stripped his outer gloves, exposing his bare skin to the biting, grease-slicked water. His fingers turned white in seconds, the cold deadening the nerves until he could barely feel the micro-thin glass strands.

Working inside the small canvas shelter-hood Aisyah held over the work site to break the flow of the current, Jin began cleaving the fiber ends with a manual ceramic blade.

One strand. Blue to blue.

*Click.*

Lin’s voice in his ear was frantic now: *"Hull list at four degrees! The refugee barricade is cracking, Jin! Berth nine is going!"*

"Shut up, Lin," Jin whispered, his breath whistling through his teeth.

Two strands. Orange to orange.

The current hammered the hood. Aisyah’s shoulder slammed against Jin’s helmet, but she didn't give an inch, wedging her knees into the concrete seam until the vulcanized rubber tore against the barnacles, sea-water flooding her left leg. She didn't make a sound. She just held the canvas.

Three strands. Green to green.

*Click.*

The tiny LED on the junction cassette flickered from amber to faint, pulsing cyan.

"Continuity!" Lin screamed over the comms. "Starboard three valve responding! Ballast pumps kicking—we're taking on water forward! She’s leveling!"

A great, hydraulic shudder ran through the caisson wall as the five-thousand-ton hull shifted back toward an even keel, the sound vibrating through the water like an earthquake.

Jin reached to snap the heavy waterproof clamps over the resin casing.

Then, from the channel abyss directly behind them, the current stopped dead.

Not a natural slack. The water went completely still for three heartbeats—then sucked violently *seaward*, dragging Jin, Aisyah, and the apprentice divers toward the deep navigation trench as a massive, low-frequency hum vibrated through the bedrock beneath their fins.

Something enormous had just entered the narrow channel mouth without navigation lights, displacing tens of thousands of tons of water in a single, blind surge.
