The backwash from the slipway surged over the slimy green timbers of Berth 9, foaming around the steel toes of Aisyah’s boots. 

She stood on the rusted lip of the drydock, her hands still trembling from the violent torque of the floodgate winch. Twenty paces out into the slick, ink-black water of the channel, the sea-glider was already little more than a low-profile shadow, its twin carbon foils shearing through the surface with a muffled, tearing hiss. There was no rooster-tail wake, no plume of diesel smoke—only two faint bioluminescent ribbons curling off the rudders as the electric drives sucked the low-voltage current from their ballast frames.

Beside her, Uncle Tan did not wave. Waving was for tourists at the ferry terminal. He stood with his shoulders hunched against the steady, lukewarm drizzle of the northeast monsoon, his swollen knuckles buried deep in the pockets of his canvas work coat. His right knee clicked faintly with every shift of his weight, the cartilage worn to gristle after forty years of squatting over steam manifolds.

"They're through the inner shoals," Tan murmured. His voice was a rasp of tobacco and salt-scoured vocal cords. "The current catches them under the beacon. If Jin keeps the foils tucked under four knots, the harbor hydrophones won't hear anything but the chop."

"Apex has two interceptors sitting dead in the water off the reef," Aisyah said. Her chest felt hollow, like an empty ballast tank echoing under a blowtorch. She wiped rain and grease from her eyelashes with the sleeve of her jumper. The rubberized fabric smelled of sulfur and hydraulic oil. "If they paint the glider with millimeter radar—"

"They won't look for fiberglass two inches off the water," Tan said, though his eyes never left the widening gap between the slipway and the fairway buoys. "They're looking for steel hulls with transponders. They’re looking for us."

A sudden, sharp glare split the low-hanging clouds over the western breakwater. A four-million-candlepower halogen beam swept across the mouth of Berth 9, dragging its harsh white cone across the corroded trusses of the gantry crane before slapping against the mud-slicked retaining wall ten feet below their feet.

Aisyah flinched, her hand dropping instinctively to the emergency cutoff key dangling from her belt lanyard. But Tan didn't flinch. He slowly drew a small tin from his pocket, thumbed it open with his calloused palm, and extracted a dried salted plum. He tucked it into the pocket of his cheek, an old habit from his boiler-tending shifts on the ore carriers, where the sour salt kept a man’s mouth wet when the ambient air was fifty degrees and dry as a furnace flue.

"Kill the slipway floodlights, Aisyah," he ordered softly. "Let the harbor see only scrap iron."

She slammed the main breaker down. The yellow sodium glow that had framed their frantic departure vanished, plunging the berth into the deep, sulfurous indigo of the tropical night.

Behind them, in the cavernous belly of the decommissioned machine shop, boots scraped on wet concrete. 

They weren't soldiers, and they weren't revolutionaries from a pamphlet. They were forty-odd men and women: tugboat engineers whose wages had been seized by receiver courts in Singapore, crane riggers with salt-rotted palms, and three generations of Bugis families who lived on the salvage lighters tied four-deep along Berth 11. They stood in the shadows beneath the crane rails, their faces pale in the ambient wash of the distant city lights across the strait.

In the front stood young Hisham, clutching a grease gun like a cudgel, his shirt soaked through with rain. "They're coming into the basin, Uncle," Hisham whispered. "The receiver’s cutter. They got three private patrol skiffs running alongside. They’re sounding their air horns at the outer boom."

Tan didn't answer immediately. He turned his heavy body around, facing the dark interior of the shop. His breath came slow and measured, wheezing slightly at the bottom of each exhalation—the legacy of inhaling powdered asbestos off the bulkheads of scrapped Greek bulkers in the eighties.

"They have paper," Tan said to the assembly. His voice didn't carry the ringing timber of Jin Wei’s quarterdeck baritone, but it settled into the damp iron room like heavy ballast. "They have an admiralty arrest warrant signed by an electronic clerk in a tower forty miles away. They think that paper owns the tidal cables. They think that paper owns the desalination pumps that keep your children’s drinking water sweet."

He looked at Aisyah. For all her brilliant understanding of high-voltage busbars and optical transceivers, she had spent her life hiding inside engine bilges and dive suits. When more than ten people looked at her at once, a red rash crawled up her neck, and she had an involuntary habit of counting the threads on the nearest hex bolt to keep her diaphragm from seizing.

*One, two, three, four,* she counted on the flange of the winch housing, her teeth set hard.

"Jin and the doctor are on the water," Tan continued, gesturing with his thumb toward the open dark. "They carry the signatures to Europe. But Geneva is ten thousand miles of saltwater, rail, and cold sky. What happens between this tide and the next belongs to Berth 9. Not to the court. Not to the syndicate. To us."

From across the basin, a metallic voice boomed through an amplified marine hailer, distorted by the wet air and the drumming rain:

*"Berth Nine, Keppel-Batam facility. You are occupying quarantined private infrastructure under maritime lien. Power distribution from the tidal turbine array is unauthorized and constitutes theft of sovereign assets. Stand down all mooring cables and prepare for inspection by harbor constabulary and authorized maritime receivers."*

The voice belonged to Assistant Receiver Lim—a man Tan had known for twenty years, a former shipping clerk who took the syndicate’s civil service pension and now carried an encrypted tablet through the docks, locking down ships whose charterers had missed a single interest coupon. Lim was not an assassin. He was an accountant who believed that if the ledgers didn't balance, the ships would stop bringing grain and the lights in the city would go out.

"Aisyah," Tan said without looking back. "The seawater monitors."

She stopped counting threads. The sudden necessity of action burned away the flutter in her throat. "Tidal batteries are at sixty-two percent. The pump manifolds have thirty atmospheres of head pressure."

"Do not aim for their crews," Tan said, his tone flat, unyielding. "Aim for their intakes. Flood their forepeaks. Let the sea weigh them down until their screws bite air."

Aisyah lunged for the manual control stand bolted to the crane’s south pillar. Her hands, mapped with small, silvery scars from arc flashes, flew over the brass gate valves. There were no digital consoles here; Jin had stripped the automated actuators two days ago to prevent remote corporate overrides. Everything was raw leverage: cold steel levers, bronze packings, and thick hydraulic hoses braided with stainless wire.

She threw her body weight against the primary lever.

High above them, mounted fifty feet up on the crane’s transfer deck, two industrial fire monitors roared to life. They didn't spit a gentleman’s stream. Fed directly by the harbor’s tidal surges via twin centrifugal pumps, they hurled two solid, four-inch columns of gray harbor water into the darkness. 

The roar was deafening, drowning out the whine of the incoming skiffs’ outboards. Down in the basin, the nearest tactical skiff—a sleek, Kevlar-hulled vessel belonging to the private security cordon—took the full weight of the jet directly across its bow. The force did not splinter the hull, but it drove the nose down six feet into the swell, swamping the open cockpit with thirty tons of foaming saltwater in five seconds. The skiff’s twin four-hundred-horsepower outboards sputtered, choked on their own flooded air intakes, and died with a violent shudder.

The megaphone fell silent, replaced by frantic shouting and the shrill scream of bilge alarms.

A second skiff swung wide, its searchlight dancing wildly across the rain-lashed crane, but Tan stepped forward to the secondary valve. Despite the arthritis that locked his shoulders, he leaned into the wheel, spinning it four turns to the left. A dense curtain of atomized seawater and low-pressure steam, drawn from the cooling jackets of the battery farm, hissed out across the low water. Within seconds, a blinding, impenetrable bank of white artificial fog blanketed the basin, scattering their searchlights into a useless, milky haze.

The tactical skiffs were blind. Their thermal scopes could see nothing through the churning wall of fifty-degree steam and cold salt mist.

In the sudden tactical lull, Tan turned to the crowd of dockers huddled under the gantry. The plum was gone from his cheek; his face was gray with the physical strain of the valve, but his posture was rigid as an anchor shank.

"Hisham. Take seven men to the tidal conduit locks. Set the physical deadbolts on the manual stops. If they bring an oxy-torch, turn the seawater relief valves into the trench. Aisyah."

She looked up from the pump manifold, her chest heaving, her knuckles white against the iron lever.

Tan pulled a heavy, tarnished brass ring from his neck. Suspended from it was the sortition counter for the shipyard—a simple mechanical clicker, scored with the marks of fifteen harbor stewards, used since the docks had first been abandoned to tally the distribution of clean water and battery hours.

He held it out to her.

"I can keep the pumps turning, girl," the old man said, his eyes softening behind his salt-crusted spectacles. "I know where the pipes rust and which valves stick in the monsoon. But the people need someone who can see the wires that bind this yard to Batam and the rest of the world. Jin Wei is gone. You are the engineer of the Commons now."

Aisyah looked at the ring. The wet brass gleamed under the dull reflection of the mist. She felt the terrifying weight of it—four hundred lives, the fragile tidal grid, the relentless pressure of the courts waiting out in the dark water.

She didn't give a speech. She reached out and took the brass ring, slipping it over her head until the cold metal came to rest against her collarbone, right above the faint scar from her first high-voltage slip.

"Sound the shift bell," she said, her voice small at first, then hardening as she looked toward the fog-choked basin. "Sound fifteen strokes for the stewards. We convene at the drydock sill. If the receivers want these berths, they’ll have to haul them up with their own bare hands."

Hisham gripped the bell lanyard. The deep, resonant bronze chime rang out across the water—one, two, three—cutting through the hiss of the steam monitors and rolling out across the Malacca Strait.

Then, from atop the crane tower, eighty feet in the air, a tiny, rapid point of cyan light began to blink through the driving rain. 

Aisyah froze, looking up. It wasn't the sweeping halogen of the harbor patrol. It was the free-space optical laser link from the Batam repeater tower, cutting through a brief tear in the monsoon squall. 

Three short pulses, one long, then a frantic, uncoordinated staccato of light. 

It was the emergency pre-arranged signal from the southern line. Aisyah pulled her pocket receiver from her coat, watching the raw binary translate into mechanical red dots across the small air-gapped display.

The message had not come from the strait. It had been bounced across the Indian Ocean relay from the Mediterranean link:

*GENEVA AIRSPACE DECLARED CLOSED BY CANTONAL POLICE. RECEIVERS AT PALMETTO TRUST HAVE FILED EXPEDITED LIQUIDATION. YOU HAVE SIX HOURS BEFORE THE SOVEREIGN EMBARGO FREEZES ALL OFFSHORE ROUTING.*

Aisyah looked from the screen out into the black fairway, where Jin Wei’s glider was already running the gauntlet of the outer acoustic gates, completely unaware that the sanctuary they were racing toward was already closing its doors.
