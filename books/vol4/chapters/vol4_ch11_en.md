The outward surge dragged Jin’s boots off the caisson rim, throwing his weight against the safety lanyard with a violence that popped the stitching along his harness. In the black churn of the channel mouth, the deadweight super-freighter slid past without a single running light, a wall of slab-sided steel five storeys high displacing tens of thousands of tons of deep water as it coasted on its own momentum toward the sandbar. It was not firing. It did not need to. It simply dropped both bow anchors into the navigation channel with a grinding roar that shook the seabed, slewing across the fairway to choke off the last open approach to Berth Nine.

Jin kicked against the undertow, his left knee screaming where old cartilage had ground to powder. He hooked his forearm under Aisyah’s collar, dragging her back into the shelter of the concrete footing. Her suit was ripped from hip to calf, the cold brine already leaching the color from her skin, but she still clamped both hands around the fiber-splice box, pressing it to her chest like a newborn.

Above them, on the crane gantry, Lin Zhang’s voice crackled through the ear-piece, pinched with terror: *"The fairway is blocked. They’ve dropped a four-hundred-meter hulk across the shallows. No supply skiffs are getting through from the islands. Jin, we’re corked."*

"Get the ladder," Jin rasped into the dead microphone of his mask, his throat raw with the taste of sulfur and heavy fuel. He spat a mouthful of oily foam into the scuppers as they broke the surface beside the slipway. "Lin. The rope."

Ten minutes later, they stood dripping on the iron plates of the fabrication shed. The monsoon heat slammed into them like a wet wool blanket, forty degrees of swamp air turning the salt crust on their skin into an itching paste.

Berth Nine was starving, and the four hundred and twelve mariners sheltered in its cavernous guts already knew it.

Inside the old mess deck, beneath overhead beams painted in flaking lead primer, the air smelled of scorched vinegar and stale sweat. Three hundred men and women—Burmese deckhands, Indonesian grease-wipers, laid-off crane operators from the container terminals—sat along the trestle benches. In front of each lay a plastic bowl containing three ounces of split peas, boiled until the skins split, and two dry biscuits gray with pantry weevils.

At the head of the galley counter, a shouting match had flared between two tug engineers over a five-gallon plastic drum of cooking oil. One had a rusted pry-bar raised; the other held an iron ladle like a truncheon.

*Clang.*

The brass telegraph bell above the scullery door rang once.

The sound was small, dry, and deliberate. Uncle Tan stood beside the bulkhead, one swollen hand resting on the counter-weight lever. He was seventy-one, his spine curved like a ship’s rib after a grounding, his fingers misshapen by decades of monsoon dampness that turned his knuckles into knobby ginger roots. As a boy in the swamp kampongs of the Kallang Basin, before the government had dredged the creeks and poured concrete over the mangrove nurseries, he had caught fighting crickets in wooden matchboxes, learning to judge the wetness of the mud simply by pressing his palm against the bank in the dark. He carried no clipboard, no megaphone. He wore only a stained cotton undershirt and faded blue drill trousers rolled above his shins.

"Five minutes," Tan said softly. His voice did not carry the bark of a bosun, but it cut through the room’s hot, stagnant air with the weight of someone who had seen three shipping empires dissolve into bankruptcy auctions. "Telegraph says cool down. Put the iron down, boys. The iron doesn't feed nobody."

The engineer with the pry-bar stared at him, chest heaving, veins standing out on his neck like hemp lines. For four seconds, nobody breathed. Then the younger man’s shoulders dropped, the tool hitting the linoleum with a dull thud.

"They cut the Batam run, Uncle," the engineer muttered, wiping his wet face on his sleeve. "The market barges won't cross the fairway with that bulk-ship sitting in the channel. Two days. That's what’s in the dry stores. Then what? We eat rust?"

"We eat what the sea gives," Tan said. He turned and pointed a crooked finger toward the dark, cavernous hollow beneath Berth Nine's drydock floor. "Grab the scrap scrapers. Bring the lye tubs. We have six thousand tons of empty iron downstairs."

Beneath the slipway lay the hull of the *Heng Shan*, an abandoned thirty-year-old bulk ore carrier that the port receivers had left to rot when her owners folded in the tanker crash of '18. Her bottom plates sat flat on the keel blocks, her twin starboard ballast tanks—each forty meters long, eight meters deep, designed to swallow twelve thousand tons of dirty seawater to balance an empty crossing—hollow as cathedral tombs.

By midnight, sixty mariners were down in the belly of Tank Port-Four.

The heat inside the enclosed steel was suffocating, thick with the stench of thirty years of anaerobic mud, dried fuel residue, and dead barnacles. Portable floodlights run off the tidal turbine's night-cycle battery cast harsh, blinding glare against the rust-scaled frames. Jin Wei swung an iron scraper against the bulkhead, his lower back knotting into a hard, fiery spasm with every stroke. Flakes of red scale peeled away, falling into the bilge water around his boots in sharp, metallic sheets.

"Not down to bare metal," Tan shouted from the access hatch, his knees clicking as he climbed down the vertical rung ladder one rung at a time, testing each steel rod with his weight before trusting it. "You scrub to the plate, the raw iron bleeds oxide into the water! The gills choke in six hours! Just knock off the slime and the petroleum crust! Then the lime wash!"

Aisyah was already wading through the knee-deep black slurry at the tank’s aft end, dragging a flexible suction hose salvaged from the bilge pumps. Her eyes were red-rimmed, ringed with dark grease, her temple bruised where the dive-rig had slammed her against the caisson, but she moved with cold, mechanical economy. Every ten minutes, the suction strainer clogged on a knot of decayed rope or thick bunker sludge; every ten minutes, she plunged her bare hands into the foul water, cleared the mesh, and cursed in quiet, steady Malay.

"We have no freshwater to fill this," Jin said, pausing to lean against his scraper, his chest rattling as he fought for breath in the damp air. "Even if Lin keeps the reverse-osmosis stack running on the tidal trickle, that’s four tons a day. We need three hundred tons just to float the bio-filters."

Tan reached into the pocket of his trousers and pulled out a fistful of dark, waxy green leaves. He crushed them between his palms, holding them under Jin’s nose.

The smell was sharp, brackish, rich with the rot of estuarine peat.

"Mangrove apple," Tan said. "Avicennia marina. Black mangrove shoots. The boys took the work-skiff into the drainage canals behind the old cement works this afternoon. Pulled two hundred seedlings out of the mudflats. They drink sea brine, Jin. They sweat the salt out through the back of their leaves like an old stoker."

He gestured toward the long, tiered staging racks the shipyard carpenters were already bolting to the tank’s interior ribs using angle-iron sliced from scrapped container flats.

"We don't desalt the water," Tan said, kneeling down and dipping his hand into the shallow channel they had carved through the center of the tank floor. He tasted the residue on his finger, his weathered face tightening as he gauged the silt. "We run the high-tide surge through the sea-chests, through two gravel beds to drop the harbor grit. Then the mangrove roots take the ammonia. We put the sea bass fingerlings from the river fish-traps on the lower tier. Tilapia in the middle. Mangrove greens on top. We don't ask the dry land for a single grain of rice."

"The water is hot, Uncle," Aisyah said, hauling the cleared hose to the next frame. "The hull plates sit in thirty-five-degree mud outside. Sea bass boil in that."

"Not when the tide runs," Tan replied. He tapped the thick steel skin of the hull with his knuckles. "Six hours in, six hours out. The channel current pulls fifteen knots of deep, cold bottom-water past this plate twice a day. We plumb the intake low, through the old sea-suction valves on the bottom floor. The sea becomes our radiator."

It was grueling, brutal work, devoid of grace. There were no digital readouts to tell them if the chemical balance was true. They relied on Tan’s tongue to measure the salt, on the smell of the bilge wash to know when the caustic soda had neutralized the fuel oil, and on the blistered hands of eighty starving sailors working by the yellow glow of work-lights to assemble two hundred meters of perforated PVC pipe.

By four in the morning, the first wash-water was drained. The twin ballast tanks gleamed dull gray under a fresh coat of slaked agricultural lime. Above the tanks, suspended from the cross-beams, rows of broad-spectrum LED strips salvaged from refrigerated fruit containers flickered on, casting a violent, magenta dawn across the rust-streaked steel.

In the shallow, aerated water of the lower troughs, forty dozen small, silver-scaled estuary bass—netted from the industrial runoff creeks by the younger deckhands during the slack tide—darted between the submerged bundles of mangrove roots.

Jin sat on an overturned chemical bucket at the edge of the tank, his palms raw, his spine locked in a dull, thrumming ache that radiated into his thighs. Across the tank, mariners were leaning over the iron rails, their faces bathed in the strange purple light, watching the small fish turn in the clear, brackish wash. For the first time in six days, nobody was shouting. The silence inside the ship’s iron belly was broken only by the rhythmic, bubbling hiss of the compressed-air lines.

Then, through the twenty-millimeter steel plate of the bulk carrier’s bottom hull, came a sound.

It was not the slap of the tide.

It was a heavy, metallic *thunk*, followed by the distinct, high-frequency whine of an underwater hydraulic core-drill biting into the steel plate directly beneath their feet.

Jin froze. Tan’s hand went still on the water valve.

A second *thunk* echoed through the framing, sixty feet forward, accompanied by the magnetic clatter of a heavy hull-crawler locking onto the exterior keel. The receiver's tactical salvage crews had not waited for starvation to do their work. They were under the hull, boring into the ballast intake chests to flood the drydock before the morning assembly could convene.
