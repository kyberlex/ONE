The horn blasts did not stop. They rolled across the shallow swell of the Malacca Strait in rolling five-second bellows, deep enough to shudder through the rusted iron plates of the watchtower floor beneath Jin Wei’s boots.

One after another, the silhouettes on the outer perimeter—sixteen laden crude carriers, nine bulk freighters wallowing low under Indonesian coal, thirty feeder vessels anchored in the roadstead—lit their deck floods. The blinding white sodium glare cut through the tropical rain, turning the gray horizon into a floating city of cold steel.

Beside him, Lin Zhang leaned her forehead against the salt-streaked glass of the watch station, her breath leaving a brief circle of fog on the pane. Her thumb tapped a silent, rhythmic cadence against her collarbone—a habit she had kept since her student days in Beijing, measuring the erratic racing of her pulse against bone whenever the exhaustion threatened to pull her under.

"It isn't an attack," she whispered, her voice gravelly from two days without sleep.

"No," Jin Wei said. His lower spine burned with an old, jagged heat, the vertebrae grinding together like dry gears every time the wind rocked the gantry. He smelled the sulfur of cheap bunker fuel drifting from the south, mingled with the sweet, vegetative stink of mangrove mud exposed by the falling tide. "They’re swinging into the current. Every last one of them."

Down on the pier, the heavy Manila mooring hawsers of the *Minerva Star* creaked under tension as dockhands threw them over the bollards. Aisyah binti Rahman was already at the edge of the caisson, her yellow slicker smeared with grease from the turbine winches. Her fingers, still tender and scarred where a short-circuit arc had bitten the flesh three weeks prior, gripped a battered bullhorn. She was not shouting orders; she was waving lanterns in short, deliberate arcs, guiding the swollen freighter into the silt-dredged pocket.

Across the water, the radio console sputtered. The commercial frequencies—the locked channels rented by Euro-Asian Maritime Finance and guarded by corporate dispatch in London—were silent, deadened by the dropped links. But the open line, the simple copper-wired channel patched through the gantry crane’s laser receiver, was hissing with human breath.

"Berth 7, this is *Crest Harmony*," an unfamiliar voice crackled through the speaker, speaking in heavily accented Tagalog-English. "Draft fourteen meters. We have four hundred tons of grain spoilage in hold two because the port clearance lockout refused our customs token. Our master has stepped off the bridge. Chief mate speaking. We are refusing the Singapore anchorage tug fee. Do you have a pilot who knows the shoals south of Pulau Bukom?"

Before Jin Wei could lift the handset, another voice broke over the carrier wave, flat and hard with Northern Chinese vowels. "Bulk carrier *Donghai Nine*. We have three days of drinking water remaining. The company account in Hong Kong is frozen by admiralty lien. We can't pay the bunker barge. Is your desal line live?"

Lin Zhang’s tablet did not show digital charts or streaming satellite telemetry. It showed only the raw pulse of the mesh: local radio nodes bouncing coordinates from mast to mast, ship to ship, an invisible web stretching fifty miles across the chokepoint. The corporate tracking grid, built to meter every drop of fuel and lease every nautical mile to the highest bidding syndicate, was going blind. It had not been hacked; its mariners had simply turned the bridge consoles toward the wall.

"Jin," Lin said softly, her eyes tracking the flickering amber pulses across the screen. "It's the whole eastern channel. The second mate on the *Aegis Osprey* just threw their satellite decryptor over the side. They’re running on magnetic compass and coastal lights."

Below them, on the gravel apron between the machine shops and Drydock 4, a crowd of mariners had gathered under the tin overhang. Uncle Tan stood among them, his wool sweater soaked through at the shoulders, his arthritic knees stiff as he ladled hot ginger tea from a twenty-liter stockpot into dented tin mugs. He did not speak of treaties or shipping charters. When a soot-streaked oiler from an Apex tug stumbled up the ramp, shivering from the cold squall, the old man simply pressed a steaming cup into the man’s calloused palms and pointed toward the dry warmth of the toolroom.

A door slammed open at the base of the gantry.

Chen Rui came taking the metal stairs three at a time, his forearms bound in tight elastic bandages to brace his swollen tendons against the vibration of the dock cranes. His face was pale, his eyes wide.

"The pilot boats," Chen panted, catching his breath against the iron handrail. "Hauer’s flagship. The *Apex Vanguard*. She just cut her engines half a mile off the western reef."

Jin Wei turned his binoculars toward the deep-water passage. Through the lashing drizzle, the low, predatory profile of the private tactical cruiser was visible, wallowing sideways in the four-knot tidal rip. Her long-range strobe arrays were dark. Her twin diesel turbines, which usually threw a rooster-tail of white foam thirty yards into the air, were gurgling at idle, barely holding her head against the swell.

Aboard the *Apex Vanguard*, Hauer stood on the bridge wing, rain plastering his gray hair against his temples.

He held the satellite terminal to his ear, but all he heard was the flat, synthetic tone of an automated liquidation notice. The Hong Kong branch of Euro-Asian Maritime Finance had initiated a margin call forty minutes ago; the fuel credit cards for his three interceptors had been invalidated mid-channel. The digital bunkering barge that was supposed to meet him at the fairway buoy had already turned its bow toward Batam, its crew refusing to pump a single liter of marine gas oil without a sovereign settlement that London could no longer guarantee.

Beside him, his young helmsman was watching the depth sounder.

"Current is dragging us toward the shoals, Commander," the boy said, his voice cracking. "We have twenty minutes of auxiliary fuel in the day tank. If we don't start the main engines and run for the commercial berths, we’ll ground the rudders on the reef."

"The commercial berths are locked," Hauer said, his voice hollowed of its usual military precision. "The port authority has shut the gates until the admiralty court opens in Geneva on Monday morning. They won't take our lines."

"Then where do we go?"

Hauer looked across the water. Through the mist, the high, orange skeletal towers of the Keppel-Batam yard burned like a cluster of torches against the black jungle shoreline. There were no armed cutters patrolling its entrance. There were only two wooden work-skiffs, driven by longtail outboard motors, circling the incoming freighters with coils of heavy braided line, guiding forty-thousand-ton giants through the narrow channel by the naked eye and thirty years of local mud-knowledge.

He looked down at his desk. His tactical roster, his automated intercept logs, his legal indemnities signed by three ministries of transport—none of them could turn a propeller without fuel. None of them could feed forty hungry deckhands whose wage cards had just evaporated into bank restructuring receivership.

Hauer picked up the VHF radio microphone. He felt the cold zinc of the casing bite into his palm. He did not call the command center in Jurong. He clicked the dial to the open local channel.

"Berth 7," he said, the words dragging out of him like anchor chain across rock. "This is *Apex Vanguard*. We have lost main propulsion due to fuel exhaustion. We have forty-two crew on board. Requesting permission to come alongside the breakwater."

In the watchtower, the room went entirely quiet except for the hiss of rain on the corrugated roof.

Chen Rui looked at Jin Wei, his mouth slightly open. Lin Zhang did not move; she only stared at the small brass telegraph timer mounted beside the radio.

Jin Wei walked over to the console. He remembered his first command, forty years ago—a rust-streaked timber coaster that had lost its rudder in a typhoon off Hainan. The port master at Kaohsiung had left them outside the breakwater for four days because their insurance certificate lacked an underwriter’s stamp, while his men ate raw rice and bailed seawater with plastic buckets until their hands bled.

He reached down and keyed the transmitter.

"Apex Vanguard," Jin Wei said, his voice level, gravelly, carrying no triumph, only the heavy, unmistakable weight of the sea. "Cut your auxiliary. Drop your port hook in four fathoms off the south spit to stop your drift. We’re sending two longtail boats with a tow line. You will surrender your steering to the harbor watch."

He paused, glancing out at the bay where eighty ships now rode the swell together, their lights reflecting in long, golden ribbons across the dark water.

"And bring your mess tins," Jin Wei added. "Uncle Tan made tea."

The line clicked off.

Lin Zhang let out a long, ragged breath that sounded like a sob, though her face remained set in stone. She turned away from the glass and began shutting down the terminal monitors, one by one. The blue glare died, leaving only the amber glow of the kerosene storm lanterns hung from the gantry beams.

"It's over here," she said quietly.

"No," Jin Wei said, looking down at his sea bag resting against the bulkhead—the old canvas sack he had packed forty-eight hours ago, containing two clean shirts, his coastal master’s license, and the wooden sortition token he had drawn in the first assembly. "The channel is open. Now we have to make sure they can never close it again."

He looked through the rain toward the deep slipway where the electric sea-glider sat low in its cradle, its carbon hulls dripping with freshwater runoff, its batteries humming as they drew the last surge of power from the falling tide.

Aisyah appeared at the top of the tower stairs, wiping salt spray from her cheeks with the back of her sleeve. "The glider is trimmed, Captain. Tide turns north in twenty minutes. If we leave now, we catch the dawn window through the Sunda Strait before the naval cordon can reposition."

Jin Wei picked up his bag. The weight was familiar, balanced against the persistent ache in his hip. He looked at Lin Zhang, who had already slung her battered leather satchel over her shoulder.

"Geneva," she said, testing the word as if it belonged to another planet.

"Geneva," Jin Wei agreed, stepping out into the wind.

Down on the pier, as their boots hit the wet timber, the horn of the *Minerva Star* answered the outer anchorage with three short, clean blasts—the ancient mariner’s signal for a vessel underway, clear of the shoals, and moving into the open sea. But as the glider’s lines were cast off and the hull slid silently into the black current, Jin Wei’s eyes caught a solitary red beacon flashing three miles out on the perimeter—not a civilian distress flare, but the tight, pulsing strobe of an approaching high-speed sovereign hydrofoil that carried no maritime identification at all.
