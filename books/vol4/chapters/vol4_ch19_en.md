"Port monitors! Now!" Jin Wei’s voice tore raw against the iron bulkhead.

The pneumatic *thwump* of the interceptor’s deck gun sounded across the churned basin like an axe striking wet cedar. A four-pronged steel grapple trailing sixty fathoms of weighted synthetic braid ripped through the low vapor, arcing straight toward the ferry’s starboard cooling intake. 

Kian didn’t drop to the deck. He threw his shoulder into the bronze wheel of the salvaged harbor tug monitor mounted on Berth 8. The valve stem shrieked, caked with hardened salt crust, before yielding with a sudden wet shudder. 

Two thousand gallons of silted seawater per minute erupted from the brass nozzle under twelve atmospheres of head pressure.

The heavy, white column of water punched through the air, catching the grapple mid-flight. The hydraulic mass deflected the trailing irons six yards off-axis; the barbed flukes slapped harmlessly against the green-slime apron of the concrete pier and skittered into the dark water below. The jet didn't stop there. Kian cranked the elevation worm-gear down, depressing the nozzle until the solid hydraulic ram slammed directly across the lead skiff’s composite foredeck. 

The two private security contractors in charcoal flotation vests were blown off their knees. The sheer kinetic tonnage of water swamped the craft’s low-slung well deck, depressing its nose until the twin waterjets sucked surface froth and cavitated with a shrill, dry whine.

"Hold them off the pontoon!" Jin Wei coughed, clutching his collarbone where the knot of old scar tissue pulled tight against his neck. The vibration from the monitor’s discharge traveled through the iron deck and straight into his lumbar spine, grinding the worn discs until a hot spike of pain radiated down his left calf. "Don't let them foul the raw-water intake! If we lose the heat exchangers, the battery pack cooks in twenty minutes!"

Across the narrow basin, the second interceptor banked hard to evade the churning wash, its waterjet nozzles throwing two tall wings of spray. The pilot didn't panic. These weren't street mercenaries hired off an alley dock; they were licensed civil asset recovery contractors operating under maritime enforcement warrants, disciplined and steady. Recognizing the defensive arc of the shore-side monitor, the second craft backed down its throttles, holding position just outside the monitor’s trajectory while the third skiff maneuvered to throw a secondary line over the outer gate's floating booms.

They were probing for an angle, burning through high-grade marine diesel, waiting for the battery banks driving the harbor pumps to drop their charge.

Thirty yards inboard, buried beneath two decks of rusted passenger seating in the ferry’s converted purser’s office, Lin Zhang heard none of the water cannons. 

She heard only the dry, relentless hum of a stripped refrigeration fan and the wet, shallow whistle of her own breath.

A copper-cased drafting stylus—blunt-tipped, completely devoid of ink, an old habit from her first year drafting circuit traces by hand in a windowless Shenzhen basement—rolled between her thumb and forefinger. Her vision was tunneling. The migraine aura had begun ten minutes earlier as a jagged crescent of yellow light in her left eye, and now it was chewing through her peripheral field, turning the steel edges of her desk into pulsing smears of zinc.

On the cathode-ray terminal before her, fed by the cyan laser pulsing down from the crane’s optical receiver, the inbound transmission from Batam was clearing. 

It was not an administrative ledger. It was the compiled core logic of Aegis-Omni’s regional risk-scoring system: sixty thousand lines of unadorned, industrial execution script.

Lin jammed the heel of her palm against her left temple, pressing hard until her eyeball throbbed, trying to mechanically crush the nerve headache back into submission. Her skin was clammy with the sickening, sour grease of third-day adrenaline.

"Come on," she whispered, her voice a cracked reed. "Show me what you call a default."

She did not use automated decompilers. Automated tools smoothed over structural anomalies, interpreting jagged human malice as routine optimization. Instead, she used her blunt stylus to tap rhythmically on the edge of the Bakelite casing, reading the raw procedural flow top to bottom, the way she used to audit power-station load balancing during the summer blackouts.

Five minutes in, her breath caught behind her ribs.

The algorithm had not calculated the creditworthiness of Jin Wei’s coastal freighter or the forty-two regional tramp steamers rotting along the outer shoals. It had never audited their manifests, their engine logs, or their actual bills of lading.

Tucked inside the deep recursion of the automated escrow routine lay a synthetic multiplier. 

Whenever an independent vessel entered the Singapore or Malacca traffic separation channels, the system pinged the vessel’s automatic identification transponder. If the registry flagged the hull as non-syndicated—free of parent-firm commercial debt, operating under municipal or private family ownership—the script triggered a covert secondary transaction. 

A ghost liability. 

It automatically generated an unnotified micro-charge for "predictive berth reservation insurance" through an offshore holding firm in Labuan. The charge was tiny—less than forty dollars an hour. But it was deliberately billed to a dormant, unmonitored clearance account that the vessel’s master had never been assigned the keys to access.

Within fourteen days at sea, the unpaid micro-charges crossed the default threshold. 

At that exact millisecond, the logic branch inverted. The synthetic debt was automatically packaged, assigned an astronomical compounding penalty rate of four hundred percent, and transferred via automated clearinghouses to Euro-Asian Maritime Finance. 

The ship had not missed a fuel payment. It had not defaulted on a hull mortgage. 

The debt was an algorithmic phantom, birthed entirely within the software to manufacture a legal pretext for an admiralty arrest warrant before the ship ever made landfall.

"They didn't go broke," Lin breathed into the hot, dim air of the cabin. Her stomach heaved, a sudden wave of acid rising in her throat. "None of them went broke."

She grabbed a grease pencil from the desk tray, her hand shaking so hard the tip snapped against the glass terminal. Cursing under her breath, she used the splintered stub to circle a sequence of memory addresses directly on the screen. 

The ships hadn’t fallen to the brutal physics of the sea or the unforgiving arithmetic of global trade. They had been harvested. The system was designed to systematically strip independent mariners of their titles, forcing their hulls into court-mandated receiver auctions where the parent syndicates bought them back for pennies on the ton, re-flagging them under corporate flags of convenience.

Footsteps clattered down the iron companionway. Uncle Tan ducked through the low doorway, his cotton singlet soaked with a mixture of salt spray and gray diesel exhaust. His knuckles were swollen, the joints thick as ginger roots from fifty years of damp bilges, and he held a chipped enamel mug of boiled rainwater laced with sweetened condensed milk.

"Jin Wei has the monitors running on low head to conserve the battery cells," Tan said, his gravelly voice remarkably calm despite the dull thuds echoing from the outer gate. "The skiffs have pulled back fifty yards. They’re floating on their sea anchors. They know the tide turns at dusk. When the channel current slacks, our turbines stop spinning, and our batteries run dry."

He set the mug on the corner of the crate beside her, his dark eyes taking in the circled hex strings on the glass.

"Did you find the leak?"

Lin pointed the broken grease pencil at the terminal, her fingers trembling. "There is no leak, Tan. There was never an audit failure. Look here. Look at this execution log from eight months ago. The *Bintang Timur*—your nephew's tanker. They said he abandoned his crew because he couldn't cover the canal transit fees."

Tan’s weathered jaw set, the tendons in his neck tightening like dry hemp rope. He didn't speak. He only stared at the phosphor numbers.

"The canal fee was paid in full six days before he arrived," Lin said, the words tumbling out of her in a furious, ragged hiss. "The system routed his clearance funds into an unlinked escrow pocket. Then it generated three false bunkering invoices from an empty terminal in Johor, declared him insolvent, and issued the lien directly to the port marshal. He didn't abandon anyone. They engineered his bankruptcy while he was still three days out in the Natuna Sea."

Tan reached out slowly, his thick, calloused index finger hovering just above the glass, tracing the serial code of the phantom lien. A slow, terrible stillness settled over his broad face—not the wild heat of anger, but the cold, immovable weight of an old anchor finding rock at the bottom of a bay.

"They used a court stamp to steal his life," Tan whispered.

"Not a court stamp," Lin said, wiping sweat and grease from her brow with her forearms, leaving a dark smudge across her temple. "A cryptographic key. An admiralty receiver's private authorization signature. It’s hardcoded right into the forfeiture routine."

She tapped the key on her keyboard, calling up the root certificate embedded in the script's execution header.

"If we broadcast this source tree across the regional mesh—if the crews in Batam, Penang, and the anchorage see the actual transaction IDs—the entire legal fiction collapses. Every single arrest warrant issued in the strait over the last three years becomes an act of statutory piracy."

Before Tan could answer, the ferry’s overhead fluorescent tube flickered and died. 

The only light left in the cabin was the sickly green glow of the terminal and the tiny amber pilot light on the wall-mounted battery inverter. 

From the companionway above, Jin Wei’s boots hit the steel steps in a hurried, uneven cadence.

"Lin! Tan!" Jin Wei reached the doorway, his chest heaving, his blue work shirt soaked to the skin. "The tactical skiffs aren't waiting for slack tide."

Lin looked up, her vision swimming with yellow floaters. "What are they doing?"

"They just deployed a tethered diving sledge from their support tender," Jin Wei said, his knuckles whitening as he gripped the doorframe for support. "They aren't aiming for the cooling intake anymore. They’re running a hydraulic cutting line directly along the harbor floor, straight for the turbine anchor catenaries. If they sever those power cables, the whole yard goes dark in ninety seconds."
