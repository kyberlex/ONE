The counterweight dropped with a dull, subterranean thud that resonated through the soles of Jin’s work boots. 

Beneath the caisson pier, the yard’s rusted gravity bypass gave way. Eighty thousand tons of trapped tidal ballast, collected across twelve hours of rising monsoon chop, tore through the lower sluice gates like a collapsing wall of slate. The displaced sea erupted out of the slipway in a churning, white-water boil, catching the lead Apex interceptor mid-surge. 

The rigid-hull raider, built for precision boarding, was lifted six feet on the crest of the outrushing current. Its twin outboards screamed in free air as the propellers lost bite, cavitating against the foam, before the craft was swept hard broadside against the heavy timber fenders of Berth 7. The fiberglass groaned, a sickening screech of resin under compression. Tactical contractors in black drysuits scrambled for handholds as dark harbor water washed over their gunwales, swamping their deck lockers and cutting their megaphonic hailer into a wet, choking squawk.

They did not panic. They were professionals working a salvage contract. Even as their boat rocked violently in the hydraulic backlash, two men at the bow leveled pneumatically driven grappling hooks, firing steel-tipped anchors into the pitted concrete coping of the pier. The winches whined, fighting the outward surge to secure their purchase.

*“They’re anchoring!”* Jin shouted through the squall, coughing as the diesel-tainted spray coated his mouth. *“Tan, the second skiff is circling wide to the crane tracks!”*

“They won’t climb the crane if the yard has teeth,” Tan said. His old fingers were white from the effort of pinning the sluice lever down. “Aisyah! The sub-station! The channel current is at peak rip!”

High above them on the gantry boom, sixty-five meters in the screaming gale, Aisyah didn’t answer with words. She had already clipped her safety lanyard to the vertical guide cable. 

Instead of climbing down the steel ladder rung by wet rung, she locked her leather-palmed descent mitts onto the braided steel line, kicked out into empty air, and dropped.

The monsoon wind slammed her against the lattice bracing, the impact driving the air from her chest, but she didn’t brake until the final ten meters. Her boots hit the wet wooden decking of the drydock gallery with a sharp crack. She unhooked in a single fluid motion, her knees absorbing the shock. Her muscles burned from sixteen hours of continuous wet-suit diving, and her fingertips—desensitized from old high-voltage flash burns—tingled with cold pinpricks in the swampy, thirty-four-degree heat.

As a girl on the kelong stilt-platforms off Pulau Tekong, she had learned that the sea never gave you time to deliberate. When a sudden squall caught her father’s shrimp traps against the pilings, you didn’t measure the height of the waves; you dove beneath the chop where the water was quiet and worked until your lungs felt like crushed paper. She drew in a long breath through her nose, biting the inside of her cheek to still the involuntary tremor in her jaw, and plunged into the access hatch of Berth 8.

The air in the subterranean bunker hit her like a wet towel soaked in vinegar. 

Here, beneath three meters of reinforced slag concrete, sat the salvaged heart of the drydock: forty tons of lithium-iron-phosphate battery cells, hauled out of the engine compartments of two decommissioned hybrid harbor tugs. They were racked in floor-to-ceiling steel cages, bolted to the damp foundation. The cells were silent, cold, and desperately empty. On the master monitoring board, the busbar indicators flickered amber-red. They had five minutes of reserve juice left to feed the cyan laser link on the crane tower. When that died, the optical line to the sister docks in Batam would sever, blinding the entire southern network.

Across the vaulted chamber, the twin drive-shafts from the deep-channel tidal turbines penetrated the seawall through heavy mechanical stuffing boxes. 

The shafts were spinning now, turning with a low, bone-jarring groan. The outward rush of Tan’s ballast release, combined with the fierce four-knot tidal shift through the Malacca strait, had caught the twin submerged hydrofoils anchored in the trench. The massive bronze gears screamed inside their oil baths, shedding heat into the damp chamber.

“Aisyah!” Chen Rui’s voice crackled through the low-frequency wall-mounted intercom, distorted by the concrete mass above. “The offshore current is spiking! The turbines are generating, but the synchronizer is hunting—it can’t find the phase!”

“Because the contacts are salt-bridged!” she yelled back into the horn, pulling an insulated nylon scraper from her tool belt. 

She ran toward the high-voltage coupling bay at the far end of the rack. The tropical humidity here was near saturation; moisture condensed on the cool copper busbars, and the sea air had deposited a thin, translucent crust of white salt across the ceramic insulator posts. If she engaged the mechanical contactors with that crust intact, forty thousand amperes of raw three-phase power would arc across the gap in a ball of superheated plasma that would vaporize the switchgear and turn the bunker into an oven.

Through the concrete ceiling, the muffled sound of hydraulic cutters echoed down. The second Apex skiff had landed a squad on the quay. They were cutting through the distribution padlock chains, enforcing the admiralty impoundment with industrial tools. They weren't monsters—they were bailiffs with tactical armor, executing a foreclosure lien for Euro-Asian Maritime Finance, convinced that clearing this yard was the only thing preventing catastrophic shipping collisions in the strait.

They had three minutes before the private contractors secured the master breaker above ground.

Aisyah dropped to her knees before the coupling rack. The air smelled sharply of ozone and rotting kelp. 

She sprayed denatured alcohol across the insulator posts, the solvent hissing against the warm metal, and attacked the salt crust with the nylon scraper. Flakes of gray-white brine showered down onto her rubber apron. Her forearms cramped, the tendons tightening like violin strings. *Scrape. Wipe. Check.* Her left hand slipped on the grease, her bare wrist brushing within an inch of an exposed ground strap. The hair on her arm stood straight up in the electrostatic field.

“Rui!” she barked toward the wall speaker. “Turbine RPM?”

“One hundred twenty at the shaft! The hydraulic pitch is locked at maximum angle!” Rui’s voice was strained, breathless. “Aisyah, the contractors are on the roof of the substation! They’re looking for the manual cutouts!”

“Tell Jin to run the seawater monitors!”

“The pumps don’t have the volts!” Rui shouted back. “The tug batteries are flat!”

“They will in ten seconds!”

She threw the scraper aside. The ceramic posts were clean, wet with evaporating alcohol. But the mechanical interlock—the heavy, cast-iron lever that slammed the turbine alternator onto the battery-charging rectifier bank—was held back by a magnetic safety latch that refused to drop. The automated sync-check was dead, fried by the lightning storm an hour earlier. 

The system was waiting for a digital handshake that would never come.

The doctrine said: *Never force an out-of-phase rotor.* If the turbine frequency didn’t match the inverter’s internal timing, the torque would snap the drive-shaft like a dry bamboo stick.

Aisyah didn’t have an oscilloscope. She had her ears and the soles of her feet.

She pressed her boot heel against the base plate of the turbine shaft housing. Through the rubber and the bone of her leg, she felt the fundamental vibration—a low, rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* that hummed in the cavity of her chest. In her mind, she matched it against the faint, sixty-cycle whistle of the inverter’s standby pilot coils.

She closed her eyes. Pulau Tekong. Midnight on the kelong. The rhythm of the tide slapping the crossbeams.

The shaft vibration accelerated, rising half a tone as a fresh surge of deep-channel water tore through the strait. *Now.*

Aisyah grabbed the emergency bypass hammer hanging from the bulkhead, swung, and smashed the mechanical trip-latch.

The heavy copper busbars dropped.

The contactors slammed shut with the force of a naval cannon, the mechanical impact rattling the teeth in her jaw. A blinding violet flash lit the vault, searing the shadow of the steel cages into the concrete wall. The smell of scorched air hit her throat, hot and metallic.

For half a second, the massive drive-shaft shrieked, its bronze couplings groaning under an instantaneous thermodynamic load as forty tons of chemical cells resisted the kinetic surge. The steel racks shivered. Then, the sound shifted from a scream to a deep, resonant, oceanic roar.

On the master board, the indicators didn’t flicker. They locked into a brilliant, steady emerald green.

Twelve megawatt-hours of reclaimed storage snapped into the line, absorbing the mechanical fury of the Malacca tide.

Above her head, through thirty feet of wet stone and rain, Berth 7 came alive.

The giant high-pressure fire monitors—salvaged from an offshore rescue tug—received full industrial power. At the caisson pier, the brass nozzles swung mechanically on their hydraulic mounts, locking onto the slipway. Twin solid jets of seawater, two thousand five hundred gallons a minute at one hundred fifty pounds per square inch, tore into the night. 

The blast struck the pier with hydraulic hammer-force. It didn’t kill. It didn’t break bones. But it washed forty yards of concrete completely clean, swamping the Apex cutting teams, blowing their hydraulic power units off the lip into the harbor, and pinning their tactical shields against the rusted plate of the caisson. The boarding ladders tore loose, clattering into the foam.

Overhead, the gantry crane’s floodlights blazed into existence, sweeping away the darkness, while the cyan laser beam atop the sixty-five-meter boom doubled in intensity, burning through the driving sheets of monsoon rain like a solid lance of blue glass.

Aisyah leaned her forehead against the cool steel mesh of the battery cage, her chest heaving, the bitter taste of ozone coating her tongue. 

Then the speaker on the wall didn't bring cheers. It clicked, and Lin Zhang’s voice came through, trembling with a panic that stripped all the blood from the victory:

*“Aisyah. Jin. Look at the radar plot on Berth 11. It wasn’t an impoundment team. The skiffs were just a distraction.”*

Aisyah pushed herself off the cage, her legs shaking as she climbed back toward the surface hatch. 

She shoved the heavy steel lid open into the monsoon downpour and wiped the rain from her eyes.

Out past the breakwater, beyond the reach of the fire monitors, a massive, unlit commercial crane barge was creeping into the outer anchorage under cover of the storm. Its heavy steel gantry was already lowering a multi-ton industrial trenching claw directly over the international shipping lane—aimed straight down at the unarmored seabed trench where the southern trans-strait optical cable entered the Batam trench. 

They weren’t trying to seize the drydock. 

They were going to sever the entire region from the continental mesh before the tide turned.
