The red strobe on the outer shoals did not blink with the lazy cadence of a channel buoy. It stabbed through the squall in sharp, mechanical pulses—four beats, a split-second fracture of darkness, then four beats again.

Jin Wei gripped the cold composite coaming of the sea-glider’s hatch. The vibration of the twin hull beneath his boots was barely a whisper compared to the violent thrum of the diesel freighters he had captained for thirty years; it felt like standing on the back of a diving ray. To his left, nestled against the gunwale, Lin Zhang sat with her knees drawn tight to her ribs, her satchel pressed against her sternum. Her thumbs moved compulsively against the frayed hem of her canvas coat, smoothing the threadbare cotton the way her mother used to level raw millet in the measuring cup before the winter rations were set.

"Hydrofoil," Aisyah said from the helm console. Her voice had the flat, spent calm of a diver who had stayed down ten minutes past the gauge’s red line. Her burned fingertips hovered above the magnetic rudder toggles. "Kevlar-sheathed hull. Deep draft when she’s idling, but once those foils bite, she’ll do forty-five knots through the rip. She isn't signaling Batam harbor control."

Down on Berth 9, forty feet above them, the iron decking clanged. Uncle Tan appeared at the rail, leaning heavily on a scarred teak walking stick he had turned himself from an old hatch cover. The monsoon damp had settled deep into the swelling of his knuckles, twisting his right hand into a claw, but his shoulders were squared against the driving spray. Beside him, Chen Rui was already shouting down into the shadows of the drydock trench, his forearms bound in stiff athletic tape to brace the burning ache in his tendons.

"Hold the release cables!" Chen Rui yelled through cupped hands. "She’s cutting across the southern bank to pin the glider in the slipway!"

The approaching vessel cleared the reef point. Even through the rain, her silhouette was unmistakable: a private enforcement interceptor, stripped of national insignia, bearing only the dull matte-gray coat of the receiver syndicates. Her twin searchlights flicked on together, carving two searing cones of white light through the rain that instantly turned the harbor water to boiling milk.

Over the slipway’s open hail-speaker, a voice crackled through the static—dry, reedy, stripped of all maritime inflection by a corporate satellite vocoder.

*"Vessel departing Berth Nine, you are in breach of maritime lien foreclosure order seven-seven-nine. The Keppel-Batam yard and all associated low-carbon auxiliary craft are under sovereign receivership. Cut your propulsion immediately and make fast your headspring. Any craft entering the channel without registered clearance will be seized under admiralty embargo."*

Jin Wei did not reach for a horn. He reached into his pocket, his fingers finding the small, dried clove he had carried since his first deckhand voyage out of Surabaya—shriveled, fragrant, a brittle reminder of soil while salt tried to drown everything. He rubbed it between thumb and forefinger, feeling the sharp ridge of the stem.

"They aren't firing," Lin Zhang whispered. Her eyes were wide, tracking the blinding beams as they swept across the glider’s canopy. "Why aren't they firing?"

"Because an iron lien requires intact collateral to sell at auction," Jin Wei muttered, climbing down into the cockpit well. "And because dead men don't sign over harbor concessions."

Up on the gantry bridge, Uncle Tan didn't answer through the radio. Instead, he pulled three long, brass-weighted chains that hung from the crane’s lower gantry deck.

Far above the berth, the tidal storage array groaned. It was not the sound of weaponry, but of counterweights shifting in their greased steel guides. Two massive ballast wedges—sixteen-ton blocks of poured iron slag recovered from the bottom of Drydock 4—dropped six feet through the vertical guide-chutes on either side of the slipway mouth. They hit the harbor silt with a subterranean thud that shuddered through the sea-glider’s hull, sending a four-foot surge of displaced water boiling out into the fairway.

The incoming hydrofoil’s bow dipped violently as the displacement wave caught her under her forward foils. Her starboard wing grazed the submerged timber of the old breakwater, throwing up a rooster-tail of yellow foam and rotten piling splinters. Her hull dropped off its lift, slamming into the chop with a concussive bang that shook loose her deck lights.

*"Berth Nine!"* the receiver’s voice barked again, losing its synthetic composure, a ragged human fear bleeding through the speaker. *"You have obstructed an authorized maritime officer! We have a financial injunction backed by the regional admiralty court!"*

Uncle Tan lifted the brass harbor horn from its cradle on the pierhead. He didn't invoke an assembly vote. He didn't cite articles of law. His voice rolled down across the water, hoarse and thick with the tobacco smoke of five decades on the Singapore roads.

"This water isn't listed on your mortgage!" the old man shouted into the storm. "Berth Seven is discharging four thousand tons of rice from the Mekong! Berth Ten is taking on fresh drinking water from the island cisterns! Every vessel in this roadstead has paid its passage in battery hours, drydock shifts, and wheat sacks! The ledger is carved into the slipway stone, boy! You have no debt to collect here, because nobody owes you a single brass coin!"

Out across the anchorage, where eighty blacked-out freighters lay swinging on their chains, the response did not come in words.

The *Minerva Star* hit her deck floods—ten thousand lumens of amber work-light illuminating her towering orange freeboard. Two seconds later, an Indonesian bulk carrier three hundred yards to the north clicked on her cargo cluster lights, bathing the channel in brilliant white. Then a Vietnamese timber freighter. Then a rusty coastal tanker from Kelantan.

Within forty seconds, eighty commercial hulls lit their working decks simultaneously. The sudden, overwhelming glare turned the midnight rain into a shimmering curtain of gold and diamond. The crew of the hydrofoil, perched high on their flybridge behind tinted composite windscreens, were suddenly blinded by three million candlepower of unvarnished maritime labor reflecting off the swells.

The hydrofoil’s twin water-jets reversed with a shuddering scream of aerated water. The vessel slewed sideways, her helmsman desperately shielding his eyes as the sheer mass of eighty steel hulls loomed over him in every quadrant—not an armada of war, but an unyielding forest of iron that refused to yield right-of-way to a paper writ.

"Aisyah," Jin Wei said quietly. "Now."

Aisyah shoved the twin throttles forward. The electric glider did not roar; it lunged. The twin carbon-fiber foils bit into the outgoing tide, lifting the main hull four feet above the roiling surface. The craft hissed through the narrow gap between the submerged ballast blocks, throwing sheets of spray to port and starboard that caught the glare of the freighters’ floodlights like sheets of hammered brass.

Lin Zhang looked back through the rear canopy.

On the pier, Uncle Tan was already turning away from the railing, his walking stick tapping rhythmically against the iron plates as he headed toward the harbormaster’s shanty where thirty cold, wet mariners stood waiting with chalk slates to register their morning offloading times. Chen Rui was by the freshwater manifolds, directing the high-pressure hoses that were feeding three thousand gallons of desalted water into the forward tanks of a grain barge from Samarinda. There were no brokers. No currency desks. No satellite clearinghouse pinging London or Singapore to shave three percent off the sweat of the men heaving the mooring lines. Just the steady, rhythmic clang of iron against iron, the hiss of steam bleeding off the tidal turbine accumulators, and the smell of boiled tea drifting from the mess shack.

The hydrofoil lay dead in the water behind them, her searchlights drowned out by the glare of the working fleet, her commander’s frantic radio calls disappearing unanswered into the roar of the monsoon. The market had not fallen to an assault; it had simply ceased to exist the moment the men who turned the winches stopped believing in the paper that claimed to own the wind.

The glider banked hard to the southwest, cutting across the deep trench where the Malacca current ran strongest. The lights of Batam receded into a low, smoldering ribbon of amber, then vanished entirely beneath the black curve of the open sea.

Lin Zhang opened her satchel. She reached past the encrypted drive to pull out a small, flat brass plate—the sortition badge she had drawn three nights ago in the machine shop. It was blank, save for three notches filed into its top edge by Chen Rui’s file.

"The passage through the Sunda Strait is clear," Aisyah said, her eyes fixed on the passive sonar display. "Twelve hours to open water. But the satellite receiver just caught an optical burst from the high tower before we lost the horizon."

Jin Wei leaned over the console. "Geneva?"

"Not the city," Aisyah said, her voice dropping. "The lake. The cantonal police have deployed floating booms across the harbor mouth at Ouchy. The Swiss sortition delegates are barricaded inside the Palais des Nations, and the clearinghouse syndicates have frozen the food delivery logistics for the entire district. If we arrive without regional standing, we aren't walking into a council."

She looked back at the two of them, the blue light of the depth sounder painting the hollows of her cheekbones.

"We're walking into a siege."
