The blue-white strobe below pulsed five times, sharp as a needle prick against the oily slick of Berth 9. 

Jin did not shout. Shouting was wasted breath in the teeth of a Malacca squall. He slapped the brass intercom horn bolted to the crane cab’s bulkhead. 

"Berth Nine," he rasped, his voice tearing against the dry ache in his throat. "Doorway. Cut the signal."

Two floors down, in the dripping shadow of the gantry legs, two figures moved. Uncle Tan didn’t run—his knees, swollen with fluid that flared whenever the barometric pressure plunged, would not allow it. Instead, he stepped out of the maintenance bay with a lead-weighted mooring line coiled over his forearm. Beside him, Aisyah moved with the flat, economical glide of a diver whose lungs were still adjusting to surface air. Her dive harness dripped sea water over her coveralls, her fingertips raw and peeling from six straight hours in the tidal pens.

Out in the black water, three hundred yards beyond the caisson gate, the salvage tug answered the signal. 

Its heavy stern dipped as twin screws bit the incoming current. The subsea trenching cutter, suspended from the aft gantry like an iron carcass, swung into the surge. The hoist winch groaned—a low, mechanical shriek that vibrated through the shipyard’s rusted pilings. The four counter-rotating blades were not turning yet, but their weight alone pulled the tug’s stern low into the chop. If those teeth reached the channel bottom, sixty feet down in the silt, the armored power umbilical anchoring Node 4 to the seabed would be severed in a single dragging sweep.

"Winch cable at thirty meters," Lin said. Her voice had dropped to a brittle monotone, the flat register she used when her migraine threatened to split her skull behind her left eye. She didn't look at the water. Her hands were clamped onto the optical transceiver’s azimuth collar, fighting the violent sway of the tower as a sixty-knot gust hit the crane structure. 

The glass lens of the receiver was fogging. Tropical humidity was creeping past the rubber gaskets, blooming into milky halos across the cyan collimator. 

"Link clarity down to eight percent," she whispered. Her thumb rubbed obsessively against the knuckle of her forefinger—a nervous habit developed during her postgraduate years in air-gapped server vaults, feeling for skin breaks to keep herself anchored against panic. "The packet buffer is dumping. If the alignment slips another millimeter, Batam drops offline. The ledger locks."

"Rui!" Jin barked into the brass horn. "The flood pumps!"

Down on the fitting pier, Chen Rui didn't answer with words. He threw his weight against the manual bypass spindle of Berth 8’s high-pressure dewatering monitor. The steel wheel bit into the palms of his hands, inflaming the tendonitis that ran like hot wire from his wrists to his elbows. He braced his hip against the riser pipe and kicked the mechanical release pawl.

A deafening hiss swallowed the roar of the rain. 

Two thousand gallons of seawater per minute erupted from the monitor’s brass nozzle, transformed into a wall of pressurized white spray that hammered across the narrow cut toward the tug’s stern. It wasn't artillery; it didn't pierce the steel hull. But the sheer volume of water slammed into the tug’s working deck like a solid wall, swamping the aft deckhands and blinding the wheelhouse windows with sheets of foaming churn. The tug’s stern swung wildly off its bearing, caught between the hydraulic shove of the monitor and the four-knot rip of the falling tide.

Inside Berth 9, the blue-white strobe flickered, stuttered, and died.

Aisyah had closed the distance without a sound. When the silhouette in the doorway turned to bolt toward the scrap piles, she didn't tackle him. She simply jammed a heavy wooden wedge under the steel roller shutter and yanked the emergency pull-chain. The iron curtain fell like a guillotine, sealing the exit. Uncle Tan stepped into the dim light of the auxiliary lanterns, his breathing heavy, his oilskin coat dripping.

The man cornered against the tool cage was not a private security contractor in tactical nylon. 

He was an assistant machinist from the dawn shift—a kid barely twenty, his face white under engine grease, holding a pocket-sized industrial alignment lamp. His hands shook so violently the lamp clattered against the corrugated iron siding.

"My sister," the boy choked out, his voice cracking on the syllable. He held up a soaked slip of thermal paper, limp as seaweed. "The maritime clearinghouse in Johor. They flagged her identity registry. Locked her insulin credit at the district depot because I was logged on this dock’s muster sheet. They sent the notice to my handset an hour ago. They said if I gave the cutter the trench line, they’d clear her ledger by midnight."

Tan stared at the kid. The old mariner’s hands, gnarled and stiffened by five decades of salt spray and rusted cable, slowly unwrapped the lead-weighted line from his arm. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't look for a weapon. He took the thermal slip from the boy’s shaking fingers, crumpled it, and shoved it deep into his own pocket.

"You pump grease on Berth Seven," Tan said softly, every word heavy with the fatigue of a man who had seen four flags change over this strait. "The boy who works the grease pump doesn't sell the ground his brothers stand on. Sit on that crate. Keep your mouth shut. When the sun comes up, we convene the three-man panel. Not before."

He locked the cage latch with a mechanical pin, turned his back, and keyed his radio twice. 

"Berth Nine secured," Tan reported. "The signal is gone."

Up in the crane cab, the victory lasted less than three seconds.

"The tug is falling back on its secondary tow line," Jin said, his binoculars locked on the foaming wash. "They don't need the ground strobe. They’ve already got the trench depth on their depth-sounder. The cutter is ten feet off the bottom."

"The laser is dead," Lin said. Her voice was barely a breath. 

On the transceiver console, the tracking light went from flickering amber to cold, dead dark. The rain had turned into a solid curtain of vertical water, scattering the cyan beam into useless refraction across the strait. On the monitor, the block-synchronization counter froze. Four thousand mutual transactions from the archipelagic fishing fleets, waiting for ratification by the Swiss node, hung suspended in volatile memory. 

"We lost line-of-sight," Lin said, her hands dropping from the collar. The cold realization hollowed her cheeks. "Without the optical bridge, we're an island. In twelve minutes, the automated registry protocols will declare Node 4 unverified. Aegis takes default receivership of the yard under the emergency salvage charter."

Jin lowered his glasses. His back throbbed with a dull, sickening grind where the lower vertebrae rubbed raw. He reached out and tapped the brass frame of the cab's storm compass three times—an unconscious rhythm learned forty years ago on a coal-fired lighter off the Natuna shoals. 

"Look east," he said.

Lin didn't move. "Jin, the physics don't care about—"

"Look east, Doctor."

Through the salt-streaked glass, past the outer channel markers where the commercial shipping lane carved its deep highway through the strait, dark silhouettes were emerging from the squall. 

They were not tactical patrol vessels. They were too broad, too low in the water, their superstructures caked in the rust of secondary feeder routes and third-tier registry ports. A three-thousand-ton modular container carrier, its deck stacked three-high with weathered red and blue steel boxes. Behind it, a low-slung bulk barge pushed by a twin-screw river tug. Then another. And a fourth.

Nine vessels in loose, staggered line-abreast, cutting directly across the inward-bound lane of the Singapore Strait traffic separation scheme.

"They're off-course," Lin whispered, squinting through the wiper blade’s arc. "They’re running straight into the shallows. Why haven't port control scrambled the harbor launches?"

"Because port control can't see them," Jin said. A thin, grim smile touched the corner of his cracked lips. 

On the cab's marine radar repeater, the sea lanes ahead of the yard were blank. Every single one of the approaching vessels had cut its satellite transponder. Their automated identification transmitters were cold. To the automated vessel tracking algorithms in the high-rise tower on the mainland, the nine ships simply did not exist; they were ghost clutter, lost in the heavy sea-clutter returns of the tropical storm.

Then, from the monkey island atop the lead feeder ship's bridge, a needle of brilliant blue light punched through the rain.

It was an optical transceiver, jury-rigged to the radar mast. 

A second needle flashed from the tug behind it. Then a third, firing cyan and infrared pulses in a tight, multi-point web across the water. The lead vessel didn't stop in the channel; it swung its blunt bow directly between the advancing salvage tug and the tidal turbine pens, placing sixty thousand tons of displacement and floating steel like an immovable granite wall over the subsea cable trench.

The salvage tug’s siren sounded—a single, desperate blast of reverse propulsion as its captain realized what was bearing down on him through the sheets of monsoon spray. The trenching cutter's hoist cable went slack as the tug threw its engines full astern to keep from being ground into kindling against the feeder’s massive bulbous bow.

Up on the crane gantry, Lin’s console didn't just flicker. It ignited.

The light cascading through the cab wasn't a single beam. It was a mesh. The nine independent freighters, chained together by line-of-sight laser transceivers spaced only four hundred yards apart, had cut straight through the squall’s optical attenuation. What a single six-mile beam across the open strait could not pierce, nine short-hop relays punched through with blinding, luminous clarity.

The tracking LED on the bulkhead flared to an unyielding, steady green.

*Incoming synchronization stream: 100% link continuity.*

The block counter began to spin, numbers cascading down the screen faster than human eyes could track, locking the mutual registries of ninety-four independent ports into the distributed trust ledger before the salvage tug's master could even reach for his emergency radio.

Jin stepped back from the window, his breath coming slow and ragged in the close heat of the crane cab. He reached down and clicked the yard-wide intercom off. 

Out in the strait, three more low whistles sounded through the rain—unregistered, unmonitored, and completely sovereign. The counter-siege was no longer holding the dock. 

It held the water.
