The channel bottom was dying in silence.

Out past the shoals, the three blackened tugs idled under twin screws, their stern winches screaming as steel drag-cables bit into forty fathoms of black silt. Jin didn’t need a hydrophone to know what was happening beneath the dark chop. The heavy four-pronged grapnels were dragging the trench floor like iron claws through muscle, trawling blind for the armoring of the seabed optical pipe. When that composite casing hooked, the shear stress would peak, the glass strands inside would shatter into hair-fine powder, and the shipyard’s low-latency window to Batam would vanish before dawn.

“Let them drag,” Jin rasped into the radio, pressing his forearm against his bruised ribs. Every breath tasted of scorched iron and marsh diesel. “Lin, let the sea floor go.”

“Are you out of your mind?” Lin Zhang’s voice crackled through the handset, shrill, frayed by two days without sleep. Behind her, through the radio’s open mic, came the chaotic din of children weeping and the metallic clanking of mess tins. “That trunk carries the bilateral ledgers! If Apex cuts that link, the clearinghouse in Johor flags us as dead water! The receivers will board under salvage rights within forty-eight hours!”

“We have the light tower,” Jin said, spitting blood into the scupper. He nodded toward the gantry’s upper crown, where the cyan optical laser housing sat beneath its rain hood sixty-five meters above the spray. “The glass under the mud was always borrowed time. Get down to the dock basin. Tan is bringing the crews through the culverts now.”

He didn’t wait for an answer. He limped along the crane catwalk, his spine grinding like ungreased gears. Behind him on the sealed causeway, the Apex transports sat with their engines rumbling, their red brake lamps pooling in the diesel-slick rainwater. They were stopped cold by twelve tons of dropped gantry steel, but the men inside were professionals on salaried standby; they would not turn around. They would wait for the cutting torches, or for hunger to do the work for them.

Down in Drydock 4, hunger was already five days old.

The dock was a sunken world of weeping concrete, three hundred yards long and fifty feet deep, built fifty years ago for supertankers that no longer existed. When the pumps were sealed, it became a cavernous stone trench, sheltered from the channel winds by thirty-foot sea-gates.

Uncle Tan stood at the head of the access gangway, leaning his bulk heavily on an oily length of galvanized scaffold pipe that served as his cane. At sixty-eight, his knuckles were swollen like ginger roots from four decades of grease and monsoon damp, his skin leathery and cured by sea salt. He didn’t wear a yellow safety helmet or an orange vest; he wore a faded batik shirt unbuttoned over a singlet and rubber boots slit down the calves to accommodate his swollen ankles.

Before him, emerging from the dark pedestrian conduit beneath the berths, filed the ghost fleet: four hundred men, women, and infants who had spent six weeks trapped on container hulls anchored in the outer roads—Indonesian, Filipino, Vietnamese, and Burmese mariners whose shipping line had dissolved into bankruptcy while their vessels were still mid-transit. Abandoned by the charterers, denied shore leave by the port authority, their fuel exhausted, their drinking water fouled by algal blooms, they moved with the slow, hollow-eyed stupor of people who had forgotten the feel of motionless earth beneath their shoes.

“Easy on the steps,” Tan called out, his voice a low, gravelly baritone that seemed to rise from his diaphragm rather than his throat, cutting clean through the drumming rain. He didn’t shout. Shouting invited panic, and panic in a concrete trench was lethal. “Watch the lime on the second landing. It’s slick as squid ink. Women and the young ones to the keel blocks on the port side. We’ve got dry tarpaulins laid out under the overhang.”

A young deckhand from an arrested bulk carrier staggered past, carrying a child whose fevered head lolled against his collarbone. The boy was shivering violently under a sodden fleece jacket.

Tan caught the man gently by the elbow. He didn’t offer a prayer, and he didn’t offer an apology. With his free hand, he reached into his deep smock pocket, pulled out a warm, foil-wrapped wedge of steamed cassava, and pressed it into the deckhand’s palm.

“Boiler three has fresh steam cycling through the copper coils under Berth 8,” Tan said softly, looking straight into the man’s red-rimmed eyes until the deckhand’s breathing slowed. “Go sit on the planking there. The wood is hot. Put your back against it. Your boy won’t cough once his chest warms.”

The deckhand swallowed, his throat bobbing, and nodded once before hurrying toward the orange glow flickering along the dock floor.

Jin dropped from the steel access ladder onto the dock floor, his knees buckling under his weight. Chen Rui followed two rungs behind, his hands trembling so hard he nearly missed the final rung. Aisyah descended last, dropping lightly onto the sump drain, her dark hair plastered to her skull by rain, her diving knife still strapped to her calf.

“The trench cable is gone,” Jin told Tan without preamble. “Apex is trawling the mud with drag-chains. In twenty minutes, the channel fiber will be sheared.”

Tan didn’t blink. He shifted his weight off his bad hip, his eyes sweeping the cavern of the dock. Across the vast floor, families were already huddling beneath massive ten-ton timber blocks once used to cradle ship keels. Between the blocks, crews had slung orange tarp roofs, anchoring the corners to the bronze mooring rings embedded in the dock floor. A mobile battery bank, hauled on a flatbed handcart from the harbor tugs, hummed with a deep, liquid vibration, feeding power through heavy rubber-jacketed cables to a pair of reverse-osmosis desalination filters.

The smell was a sharp mix of wet concrete, brackish mud, and the faint, sweet steam of boiling rice coming from an old oil drum turned into a communal kettle.

“A cable in the dirt belongs to whoever owns the dirt,” Tan said, adjusting his grip on the pipe cane. “The sky is different. Is the crane laser aligned with Batam?”

“Not yet,” Chen Rui said, his voice cracking. He held out his arms; the muscles were twitching in involuntary spastic ripples from twelve hours of hauling counterweights. “The optical head is shuddering in this squall. Every gust knocks the beam three milliradians off the transponder across the strait. We don’t have an active tracking gimbal. The bearings rusted out when the yard closed.”

“Then we track it by hand,” Aisyah said flatly. Her fingertips were white, scorched by a high-voltage backfeed during the gatehouse scramble. She didn't look at her injuries; she looked south toward the channel mouth. “I’ll climb the crane truss. With a turnbuckle and two guide lines, I can hold the sightline on the Batam relay tower within half an inch.”

“In forty-knot gusts?” Chen Rui stared at her. “You’ll be blown off the boom!”

“Then tie me to the frame,” she said, her voice dropping into that chilling, quiet cadence that ended arguments.

Before Chen Rui could protest, the concrete beneath their boots shuddered.

It was not the wind. It was a rhythmic, thudding percussion—the unmistakable hydro-acoustic slap of twin high-speed diesel skiffs coming over the dock sill at thirty knots.

Lin Zhang rushed down the final flight of iron stairs, her spectacles smudged with rain and engine soot, clutching a ruggedized hand tablet wrapped in a clear freezer bag.

“Two Apex fast-interceptors just cleared the outer breakwater!” she shouted over the wind. “They aren’t waiting for the morning tide! They’re entering the wet dock slipway—they’re trying to seize the outer caisson gate before we can seal the sea-locks!”

The mariners under the keel blocks heard. The murmur of four hundred exhausted people died in an instant. A woman pulled her infant tighter against her chest; men stood up from the steam coils, their hands instinctively reaching for scrap iron, pipe fittings, rusted wrenches.

Tan raised his cane—just three inches off the floor—and struck the iron edge of a mooring cleat.

*CLANG.*

The single, resonant ring echoed off the towering concrete walls like a cathedral bell. The mariners froze, their eyes locking onto the old dockmaster.

“No iron in our hands,” Tan said, his voice dropping into that deep, immovable bedrock register that demanded absolute silence. “Under the Covenant, this dock is an open sanctuary. If an Apex man steps on this stone carrying a rifle, he is a pirate under the Law of the Sea. If we pick up a pipe, we are an unlawful militia, and the receivers will turn the gunboats loose on our women. Put the wrenches down.”

A silence followed, heavy and suffocating, punctuated only by the hammering squall. Slowly, two dozen deckhands dropped their rusted bars back into the gravel.

“Aisyah,” Jin said, turning his back on the crowd. His engine-room cough caught him, tearing at his lungs until he spit black into the mud. “Take the upper boom. Lock the cyan optical link to Batam. Don’t let that beam drop, no matter what happens on the water.”

She was already gone, her boots slapping the wet iron rungs of the gantry ladder, climbing straight up into the howling dark.

“Jin,” Tan murmured, turning toward the seaward end of the dry dock where the massive, hollow-steel caisson gate stood open to the incoming surge. “The high-pressure fire pumps at Berth 7. Are they primed?”

“The intake valves are open,” Jin said. “Two thousand gallons a minute at one-fifty psi. But the motors are pulling off the tug batteries. If we light them up, we burn half our overnight storage in ten minutes.”

“We won't need ten minutes,” Tan said, his eyes narrowing as two blinding white halogen spotlights swung around the breakwater, cutting through the monsoon sheets.

The first Apex interceptor emerged from the mist, its twin supercharged outboards roaring like chain-saws, a rigid-hull raider carrying six tactical security contractors in black drysuits and ballistic helmets. They were coming fast, their bow cutting a violent white furrow across the slipway, aiming directly for the caisson control platform.

On the gantry boom, sixty-five meters overhead, a pencil-thin needle of brilliant cyan light flared to life, piercing through the driving rain toward the distant silhouette of the Batam hills.

For one second, the light held steady.

Then, from the channel below, the second Apex skiff raised a high-intensity marine spotlight, blinding the crane cab, while the first craft slammed against the caisson pier with a sickening crunch of reinforced fiberglass.

A voice blared through a megaphonic hailer from the lead skiff, sharp and amplified:

*“Notice of Admiralty Impoundment! Clear the caisson walkway immediately! We are boarding under authority of Euro-Asian Maritime Finance!”*

Jin caught Tan’s arm. “They have legal cover.”

Tan didn’t flinch. He reached out with his calloused hand and gripped the brass lever of the yard’s ancient mechanical sluice release—the gravity bypass that held back eighty thousand tons of tidal ballast.

“Let them read their paper to the tide,” Tan said, and slammed the lever down.
