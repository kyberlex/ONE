The hydrofoils carved through the black swells like paired scalpels, leaving twin scars of phosphorescent foam that died against the mangrove roots. Through the rain-slicked lenses of his binoculars, Jin saw the low, matte-carbon geometry of the lead hull: an Apex Marine fast-interception skiff, stripped of running lights, riding high on its automated trim tabs. Behind it came two more, running in tight echelon, their twin supercharged outboards emitting only a muffled, rhythmic thrum that vibrated the salt-crusted glass of the crane cab.

"Three minutes to the mudflat riser," Lin said. Her voice was flat, dried out by thirty straight hours of stale air and the harsh phosphorescence of the optical terminal. She did not look up from the display. With her left hand, she pressed two fingers hard against the bridge of her nose—a habitual reflex whenever the retinal flare of the beam-tracking lasers brought on the stabbing ache behind her right temple. "They aren't pinging the commercial channel. Their forward arrays are locked on the thermal plume from the cooling manifold."

"They know exactly where the bronze busbars meet the mud," Jin rasped. A raw, wet fit of coughing caught him, rattling deep in his sternum, a parting gift from thirty years of inhaling heavy fuel oil mist in the bilge spaces of bulk carriers. He spat a bitter trace of clove into his palm, wiped his mouth on his oilskin sleeve, and hauled back on the crane’s traverse lever. "Chen. Aisyah. Are the manual bypass gates dogged down?"

The sound-powered telephone mounted to the cab bulkhead buzzed—a dry, mechanical chirp powered solely by the speaker's vocal diaphragm, impervious to the signal-dampers the approaching gunboats undoubtedly carried.

"Dogged and pinned," Aisyah’s voice crackled through the brass earpiece, breathless and edged with grit. Down on the tidal pontoon at Berth 9, sixty feet below, water was already lapping over her boots. A stray wire from the afternoon's high-voltage splice had flayed the calluses across her thumb and forefinger, the raw skin now white and puckered from seawater, but her grip on the iron spanner did not slacken. "Pumps two and four are drawing twelve hundred amps off the turbine bank. The lines are shuddering, Captain. If we open the valves wide, the backpressure will shake the deck-plates out of their welds."

"Let them shake," Jin said. "Chen Rui, what’s the head pressure?"

Across the pontoon, Chen Rui knelt in the torrential downpour, his forearms locked tight against the vibration of the four-inch distribution manifold. The tendonitis in his wrists burned like dipped acid, an old injury from his years lofting frames in the drydocks of Dalian, reawakened by six hours of torquing rusted municipal flanges. "One hundred and forty pounds per square inch," Chen shouted into the brass horn, water cascading off the brim of his hardhat into his collar. "If we trip the main monitor now, we strip the seals on the cooling jacket!"

"Hold until their bows lift," Jin ordered. "They’re running hydrofoils in five fathoms of water. They need their optical track to stay off the sandbars."

Out in the black water of the channel, Commander Marcus Vance stood braced behind the armored windscreen of the lead interceptor, the *Apex Hunter*. His hands, clad in textured neoprene, rested lightly on the fly-by-wire helm. Vance did not hate the dockers or the fishermen whose lights had just flared along the mangrove fringe. He viewed them with the grim, detached pity a civil engineer might feel for squatters building shacks inside a spillway. The global supply chain through the Singapore Strait was an arterial pump moving twenty percent of world trade; it could not tolerate an unmonitored sovereign blind spot powered by pirated subsea turbines. If the local grid took root, the clearinghouses in Zurich and Tokyo would reclassify the entire south passage as contested waters, tripling insurance premiums and stranding grain shipments in the roads.

"Target riser locked," his weapons officer murmured, eyes fixed on the thermal screen. "Forty meters off the port point. Two pneumatic grapnels ready. One high-velocity diamond-wire cutting charge."

"Take the riser clean at the mudline," Vance said, his voice measured, perfectly modulated by years of naval service. "Disable the junction box. No kinetic rounds on the pontoon. We aren't here to give the admiralty court a pile of martyrs. Drop the grid, secure the cable head, let the morning tide flood the trench."

The *Hunter* banked into the river mouth, its foils slicing through the surface chop at thirty-four knots. The bow rose, throwing two great wings of white spray that caught the ambient glare of the distant coastal lights.

High above, in the crane cab, Jin Wei let the breath out of his teeth. Decades at sea had taught him the exact weight of displacement; he knew the feel of a hull fighting the drag of shallow water, the precise instant when speed outran buoyancy.

"Aisyah," Jin whispered into the horn. "Open the river."

Down on the pontoon, Aisyah threw her full weight against the long-handled bronze butterfly valve. Chen Rui lunged beside her, his good shoulder slamming into the cast-iron wheel of the secondary bypass. Metal shrieked on metal. A slug of trapped air hammered through the five-hundred-meter run of salvaged dredge pipe, a sound like a naval gun discharging underground.

From the sunken concrete lip of the drydock sill, four industrial firefighting monitors—reclaimed from an obsolete liquefied gas carrier and fed directly by the roaring surplus of the deep-channel turbines—erupted into the dark.

Ten thousand gallons of seawater per minute hurled upward in a solid, terrifying arc, crashing together sixty feet above the mangrove flats. It was not a spray; it was a localized deluge, an impenetrable mechanical cataract that slammed down into the path of the advancing skiffs. The water did not merely fall—it atomized against the high-velocity discharge from the turbine’s cooling loops, creating a blinding, super-dense fog bank laced with boiling brine and thick, churning mist.

Inside the cockpit of the *Apex Hunter*, the thermal imaging array instantly whited out.

"Laser scatter!" the technician shouted, tearing his goggles away as the return beam from the lidar rangefinder bounced off billions of suspended saltwater droplets, blinding the sensor suite in a wash of incandescent green noise. "We lost the bottom profile! Depth sounder is echoing off the spray!"

"Manual steer!" Vance barked, his calm cracking for the first fraction of a second as the physical world reasserted itself over the instruments. He threw his weight against the helm, trying to haul the skiff to starboard, away from the roaring curtain of water.

It was three seconds too late.

The *Hunter* punched into the edge of the water curtain at thirty knots. The sheer kinetic mass of the downpour struck the carbon-fiber deckhouse like gravel dumped from a hopper. The engine air intakes gulped atomized brine, sputtered, and choked as the oxygen sensors panicked. Deprived of lift, the port hydrofoil plowed into the crest of an outgoing tidal rip. The foil caught the submerged crest of a shifting mudbank, twisted along its sheer pins, and snapped with a sharp, sickening crack that shuddered through Vance’s boots.

The skiff slewed violently forty-five degrees to port, burying its snout into the soft, unyielding mangrove silt. The two trailing interceptors, their forward thermal screens equally blinded by the wall of churning salt fog, broke formation in wild, desperate maneuvers, their throttles howling as they reversed pitch to avoid t-boning their commander’s wallowing stern.

Down on the pontoon, the cold spray rained back down over Aisyah and Chen Rui, soaking them to the marrow. The iron valve wheel was vibrating so hard under Chen's palms that the numbness traveled all the way to his collarbone, but he kept his boots wedged against the riser frame, refusing to yield an inch of pressure.

"First bird is grounded," Lin called down through the horn, her gaze locked on the optical transceiver's real-time silhouette tracking. "The other two have backed off into the deep gutter. They're circling three hundred yards out."

In the crane cab, Jin did not cheer. His hands remained resting on the cold levers, the wind-driven rain leaking through the cab's cracked rubber weatherstripping and dripping steadily onto his knuckles. His left knee, worn flat by forty years of ladder-wells, throbbed with a dull, rhythmic ache that mirrored the thrum of the turbines below.

He leaned forward, wiping a clean circle on the misted windshield with the heel of his hand.

Out in the churn of the channel, just beyond the edge of the roaring water curtain, the second skiff had killed its outboards. It wasn't retreating.

Mounted on its foredeck, a low, pneumatic canister hissed. A dark, spider-like tethered crawler, fitted with magnetic tracked treads and twin articulated grapple arms, dropped into the waist-deep surf of the mudflat. It was below the reach of the fire monitors, crawling along the sea floor, its internal gyro humming as it ground steadily forward toward the unshielded power riser.
