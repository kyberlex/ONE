The electric sea-glider did not fly so much as skip across the surface tension of the South China Sea, riding its own trapped pocket of pressurized air like a flat stone skimming black ice.

Inside the composite cabin, the noise was not the roar of combustion, but a dry, pneumatic whistle—the shriek of displaced seawater slicing beneath the ventral foils at ninety knots. Every five seconds, an ocean swell slapped the carbon underbelly with the dull, concussive thud of a muffled sledgehammer.

Captain Jin Wei wedged his shoulder into the bulkhead padding, his right hand clamped around a damp structural rib. The engine room vibrations of forty years at sea lived permanently in the cartilage of his lower spine, a dull ache that flared white-hot with every jolt of the glider. Beneath his oilskin jacket, the old chemical burn on his collarbone prickled against the stale nylon lining, irritated by a film of salt-sweat that never dried. He kept his jaw shut tight; biting one’s tongue at ninety knots meant swallowing your own blood while strapped to an experimental battery frame.

Across the narrow aisle, Dr. Lin Zhang sat hunched over a canvas lap-desk, her fingers moving across a matte slate with mechanical twitching speed. Her skin had taken on the greenish cast of unbaked dough, her pupils dilated behind grease-smudged lenses. Three nights of salt-basin siege without sleep had stripped away her clinical composure, leaving only the brittle, hyper-focused tremor of exhaustion. She smelled of scorched wiring and the bitter chicory dust she had been chewing raw to stave off collapse.

"Optical relay just dropped forty percent," Lin muttered, not looking up. Her voice was flat, frayed at the edges. "Atmospheric moisture over the Bay of Bengal is turning the beam to soup. Scattering loss."

"Keep it narrow," Jin Wei said, his throat raw from bunker fumes he hadn't breathed in two days. His thumb found an old brass button in his trouser pocket, rolling the smoothed metal over and over—a nervous habit he had hid from his deckhands for thirty years. "If the Batam laser loses our tail before we clear the Andaman acoustic gates, we’re blind to the western corridors."

Lin didn't answer. She tapped a mechanical copper interlock on her slate, manually adjusting the roof-mounted collimator by micro-increments. Outside, through the blister canopy, the sea was an ink-black void streaked with phosphorescent foam, but through Lin’s air-gapped terminal, the invisible geography of the planet appeared as a skeletal web of light and shadow.

A tiny green diode pulse signaled a clean packet.

"Detroit’s through," she breathed, her shoulders dropping an inch. "Toby routed via the salt-mine thermal tap. Low-bandwidth burst. Three lines."

Jin Wei leaned over, wincing as his lumbar vertebrae protested the motion. On the phosphor screen, the plain text flickered in amber phosphor:

*FOUNDRY COLD-DRAWN COILS RATED TO SIX HUNDRED VOLTS. RAILS THROUGH TOLEDO HEAVILY MONITORED BY PRIVATE RAIL POLICE. WE ARE MOVING GRAIN AND COPPER AT MIDNIGHT BY TRACTOR FLOTILLA. HOLD THE LINE IN THE PASS.*

"Kids are moving iron in the dark," Jin Wei murmured. A strange, sharp pride cut through his nausea. "No trains. They’re using the old tow-paths."

"They have to," Lin said grimly. "Chicago clearinghouse froze every municipal transit credit five hours ago. Toby says the grain elevators in Michigan are running on local barter chits stamped from scrap tin. If Geneva folds, those chits become kindling."

The glider lurched violently to port as the automated terrain-following sonar detected a half-submerged cargo container bobbing in the shipping lane. The twin contra-rotating electric props wailed, biting thin air before settling back into the brine with a heavy, shuddering splash that soaked the forward windshield in bioluminescent slime.

Before Jin Wei could steady his balance, a second pulse flickered onto the slate. A colder, crisper cadence.

"Turin," Lin whispered, her knuckles whitening against the frame.

The Italian link had bounced across three independent mountain repeaters, riding the catenary wires of abandoned alpine funiculars before dropping to the coastal maritime mesh:

*DORA BASIN UNDER ARMED PROVINCIAL CORDON. HYDRAULIC VALVE SEALS SURROUNDED BY CARABINIERI ADVISORS. WE HAVE SHUT OFF THE RIVER GATES FROM BELOW GROUND. NO WATER REACHES THE PRIVATE COOLING ARRAYS UNTIL THE SORTITION DELEGATION LEAVES FOR THE LAKE. BEATRICE IS WALKING THE MONCENISIO TRAIL ON FOOT.*

"On foot," Jin Wei rasped. He could see the girl in his mind—the thin-lipped apprentice from the rail yards, carrying the printed ledgers through high snow while private security patrols combed the mountain highways with thermal cameras. "She’ll freeze if the gale catches her on the ridge."

"She knows the paths," Lin said, though her voice lacked conviction. Her eyes twitched toward the glider’s internal reserve indicator. "Jin. Look at the pack."

Jin Wei turned toward the forward console. The bank of salvaged lithium-iron-phosphate cells—plucked from the guts of an impounded Batam tugboat—was glowing dull crimson under the cooling manifold. Salt crust was already forming around the secondary intake valves, choking the passive heat exchangers.

"Temperature is climbing four degrees every ten miles," Jin said, reading the physical needle gauges. "We’re dragging too much spray into the vents."

"If we drop altitude to shed the spray, the surface drag increases fifteen percent," Lin countered, her voice sharpening with real panic. "We run dry six hundred miles east of the Gulf of Aqaba. We don't have the juice to reach the Red Sea corridor, Jin. We’ll be dead in the water off Socotra, sitting ducks for the admiralty cutters."

"Then we don't drop altitude," Jin Wei said quietly. "We dump ballast."

"What ballast?" Lin looked wildly around the stripped cabin. There were no passenger seats, no cargo holds, no unnecessary bulkheads—only the high-voltage bus bars, their two survival packs, and the zinc-lined transit trunk containing the physical ratified scrolls of the Malacca Covenant. "Everything on this craft was weighed down to the gram before we cleared the breakwater."

"The auxiliary cooling tank," Jin said. "Forty liters of fresh water."

Lin stared at him. "That’s our drinking reserve for the desert leg. If the overland link in Jordan fails and we have to put down—"

"If we don't clear the acoustic line in forty minutes, there is no desert leg," he cut in, his voice dropping into the flat, immovable timber of a master mariner who had grounded three bulk carriers to save thirty lives. "Open the ventral drain valve, Doctor. Let the sea have it."

Lin’s breath caught in her throat. She looked at the small plastic jerrycans strapped beneath the central console. Forty liters. In the dry heat of an inland landing, forty liters was the difference between three days of life and a swollen tongue choking a dry throat. But the thermal needle was already edging toward the yellow hash-mark.

She reached down, unpinned the safety wire, and kicked the brass dump lever.

A hollow gurgle vibrated through the floorboards as forty kilograms of fresh, clear water evacuated through the keel, swallowed instantly by the roaring foam. The glider’s bows lifted four inches. The pneumatic shriek smoothed into a continuous, high-pitched hum. The thermal needle hesitated, then crawled back three millimeters.

"We gained two knots," Lin said, her chest heaving as she slumped back into her harness.

"Small mercies," Jin muttered.

Then the slate chime rang again—a double-tone, jagged and urgent.

It was not Detroit. It was not Turin.

The signal was stamped with the cryptographic hash of the high-altitude Atacama mesh, beamed across an unmonitored salt-flat laser array and bounced off the hull of an unflagged Japanese bulk carrier crossing the Pacific.

Lin translated the binary burst manually, her pencil scratching furiously onto a scrap of oil-stained paper. When she stopped, the graphite lead snapped against the pad.

"What is it?" Jin Wei asked, his hand dropping from his button. "Did the copper miners hold the switchyard?"

Lin raised her eyes. The smudged lenses could not hide the sudden, paralyzing terror in her face.

"It’s not about the switchyard," she whispered. Her fingers trembling, she held up the scrap of paper.

*SANTIAGO EMBASSY JUST ISSUED AN ADMIRALTY RED NOTICE. APEX PRIVATE MARITIME PMC HAS DISPATCHED THREE HYDROFOIL INTERCEPTORS FROM DIEGO GARCIA. THEY ARE NOT WAITING FOR US IN THE RED SEA.*

Jin Wei’s head snapped toward the forward blister.

Out across the horizon, beyond the reach of the glider’s low-mounted headlights, the black water of the Indian Ocean lay undisturbed under a starless sky. But through the cabin’s low-frequency acoustic receiver, a new sound was bleeding through the rhythmic thrum of the swells.

Not the wind. Not the sea.

A synchronized, multi-engine whine—twin gas turbines running at maximum sprint, slicing directly across their flight vector from the northwest.

"They aren't trying to board us," Lin said, reading the Doppler frequency spiking on her audio spectrum display. Her voice was barely a breath. "They know the Hague Trust requires physical presentation of the covenants before the midnight assembly in Geneva. If we don't reach the shore..."

"Hold on," Jin Wei snarled, his weathered hands slamming down onto the manual trim toggles, overriding the autopilot.

Beneath them, the glider banked hard into the trough of a fifteen-foot wave, its carbon wing-tips slicing green fire through the dark water as the first searchlight beam from the approaching interceptors slashed across the sky ahead.
