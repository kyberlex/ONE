The diesel rumble did not come from the open strait. It crawled up from behind them, vibrating through the cracked bitumen of the coastal spit.

Jin Wei wiped seawater from his eyelashes and spun toward the landward perimeter. Across the two-lane causeway that tethered Berth 9 to the mainland industrial park, pairs of high-mounted halogen lamps cut through the driving monsoon rain. Four heavy tactical transports—low-slung, multi-axle personnel carriers bearing the stencil of Apex Marine Tactical—idled in single file at the far gatehouse. Their undercarriages hissed as hydraulic suspension systems lowered them into a stable cordon. Behind them, two private wrecker cranes with boom-mounted winches grumbled in low gear, brought in to clear barricades under the authority of the Singapore-Riau Receiver’s warrant.

They were not coming to fire on the dock. They were coming to foreclose it on dry tires, under the clean color of corporate tenancy law, while the heavy salvage cutters choked off the sea.

“Jin!” Uncle Tan came shuffling across the slipway apron, his yellow oilskin slick as seal fat. The old man held his swollen wrist tight against his chest, his knobby knuckles pale from the sudden barometric drop. “The gate wire won’t hold two tons of bumper. Some of the younger deckhands from the bulk carriers—they’ve got scrap iron bars. Pipe wrenches.”

“Take them away,” Jin rasped, his throat raw from salt mist and bunker fumes. “Get them inside the pipe shed. Bar the rolling door from the inside. If a single pipe hits a windshield, Apex logs it as an armed insurrection under the Admiralty Transit Code. That gives their cutters the right to blow the sea pumps.”

Tan searched Jin’s face, saw the absolute finality there, and nodded once. He turned, his damp boots squeaking on the oil-filmed concrete, his voice calm and steady as he coaxed the terrified crewmen backward into the shadow of the crane rails.

Jin took the steel gantry ladder three rungs at a time. Every jolt sent a needle of pain through his lumbar vertebrae, the lingering souvenir of a snapped crane harness thirty years ago off the Natuna rigs. He had never told Lin or Chen Rui why he favored his left leg on metal stairs; he kept it locked straight out of sheer habit, using the ball of his right foot to take the brunt of the climb.

Thirty feet up, the landward gantry platform hung suspended over the narrowest pinch-point of the access road. Here, between the abandoned cement silos and the high-voltage substation, the causeway narrowed to twenty feet of asphalt flanked by salt marshes.

Chen Rui was already there, kneeling in a pool of brackish rainwater beneath the drum brake of the manual counterweight hoist. His forearms were bound in blue elastic athletic wrap to keep his inflamed tendons from seizing under tension. He was furiously working an eight-pound sledge against an oxidized locking dog.

“The air actuator is dead,” Chen Rui called out, breathless, shouting over the drumming squall. “Corrosion ate the solenoid seals. If we drop it, we drop it by gravity!”

“How much weight is on the carriage?” Jin reached the staging deck, his oilskin dripping onto the tread-plate.

“Sixteen tons of scrap rail ballast inside the cradle,” Chen Rui said, leaning his entire body into the lever. The cold spray plastered his hair against his forehead. “Suspended forty feet over the choke point. If the pawl clears, the counterweight drops into the marsh cradle and the drop-frame swings down on its pivot. It blocks the road solid. But the bronze trip-pin is jammed with crystallized salt.”

Through the curtain of rain below, the lead Apex transport surged forward. Its roof-mounted searchlights snapped on together, blinding white beams washing over the rust-streaked plate of Berth 9. Over the vehicle’s public-address system, a voice came flat, amplified, and entirely devoid of malice:

*“Notice of Admiralty Impoundment and Emergency Asset Preservation. All unauthorized personnel occupying Berth 9 are in violation of Strait Transit Order 41-B. Clear the roadway. Step clear of heavy equipment. Apex Tactical contractors are executing lawful court-ordered access. Clear the roadway immediately.”*

They were three hundred yards out, moving at a cautious eight miles an hour, tires throwing rooster tails of filthy brown runoff into the reeds. They had no reason to rush. The law, the capital, and the tide were all on their side. Behind the armored grilles of the transports sat private security contractors earning hazardous-harbor pay, men with families in Jurong and Batam who believed with simple, pragmatic certainty that stranded mariners squatting on industrial docks were an intolerable hazard to the navigation of forty percent of the world’s petroleum.

“Aisyah!” Jin roared over the railing.

From the maintenance walkway six yards out along the swinging boom, a slight figure swung down by one arm from the crane’s diagonal bracing. Aisyah dropped onto the platform with the spring-heeled balance of a commercial saturation diver. Her neoprene dive jacket was torn at the shoulder, smelling of bottom mud and diesel fuel.

“Pin’s seized in the sleeve,” she said, her speech clipped, spitting rainwater from her lip. She held up an old three-foot brass drift and a cold-chisel. “The housing took a warp when the gantry was decommissioned six years ago. The brass won’t shear, but the iron collar is out of round by three millimeters.”

“Can we drift it out?” Jin asked.

“Not with a light mallet,” she said. “We need three hundred foot-pounds of dead blow on the collar face while someone slacks the secondary dog. If the dog catches on the recoil, it takes a man’s hand off.”

Jin knelt beside the iron pedestal. The mechanism was brutally simple, forged in an era when shipyard machinery was designed to survive typhoons without a computer chip in sight: an oversized rack-and-pinion catch holding back a massive structural beam weighted with rusted railroad ties. If dropped, the horizontal steel truss would bridge the causeway like a fallen drawbridge, wedging its teeth into the concrete abutments on either side. It would not crush the approaching vehicles; it would present a physical barrier of solid steel and scrap iron that no tow-rig could move without a twelve-hour demolition permit.

The lead tactical transport crossed the two-hundred-yard mark. The glare of its high-beams lit the underside of the gantry platform, throwing long, jittering shadows of Jin and Chen Rui against the cloud cover.

“Chen,” Jin said softly, his voice dropping below the roar of the wind. “Take the secondary dog with the pry bar. When I say pull, you pull until your shoulders come out of the sockets. Do not let it bounce.”

Chen Rui swallowed hard, his knuckles white against the rubber grip. He didn't offer a platitude. He braced his steel-toed boots against the deck coaming, his forearms trembling under the wrap.

“Aisyah, set the drift,” Jin ordered. He reached down and took the eight-pound sledge from the decking. The hickory handle was dark with machine oil, the head chipped from thirty years of unrecorded strikes.

Jin adjusted his stance. His lower back fired a white-hot spike of agony down his left hip—that familiar, crushing pressure where the disc had worn away against the sacrum. For a split second, the rain blurred, and the iron platform beneath him felt as narrow and fragile as the catwalk of a tanker engine room during a black-start. He thought of his bunk in the lower quarters: the small, unglazed porcelain cup his daughter had molded in primary school, still sitting on his steel locker, holding three rusted washers and an unspent collar stud. He had never brought it ashore. As long as it sat in the locker, he was coming back to clear it out.

Below, the lead Apex truck touched the fifty-yard line. The driver slowed, recognizing the massive silhouette of the overhead truss, but did not stop. His orders were to take the gate. The crane was derelict; the cameras said it hadn't moved since the previous decade.

*“Clear the gantry platform,”* the bullhorn warned, closer now, its acoustic pressure shuddering in Jin's chest. *“Personnel on the crane: step down. Stand down.”*

“Now!” Jin shouted.

Chen Rui heaved back on the pry bar with a guttural grunt, his chest straining against his work shirt as the secondary pawl groaned and backed off its seat by a fraction of an inch.

Aisyah held the brass drift against the warped collar with both hands, her head turned aside to spare her eyes from the steel flakes.

Jin brought the sledge down.

The blow rang like a church bell in a flooded cellar. The impact shot through Jin’s wrists, rattling his teeth in their gums, but the pin didn't move. The rust holding the sleeve was like cured resin.

Thirty yards. The front tires of the lead carrier hit the broken concrete of the shipyard boundary line.

Jin drew breath, ignoring the spasm in his spine, and swung a second time, putting every ounce of his failing ballast into the arc.

The sledge struck the drift dead center. A shower of burnt-orange rust flakes exploded into the monsoon wind. The distorted pin sheared through its corrosion seal, spitting out the far side of the casing and clattering seventy feet down into the mud below.

For half a second, nothing happened. The grease on the guide rails was cold and stiff as lard.

Then the counterweight shifted.

A sound like an iron vault door tearing free of its hinges echoed across the basin. The sixteen-ton cradle of railroad ties plunged downward into the marsh with a sickening, accelerating rush of displaced air. On the causeway side, the massive structural truss swung down through its fifty-foot arc, slicing through the storm like a falling guillotine.

The driver of the lead transport hit his air brakes. The vehicle’s front tires locked, smoking against the wet asphalt, its front bumper diving toward the dirt.

The steel drop-frame struck the concrete causeway two yards ahead of the transport’s grille.

The shock wave lifted Jin off his feet, throwing him against the gantry railing. A deafening crack rolled across the water as the truss teeth bit into the reinforced concrete foundations of the old gatehouse, wedging themselves four feet into the foundation stone. The massive beam came to rest at a sixty-degree angle across the roadway, dead center, sealing the causeway from ditch to marsh.

A cloud of pulverized concrete dust and steam rose around the transport’s headlights.

Inside the cab, the Apex driver sat motionless behind the wire-mesh windshield. His lights illuminated the steel web of the gantry truss—massive, cold, and entirely immobile. To move it, they would need four industrial burning torches and three days of continuous oxygen delivery.

The three transports behind him halted, their red brake lights washing the marsh in crimson.

On the platform above, Chen Rui sank to his knees, his breath hitching in his chest, clutching his forearms. Aisyah was already leaning over the rail, her eyes clear and unblinking, watching the private security guards step down from their cabs with their rain-hoods up, looking at the barrier not with fury, but with the hollow, defeated calculation of salaried contractors who knew their shift had just become impossible.

Jin hauled himself upright against the crane cab, gasping as his back seized in a tight, burning knot. He didn't cheer. He spat a mouthful of rust-tinged phlegm over the edge.

Across the slipway, Lin Zhang’s voice cut through his handheld radio, urgent and jagged with panic:

*“Jin! Look seaward! The tugs—they aren't maneuvering for Berth 9!”*

Jin turned his back on the sealed land gate and looked south into the gloom of the channel.

The three darkened salvage cutters had not split up. They had swung their sterns around into the fairway, dropping massive, heavy-duty sea anchors directly across the deep-water access trench—the only channel deep enough for container traffic.

They weren't trying to board the shipyard.

They were laying down an acoustic hydrophone array and deep-water steel drag-chains to snag the municipal fiber-optic trunk that ran through the channel floor. They were going to sever the optical lifeline to Batam from fifty fathoms down, where no crane could drop and no fire monitor could reach.
