The vibration traveled up through the ferry’s steel keel, a dull, resonant grind that set the tea dregs dancing in Lin’s dented mug.

"They’re dragging the seabed," Jin Wei barked, his voice raw as iron filings. "Ballast control—now!"

Lin didn't argue. In the belly of the vessel, there was no room for economic models or legal philosophy. Her thumb found the manual release lever beside the flooded cofferdam. The brass handle was cold, slick with condensed salt sweat. She threw her weight against it, her boots skidding on the rusted diamond plating. 

A pneumatically sealed valve shrieked open two decks below. 

Twelve thousand gallons of seawater, held high in the ferry’s starboard trimming tanks, dropped through the bottom scuppers in a violent, cascading purge. The surge struck the shallow harbor mud like an underwater landslide. Through the open companionway, the sound reached them: a muffled, hydraulic *thud* as the wall of displaced water smashed into the channel floor, tumbling the privateers’ remote cutting sledge off its tracks and burying its carbide blades in deep silt.

The vibration ceased. The anchor catenary held.

Jin Wei slumped against the companionway bulkhead, his blue work shirt plastered to his ribs. Beneath the salt-stiffened cloth, his breath hitched—a wet, rattling catch that he masked by pressing his forearm hard against his sternum. Before the shipping consortium had revoked his master’s ticket, he had spent his shore leaves in a quiet Pontianak garden tending moth orchids, misting their roots with rainwater from an enameled cup. Now, his hands smelled exclusively of stale diesel and bilge lye, the skin across his knuckles split into tiny red fissures that never quite closed.

"They'll clear the intake filters in two hours," Jin Wei wheezed, wiping a streak of engine grease from his cheek. "The tactical skiffs will circle the reef until the tide turns, then they’ll drop another rig. We can’t sit behind the sea gate. We have three grain lighters and two water barges trapped in the shallows. If we don’t move them across the channel before midnight, the privateers will seize the crews under the admiralty lien."

"Who navigates them?" Lin asked, her fingers digging into her temples where a migraine throbbed behind her left eye. She had bitten the inside of her lip raw during the dive scare, and the sharp taste of copper lingered on her tongue. "The port authority has blacklisted every senior master in the basin. The moment an old captain touches a helm, the harbor grid flags their biometric license and locks the steering thrusters."

"Then no captain takes the helm," a low voice said from the ladderwell.

Uncle Tan hauled himself up into the radio room, moving with the careful, deliberate stiffness of a man whose knees were swollen with fluid. Around his left wrist, he had bound a strip of faded green batik cloth—an old lighterman's trick to compress an inflamed tendon without a splint. In his other hand, he carried a heavy, hexagonal drum made of riveted marine brass, its cylindrical sides scuffed dull by decades of salt spray.

"The charter is clear, Lin," Tan murmured, resting the brass drum on the chart table. "Article Four. When operational command is frozen by legal sabotage, authority reverts to the assembly. We do not pick a hero. We draw the lot."

"Tan, the skiffs are out there," Jin Wei said, straightening up with an involuntary grimace as his lumbar spine ground bone on bone. "A single wrong heading through the south spit and thirty thousand bushels of rice end up under corporate impound."

"The lot does not care about the skiffs," Tan said simply. "And the sea does not care about licenses. Rouse the fifteen."

***

They convened in the hollowed-out lower hold of the grounded ferry *Pulau Tujuh*. The air was thick with the suffocating humidity of the Malacca Strait, ninety-eight percent relative moisture hanging trapped beneath corrugated iron and peeling red lead primer. The smell was ancient and immediate: bilge oil, wet hemp line, frying shallots from the shelter galley down the passageway, and the sharp, vinegar tang of lead-acid cells venting under load.

Fifteen upturned cable spools had been arranged in a tight, inward-facing circle on the bare deckplates—strict odd parity, as the charter demanded. 

No dais. No microphone. Only the dull thrum of the tidal turbines vibrating through the hull, keeping the low-slung work lamps burning with a steady, yellow pulse.

The fifteen seated there were not politicians. They were the human residue of three collapsed regional shipping lines: a boiler-room wiper with chemical burns across his collar; a crane rigger whose spine was permanently curved from forty years of sling work; a cook from an arrested inter-island tramp; three young line-handlers from the Batam wet-docks; two river pilots whose small craft had been seized for unpaid canal dues. They sat with their forearms resting on their knees, shoulders hunched, watching the brass drum in the center of the circle.

Tan stepped forward. He did not deliver a speech. He reached into his pocket, pulled out an iron key, and unlatched the drum’s hinged inspection plate. Inside lay ninety-seven identical wooden tokens turned from mangrove heartwood, each stamped with an identification notch corresponding to a registered deckhand, engineer, or lighterman currently sheltering in the yard.

"Operational mandate for shipping dispatch," Tan announced, his voice carrying clearly without amplification over the rhythmic slosh of the tide against the outer plates. "The dispatch leader holds sole tactical authority over the southern passage until dawn. Simple majority confirms. If rejected, the drum turns again."

Tan gave the brass crank three full revolutions. The wooden tokens clattered inside like dry seed pods.

He reached in without looking. His calloused fingers emerged holding a single mangrove disc. He squinted at the notched edge, then read the registry number stamped into the grain.

"Eighty-four," Tan said. "Kasim."

A sudden, sharp rustle ran through the circle. Heads turned toward the back row of spools.

A young deckhand sitting near the shadow of a web frame froze, a half-rolled clove cigarette pinched between his fingers. 

Kasim was twenty-eight, though the harsh equatorial sun had already etched deep, crow-footed creases around his pale hazel eyes. He was missing the lower half of his left earlobe—torn away six years ago when a synthetic mooring line had parted under tension on a Singapore bunker barge—and his left ear remained half-deaf from the sonic crack of the snap-back. His hands, resting flat against his grease-stained work trousers, were broad and square, the fingernails permanently rimmed with black zinc compound that resisted every brand of pumice soap.

"Me?" Kasim’s voice was dry, cracking slightly on the vowel. He looked around the circle as if expecting someone to point out an obvious clerical error. "Uncle, I’m a deck hand. I grease fairleads and haul wire. I don’t draft passage plans."

"You know the south spit?" Jin Wei asked from the perimeter of the circle, his voice stripped of rank, flat and testing.

"I know it," Kasim muttered, his thumb tracing the frayed cuff of his sleeve. "Ran mud-scows through the mangrove channels during the sand embargo. But that’s manual steering. You’re talking about leading four loaded barges through an automated radar gate."

Across the circle, an older man with the clipped, graying mustache of a former deep-sea second mate leaned forward, his face hardening. "This is madness. Kasim doesn't know the optical mesh protocols. If the port marshal’s patrol launches close within three cables, they’ll run an active vector scan. A deckhand will panic and heave-to. We need an officer who understands admiralty arrest procedure."

"An officer with a certified ticket is a beacon on their scanners," Lin Zhang cut in from the shadow of the ladder, her voice sharp, cutting through the rising murmur. "The moment your signature hits the navigation network, their automated receivers execute the lien. Kasim has no ticket. To their surveillance algorithms, he is invisible—just another piece of ballast shifting in the hold."

"It isn't about paperwork," Kasim said suddenly.

The circle went quiet. Kasim had not stood up, but his posture had changed. The diffidence was gone, replaced by the hard, defensive economy of a man who had survived three sinkings in five years.

"The patrol launches don't watch the bottom," Kasim said, staring directly at the skeptical former mate. "They run on deep-draft bathymetry models sold by the Singapore hydrographic office. Those charts were surveyed four years ago. After the monsoon floods last November, the mud bank behind Pulau Semakau shifted two hundred meters north. If you follow an admiralty passage plan at half-ebb, your tug’s skeg hits the clay and stays there until the tide comes back. You want to run the rice barges tonight? You don't use the channel. You run them five yards off the mangrove roots, through the oyster beds, where the water’s three feet shallower than their patrol craft can draft."

He looked at his own scarred palms, then looked up at Tan.

"I can take them through," Kasim said softly. "But I don't use their harbor radio. I steer by the light of the kerosene torches on the fish stakes. If one of their tactical skiffs tries to box us in, I won't reverse engines. I'll put the barge into the soft mud and let their own wash swamp them."

Tan nodded once, then raised his right hand. "The lot has spoken. Confirm or return to the drum."

The silence in the hold lasted four heartbeats. 

Then the crane rigger raised his thick, calloused palm. The wiper followed. The young line-handlers raised theirs without hesitation. Even the graying second mate, after a bitter, swallowing pause, lifted his hand into the dim light.

Twelve of fifteen. Qualified operational majority.

Tan picked up the mangrove token, dipped it into an open tin of red lead paint, and pressed it firmly onto the wooden dispatch board hanging from the ferry’s center stanchion. The scarlet ring gleamed wetly against the gray grain.

"The passage is yours, Kasim," Tan said. "Take the barges."

Kasim swallowed, stood up, and took the laminated channel chart Lin held out to him. His hands were steady now, entirely free of the tremor that had shaken him five minutes earlier.

Before he could reach the ladder, the high gantry optical relay above the drydock gave a sharp, mechanical groan.

The blue line-of-sight laser overhead didn't just flicker—it sheared completely off, its cyan glow snapping into darkness. From the outer pier, a young rigger dropped through the hatchway three steps at a time, his bare feet slapping wet against the steel treads.

"Kasim! Captain!" the boy gasped, clutching the hatch coaming to keep from falling. "The tactical skiffs didn't circle back to the anchorage! They’ve hooked a towline to an abandoned container hull outside the breakwater—they're pulling it across the channel mouth to choke the reef!"

Kasim didn't look back at Jin Wei. He rolled his chart into a tight cylinder, stepped past the boy, and threw open the weather door into the pelting monsoon rain.
