The yellow paper felt dry as birch bark between Ananya’s thumbs. The fibrous draft sheets, ruled in faded blue and scored with Miriam’s neat, narrow fountain-pen script, gave off the faint, chemical bite of cheap bunker stationery.

Ananya did not read the words again. She didn’t need to. She had seen the cadence of those clauses before—in Bombay municipal housing decrees, in district magistrate clearances that declared slum settlements "pathologically disorganized" before the bulldozers rolled in, in the sterile English circulars her mother used to bring home from the Girgaon textile mills. Her mother could not read the Queen’s alphabet, but she could calculate spindle ratios in her head down to the ounce, wrapping bruised fingers in damp turmeric rags while the Oxford-educated clerks docked half the shift’s pay for "insubordinate syntax."

Ananya gripped the sheaf with both hands.

The sound of the paper tearing was shockingly loud in the enclosed concrete air of Station 74-Nord. It was not a clean slice, but a ragged, shearing rip that tore through three layers of Miriam’s legal pad, jagged and fibers-first, down through Subsection Three, down through the margins, down through Tariq’s penciled footnotes.

She let the halves fall. Then she picked up the pieces, doubled them over, and tore them again.

Confetti of yellow legal paper fluttered into the steam curling off the chicory pot.

"Ananya!" Miriam’s voice broke on a sharp, inhaled gasp. Her fountain pen rolled off the zinc-wrapped tabletop, clattering against the edge and striking the granite floor with a small, sharp *tink* that split its brass nib. "That was three days of work. We were structuring the baseline—"

"You were structuring the gate," Ananya said. Her voice remained flat, dry, carrying none of the performative fury of an orator. It was the voice of a dockside weighmaster catching a thumb on the scale. "Every colonial civil service examination ever written started with this exact sentence. The British in Bengal. The Dutch in Batavia. The French in Algiers. It is never called exclusion. It is always called *an objective standard of moral fitness*."

She dropped the shredded scraps onto the center of the table. A draft from the ventilation conduit caught several slips, sending them skimming across the cold zinc into Tariq’s lap.

"Look at them," Ananya said, staring directly at Julian, whose hands were clasped around a tin mug so tightly the skin across his knuckles was waxy and bloodless. "Look at what you drafted. 'Indicators of behavioral instability.' 'Chronic somatic stress reactions.' A woman whose brother was shot dead in an unregistered textile strike has a somatic stress reaction. An illiterate dockhand who watched his sister’s lungs turn to red mud from silica dust has persecutory fixation. You aren't screening for balance, Julian. You are screening for people who have never been hit."

Julian Mercer’s jaw twitched. A tremor ran through his left eyelid, subtle but persistent, the telltale rattle of an exhausted central nervous system running on dry adrenaline and chemical dampeners. He set his cup down with deliberate precision.

"We are building a governance protocol for a distributed planetary network, Ananya, not a grievance tribunal," Julian said, his accent clipping every consonant into a razor. He spoke with the exhausting, methodical patience of a man explaining compound interest to a child. "If the selection algorithm draws an individual who is actively decompensating, who cannot read a water-treatment balance sheet or process a cold-chain distribution deficit without suffering an emotional seizure, the entire node stalls. People starve. Factions take the pumps. You cannot run life-support infrastructure on unvetted rage."

"And who writes the vetting exam?" Nayra’s voice drifted from the shadows beside the cold copper ductwork.

She had not uncurled from her corner, but her slender fingers had stopped braiding the greasy brown alpaca strand. Her eyes, sunken beneath the ridges of her brow from eighteen days of subterranean charcoal dust, fixed on Julian like twin iron studs.

"The managers who dammed the upper Loa basin passed every aptitude test the mining ministry devised," Nayra said softly, her Spanish-inflected cadence cutting cleanly through the hum of the air-handling unit. "They had pristine cognitive markers. They scored in the ninety-ninth percentile for rational empathy on the state’s executive matrix. And when the runoff poisoned three thousand hectares of communal quinoa terraces, they wrote twenty-page memoranda explaining why the loss of our children's kidneys was an acceptable variance in national export balance."

Nayra stood up, shaking the dust from her wool poncho. She took three slow steps forward, into the yellow spill of the halogen work-lamp.

"Sociopaths are the best test-takers in human history, Julian," Nayra whispered, leaning both forearms against the table rim. "They do not shake when they lie. Their pulse stays at sixty beats a minute while they draft an order to cut off electricity to a maternity ward. Your psychological filters will not stop them. Your filters will give them an honorary diploma and a velvet key to the master switches."

Julian’s face went white. The skin around his nostrils flared. "That is an absurd, bad-faith reduction. We are proposing verifiable, psychometric—"

"You are proposing a priesthood!" Ananya’s voice finally rose, not into a shout, but into a jagged, grating rasp that vibrated in her chest. Her right knee gave a dull, grinding spasm as she shifted her weight forward, the scar tissue pulling tight like dried rawhide across the bone. "You sit there shivering in a sub-zero bunker, half out of your mind with your own terror, and you are still trying to invent an entrance exam so you don't have to share a room with an assembly-line worker who smells like kerosene!"

"Because that assembly-line worker will burn down the cooling towers if his union boss tells him to!" Julian barked, slamming his open palms down against the table. The zinc rang like a muffled gong. He rose to his feet, leaning across the expanse of wood, his breath misting grey in the seven-degree chill between them. "You think pure chance is noble? You think drawing a name out of a hat is salvation? It is a suicide pact, Ananya. You are reckless. You have always been reckless. You mistake the destruction of order for liberation, and if you leave the levers of survival to the unwashed lottery of a traumatized mob, we will all freeze to death before the first thaw!"

The word *mob* hung in the cold, dense air like poison.

Tariq leaned back, his chair scraping violently against the concrete. Koffi’s brass timer gave a sharp, mechanical *tock* as his hand tightened around its frame.

Ananya didn't yell. She didn't retreat.

She dropped her center of gravity, ignoring the white-hot flare of agony that ripped through her shattered knee, and wedged both of her callused, scarred hands beneath the thick, lip-bound oak crossbeam that supported the central assembly table.

"Ananya, don't—" Miriam cried out, reaching forward.

With a guttural, tearing heave from the core of her hips and back, Ananya threw her entire body weight upward.

The four-hundred-pound oak-and-zinc refectory table groaned, its iron lag bolts screaming against the framing. Then, with a violent, deafening crash, it inverted.

Tin mugs, enamel bowls, and steaming chicory went airborne in a chaotic arc. The ceramic filter carafe hit the granite floor and exploded into thirty jagged shards. Hot, bitter liquid splashed across the concrete, instantly hissing as it met the cold stone. Miriam’s remaining legal pads, weeks of painstakingly drafted constitutional codices, scattered like dry autumn leaves into the spreading brown pool.

The heavy oak slab slammed onto its edge, then crashed flat onto the concrete upside down, its raw-timber underbelly exposed to the fluorescent glare, iron brackets sticking up like broken ribs.

For half a heartbeat, the bunker was dead silent save for the dripping of chicory from the edge of the overturned zinc.

Then Julian lunged.

His face was contorted into a snarl of pure, defensive panic, his hands clawing forward to shove Ananya back. "You madwoman—!"

Before his fingers could reach her coat, a slab of forged steel split the space between them with a vicious, air-cutting whistle.

Kenjiro Sato stepped directly into the breach. He did not yell. He simply planted his iron-toed work boots into the pool of chicory and brought his fourteen-inch industrial spud wrench up to chest height, holding it two-handed like a stave, the grease-darkened steel flat against Julian’s sternum.

Julian slammed into the cold steel bar, knocked back three paces, his heel catching on an overturned tin cup.

"Step back, Mercer," Kenjiro said. The words were flat, level, and utterly lethal. His deaf left ear was turned to the wall; his right eye remained fixed on the center of Julian’s throat.

"Get your hands off me!" Julian choked, his boots slipping on the wet floor. His left hand flailed, striking the broken remnants of the ceramic carafe on the floor to break his fall. A shard of glazed porcelain sheared through the skin of his palm, opening a deep, dark red trench from his index knuckle down to the heel of his hand. "Look at what you’ve done! Look at this!"

"Stop it! *Stop it!*"

The shriek came from the doorway of the battery gallery. Min-Jun Kang was clutching both sides of his head, his fingers tangled in his grease-matted hair, his eyes wild and dilated to the rim. His chest heaved in shallow, frantic gasps, his grey wool sweater soaked with sweat despite the biting chill of the vault.

"It's a prison!" Min-Jun screamed, his voice cracking into high, hysterical registers that echoed off the damp ceiling vaults. "It’s not a safe-house! It’s a tomb! We’re locking ourselves in a concrete box to cut each other’s throats while the ice closes in! We have twelve days before the trawler leaves us, and we are going to die down here killing each other over paper!"

No one moved.

Blood from Julian’s severed knuckle dripped steadily, rhythmic and heavy, falling with a quiet, wet *pat-pat-pat* onto the shredded yellow sheets floating in the chicory puddle.

Julian remained on one knee, his chest heaving, his right hand balled into a shaking fist, his eyes burning with an unmasked, murderous hate as he stared up at Ananya Patel.

Ananya stood balanced on her good leg, her fists clenched at her sides, her breathing ragged and raw, her jaw set like an anvil.

Between them, the steel of Kenjiro’s wrench remained level, unyielding, and seconds away from caving in a human skull.
