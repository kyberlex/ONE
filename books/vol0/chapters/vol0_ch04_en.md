The cold in the service trench did not settle on the skin; it sank into the marrow, dull and persistent like an untreated toothache.

Dr. Min-Jun Kang pressed his back against the weeping granite wall, his breath feathering into the damp air in short, ragged bursts. Beneath his thermal undershirt, the nicotine patch on his collarbone had long since lost its stick, peeling away against clammy skin. His temples throbbed with the dull, hammering ache of seventy-two hours without coffee or sleep, every nerve ending rubbed raw by the silence. 

Ahead of him in the crawlway, Kenjiro Sato knelt in the slush that pooled along the drainage conduit. Kenjiro’s heavy canvas jacket was smeared with black graphite grease, his breathing steady, measured, almost meditative. In his right hand, the machinist carried a solid brass drift punch; in his left, an analog loop wand wired back to the bulky spectrum analyzer strapped across Min-Jun’s chest.

On the bulkhead above their heads, the brass cylinders of the central countdown chronometer were hidden behind three meters of reinforced rock, but Min-Jun didn't need to see them. The number was burned into the back of his eyelids.

`648:00:00.` 

Four days gone. Twenty-seven days before the pack ice choked the fjord and sealed the mountain for the long dark.

"Copper continuity at the outer seam," Kenjiro said. His voice was a low rasp, gravelly from a lifetime near industrial drop-forges, his left ear tilted slightly toward the rock face to compensate for his deadened eardrum. "Zero grounding faults on the lower flange. Mesh is solid."

Min-Jun looked down at the analyzer’s small, circular phosphor display. The green trace was a flat, unbroken horizon. He nudged the sensitivity knob with a thumb gone numb with frost. The line didn't even shiver. 

"Nothing," Min-Jun whispered, his voice cracking from disuse. "No high-frequency ripple. No microwave carrier. Not a single microwatt leaking through the mountain."

"Good," Kenjiro grunted, shifting his weight forward onto his knees. The damp slate bit through his work pants, but the older man didn't flinch. "Means nothing gets in. Means no one knows we’re breathing."

"Or that we’re buried alive."

Kenjiro didn't answer. He laid the palm of his bare hand flat against the rock, his callused skin feeling the deep, subsonic tremor of the Earth. As a young apprentice in Nagoya, before the auto plants had automated every stamping press, Kenjiro had learned to detect a hairline fracture in a five-ton crankshaft purely by resting his palm on the housing while the motor turned. He used that same ancient, physical patience now, reading the mountain’s bones. 

"Intake valve twelve," Kenjiro murmured, nodding toward an iron wheel set deep into an alcove where the wall wept black water. "Secondary atmospheric bypass. Decommissioned sixty years ago. Let's see if the sea air ate the spindle."

Min-Jun scrambled after him, his knees sliding in the cold muck. The analyzer banged against his ribs, its brass carrying handles cold as ice. The bunker smelled of decaying slate, brine, and the sulfurous taint of the geothermal vent two hundred meters below them. 

The wheel was five feet across, forged from heavy maritime cast iron, its rim encrusted with a thick rind of dried red lead and crystalline salt. Kenjiro spat into his palms, gripped the pitted iron, and braced his steel-toed boots against the drainage curb.

"Take the lower spoke," Kenjiro muttered. "On three. Don't jerk it. Shear the brass pin, and we lose the seals."

Min-Jun hooked his fingers under the cold metal. His knuckles, swollen from the damp chill, flared with sharp, stinging pain. He missed the quiet click of server switches, the antiseptic hum of air-conditioned server farms in Pangyo where problems were solved with a stroke of a key. Out here, survival was measured in foot-pounds of torque and the shear strength of dead men’s rivets.

"One," Kenjiro breathed. "Two. Heave."

They threw their weight against the iron. Min-Jun felt the strain tear through his lower back, his breath catching in his throat. The metal groaned—a high, screeching shriek of rust grinding against rust that vibrated through the granite floor. For a terrible second, nothing gave. Min-Jun’s vision swam with grey specks, the nicotine withdrawal squeezing his skull in a vise.

Then, with a hollow, echoing *CRACK*, the valve broke free. 

The wheel turned three inches, releasing a blast of dead, freezing air that smelled of ancient soot and forgotten snow. Kenjiro immediately reversed the pressure, spinning the wheel back until it hit the mechanical stop with a dull, iron thud. 

"Seals hold," Kenjiro said, wiping a streak of black grease across his forehead with the back of his wrist. "Counterweights are intact. If the primary scrubbers choke on coal dust, we can pull surface air by hand. It’ll freeze our lungs, but we won't suffocate."

Min-Jun slumped back against the rock, panting, his chest heaving under the weight of the analyzer. He adjusted the instrument's shoulder strap, his fingers brushing against a square maintenance hatch bolted directly into the bedrock beside the valve. 

A conduit of thick lead piping disappeared into the hatch—not electrical conduit, but heavy, armored fiber-sheathing, lined with pressurized cooling jackets. 

"What's routed through this spur?" Min-Jun asked, his engineer’s eye catching the precision with which the lead had been swaged into the stone. "The main network trunk runs through the central trench."

"Old hydrophone feeds," Kenjiro said, already repacking his drift punch into his canvas roll. "Submarine tracking arrays laid across the seabed in sixty-two. Cut off during the thaw."

"No," Min-Jun said softly. He pulled a heavy flathead screwdriver from Kenjiro’s tool roll without asking. "Hydrophone leads are coaxial copper. This is clad quartz. Optical."

He levered the screwdriver under the corner of the plate. The screws had been treated with anti-corrosion grease; they backed out smoothly, modern threads slipping free of ancient granite. The heavy plate clattered into the slush.

Behind it lay an optical junction block the size of an ammunition tin, machined from solid billet aluminum. It wasn't Cold War surplus. It was pristine. 

Min-Jun unclipped his inspection torch, clicking the beam onto the internal array. Inside the sealed glass chamber, an intricate lattice of beam-splitting prisms sat suspended in clear synthetic oil. The geometry of the prisms was strange, asymmetric—cut at non-Euclidean angles designed to fold light across twelve reflective planes simultaneously, multiplying the logic paths without generating heat.

Min-Jun’s breath stopped. The headache behind his eyes vanished, replaced by an electric jolt of recognition.

"Min-Jun?" Kenjiro asked, pausing. "What is it?"

"The refractive geometry," Min-Jun whispered, his fingers hovering over the glass without touching it. "It's an optical matrix for non-linear state verification. It routes dynamic logic across sixty-four channels using multi-variable wave interference."

"English," Kenjiro said.

"It’s not military," Min-Jun said, his pulse hammering against his ribs. "The theoretical math for this specific folding lattice was only published three years ago. In a doctoral monograph on decentralized aquifer routing." He swallowed, the dry air sticking in his throat. "Published by the Department of Applied Mathematics at Tsinghua."

Kenjiro stood motionless in the dim crawlway, the beam of his headlamp cutting through the swirling frost. "Chen."

"Dr. Chen Liang," Min-Jun said. "She didn't just come here because she was blacklisted. She designed the physical hardware backbone before we even set foot on the trawler. This whole facility... the optical core wasn't found here. It was planted."

A heavy, suffocating weight settled between them. In the dark belly of the mountain, every piece of stone they had assumed to be neutral, historic, or abandoned suddenly felt deliberate. Orchestrated. 

"We don't speak of it," Kenjiro said quietly, his voice dropping an octave, dead and flat. "Not until we understand the architecture. Not until the sortition assembly convenes."

"Kenjiro, she's sitting upstairs right now, drinking tea like an exiled clerk while her code is running the logic that controls our life support—"

"I said not yet," Kenjiro repeated, gripping Min-Jun’s shoulder with fingers that felt like cold iron clamps. "Look around you, Min-Jun. We have twenty-seven days. If we start hunting each other for ghosts before the ice sets, we die in this hole. Put the cover back."

Min-Jun stared at the older man, seeing the deep burn scars running along the machinist's knuckles, the calm, unyielding certainty in his eyes. Slowly, Min-Jun knelt, seated the aluminum faceplate back into the stone recess, and drove the screws home until the metal bit flush.

They crawled out of the trench in silence, dragging their boots through the slush until the narrow tunnel widened into the lower corridor of the living quarters. 

The bunker was quiet. The others were asleep or hollowed out in their bunks, exhausted after four days of ideological attrition and freezing rations. The only sound was the faint, rhythmic hum of the air scrubbers and the distant, heavy tick of the chronometer high above.

Min-Jun unbuckled the spectrum analyzer, preparing to set it on the workbench near the door. Before powering it down, he ran the broad-spectrum wand in an idle, habitual arc across the room to calibrate the zero-point noise floor of the living area.

The phosphor screen should have shown a flat, lifeless line. 

*Pip.*

Min-Jun froze. 

Kenjiro stopped halfway through untying his boots, his head turning slowly.

The wand had swept past the row of curtained bunks along the western wall. 

*Pip... pip... pip...*

It wasn't radio noise. It wasn't the atmospheric crackle of northern lights or static bleed from the geothermal dynamo. It was an ultra-low-frequency, micro-burst signal—less than half a milliwatt, pulsed at precise, mathematical intervals of eight hundred milliseconds.

Min-Jun raised the wand again, his hand trembling slightly from exhaustion. He took a single, silent step forward. 

The trace on the phosphor display jumped, a clean, sharp spike rising from the green line like an executioner’s blade. 

*Pip.*

Min-Jun took another step down the narrow aisle of bunks, following the signal. His boots made no sound on the salt-stained concrete. He extended the sensor wand, passing the drawn curtain of Tariq’s bunk—nothing. Past Ananya’s empty cot—flatline. 

He stopped at the third bunk. 

The curtain was slightly parted. Inside, wrapped in three layers of damp grey wool, Julian Mercer lay fast asleep, his breathing thin and shallow, his pale knuckles curled tightly against his chest, clutching an empty plastic pill bottle.

Min-Jun lowered the tip of the wand. 

The phosphor needle spiked hard against the glass margin, screaming silently. The rhythmic electronic tick was coming from directly beneath Julian’s mattress.
