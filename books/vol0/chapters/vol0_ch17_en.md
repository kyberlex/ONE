The echo of Min-Jun’s scream died against the dripping granite, swallowed by the subterranean draft that always smelled faintly of dead coal and brine. 

Julian was still on his knee, blood welling thick and dark from the severed flesh of his palm, dripping through the chicory puddle to soak Miriam’s shredded yellow legal pads. Kenjiro’s spud wrench did not waver. Its forged iron edge hung four inches from Julian’s breastbone, immovable as an anvil.

Koffi Adebayo did not hurry. He had learned thirty years ago, during the dock strikes in San-Pédro, that running toward a drawn weapon only quickened the striker’s pulse. He stepped between them, his heavy wool overcoat brushing Kenjiro’s knuckles, his broad shoulders sliding into the narrow lane of violence. He did not touch the iron bar. Instead, he reached into his deep left coat pocket and brought out a squat, egg-shaped kitchen timer made of solid, dull-finished brass.

The metal was scuffed from decades of damp cupboards and tea-stained tables. Koffi placed it squarely on an upturned wooden salt-cod crate between Julian’s bleeding hand and Ananya’s boots.

With a harsh, ratcheting scrape, he twisted the dome clockwise, past the zero mark, then set it back to five.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.*

The escapement inside was loud, deliberate, and entirely indifferent to the room.

"Kenjiro," Koffi said, his voice a low, gravelly baritone that vibrated through the cold air. "Lower the iron. Back two paces."

Kenjiro’s right eye flicked to Koffi, assessed the lack of panic in the old man’s jaw, and slowly brought the spud wrench down to his thigh. He stepped back.

"Julian," Koffi murmured, not looking down at the blood. "Stand up."

Julian struggled against the wet floor, his boots sliding in chicory before he found purchase on a dry seam of granite. He dragged himself upright, clutching his wrist to slow the bleed. His face was gray, the skin around his eyes pulled drum-tight from three weeks of half-doses of heart medication. His chest hitched in sharp, shallow sputters.

"You have five minutes," Koffi told him.

Julian blinked, his lips trembling with rage. "I’m not performing for your—"

"You will speak," Koffi interrupted, without raising his voice by a decibel. "You will look directly into Ananya’s face. You will not explain your balance sheets. You will not quote property statutes. You will tell her what terror wakes you up at three in the morning. And you, Ananya." Koffi turned his heavy, bloodshot gaze toward her. Her right leg was trembling under the strain of carrying her weight off her damaged knee. "You will not blink. You will not scoff. You will not breathe a single syllable. If your tongue moves behind your teeth, I reset the spring."

Ananya’s jaw tightened until a knot of muscle pulsed along her cheekbone, but she did not break. She planted her heels and locked her dark, burning eyes onto Julian’s shivering pupils.

"Go," Koffi said.

The brass mechanism chopped the seconds into bone-dry fragments. *Tick. Tick. Tick.*

Julian opened his mouth to spit an insult, caught Koffi’s flat, unyielding stare, and faltered. The silence pressed in on him, cold and suffocating. His fingers twitched against his torn palm, smearing dark red across his cuff.

"You think... you think it’s greed," Julian whispered. The rasp was cracked, stripped of all parliamentary polish. "You think men like me sit in high rooms counting gold while the world burns because we enjoy the fire."

Ananya’s chest rose and fell in a slow, furious rhythm. She didn't blink.

"My mother died behind bolted mahogany doors in ninety-seven," Julian said, his voice dropping into a raw, hollow register that made Min-Jun flinch in the dark doorway. "The fuel subsidies collapsed. The grid failed in forty-eight hours. The people outside weren't an assembly. They weren't a brotherhood. They were three thousand starving men with rusted rebar and gasoline rags. They didn't come to distribute bread, Patel. They came to tear human meat off the bone because their children were crying."

A drop of sweat ran through the grime on Julian’s temple, freezing near his ear.

"If the ledger breaks—if the contract dissolves—there is no commune waiting beneath it. There is only the dark, and the club, and the strongest animal in the pack. I built walls because without walls, the screaming never stops. That is what you want to invite in. That is what you call justice."

He stopped, his breath sawing in his throat, his eyes wide, pleading and terrifying all at once. 

The brass timer clicked. Thirty seconds of absolute stillness hung in the vault. Julian’s mouth hung open, empty, his soul scraped raw against the granite floor.

*Ping.*

The brass bell sounded, sharp and bright, cutting the tension like a straight razor.

Julian slumped, his head falling forward, his shoulders caving inward under his wool sweater.

Koffi did not pause. He reached down with his callused, dark fingers, gripped the brass crown, and twisted it again.

*Scrrr-tch.* Five minutes.

"Julian," Koffi said softly. "Raise your eyes. Keep them on her."

Julian forced his chin up. His knuckles were white where he squeezed his wounded hand.

"Ananya," Koffi said. "Five minutes. No theories. No manifestos. Give him the blood."

Ananya took a short, sharp inhalation through her nose. The smell of copper from Julian’s hand was heavy between them. She did not raise her voice. When she spoke, it possessed the flat, terrible weight of an iron hull sliding into cold water.

"In the dry season after the rains failed," she said, her voice gravelly, "four hundred men walked off the grain berths at Nhava Sheva. Not for politics. The grain was rotting in private silos while their daughters ate paste made from boiled newspaper and floor sweepings. They sat on the rails. They held hands."

She took half a step forward, ignoring the fire in her torn cartilage. Julian didn't flinch, but his pupils contracted.

"The shipping company held an injunction signed by a judge who owned two thousand shares in the holding trust. The state did not send mediators. They sent three platoons of state reserve police with four-foot bamboo lathis weighted with lead caps."

She lifted her left hand, slowly turning the palm upward, showing the ragged scar tissue that ran from her wrist into the muscle of her forearm.

"They did not disperse us. They broke every finger on every man’s right hand so they could never hold a wrench again. My cousin was nineteen. He fell under the wheels of an uncoupled freight car while running from tear gas. A sub-inspector put a steel-shod boot on his neck and held him there until the weight crushed his windpipe."

Julian’s throat hitched. He tried to look down at his bleeding hand, but Koffi’s shadow loomed behind the crate, silent and unmoving. Julian forced his eyes back to hers.

"Your contract did that," Ananya whispered, the words freezing in tiny white clouds before her lips. "Your order did that. Your peace is a cemetery where the headstones are kept very clean by men who never have to see the bodies being dumped in the lime. You fear the mob because you know what you have earned."

The brass teeth of the timer clicked relentlessly. 

Julian’s lips parted, an instinctual defense rising to his tongue, but Koffi’s boot shifted an inch on the stone. Julian swallowed the retort down into his dry throat, choking on it. He stood there, trapped in the light of the kerosene lantern, forced to carry the dead boy on the railroad tracks without the armor of his ledgers or his courts.

*Ping.*

The chime cleared the air like a sudden drop in barometric pressure.

Silence rushed in, heavier than before. Nobody moved. Nayra stood by the drafting table, the alpaca cord wound so tightly around her fingers that the tips had turned purple. Dr. Chen held his temple with two ink-stained fingers, his breath entirely soundless.

Koffi picked up the brass timer. The warm metal felt solid in his broad palm. He slipped it back into his coat pocket.

"The assembly is suspended," Koffi said. His eyes swept across the ten survivors of the broken century. "Stage Two. Twenty-four hours. Total silence."

Julian blinked, his voice a hoarse croak. "We have twelve days before the fjord—"

"Not a word," Koffi cut him off, the tone absolute. "No debate. No drafted compromises. No whispers over tea. If you need water, you point. If you need fuel, you lift the bucket. If a single human voice sounds in this vault before the chronometer reaches two-hundred-and-fifty-two hours, I will personally unseal the manual airlock release and walk into the snow."

He looked at Min-Jun, then at Ananya, and finally at Julian.

"Now pick up the glass."

Koffi turned, walked to the maintenance alcove, and retrieved a rusted iron dustpan and two stiff horsehair hand-brushes. He did not hand them out with ceremony. He dropped them on the salt-cod crate with a dull clatter.

For thirty seconds, nobody moved. The only sound in the bunker was the wet, steady *drip-drip* from the overhead condensation pipe feeding the gray galvanized barrel in the corner.

Then, slowly, Kenjiro leaned his spud wrench against the wall. He knelt on both knees into the cold slush of chicory, took one of the horsehair brushes, and began sweeping the yellow scraps of shredded parchment into a neat, damp heap.

Julian stood rigid for a moment, his wounded hand throbbing with each beat of his laboring heart. Then, with a slow, trembling breath that fogged the dim lantern light, he sank down onto his haunches across from Kenjiro. 

His right hand reached out. He picked up a jagged shard of white porcelain. His fingers were shaking so violently the shard rattled against the granite, but he didn't drop it. 

Across the crate, Ananya limped forward, picked up the second brush, and knelt opposite Julian.

Their eyes met once—hollow, stripped of rhetoric, cold with an exhaustion that ran deeper than bone—before both looked down at the mess on the floor. 

Side by side, their sleeves touching in the damp cold, they began to clear the wreckage away. Above them, bolted to the damp rock, the mechanical chronometer clicked over in the quiet vault: 

`275:59:59`... `275:59:58`...

Not a single voice broke the freezing dark. In the silence, someone passed a dry rag to Julian's bleeding hand. He took it without looking up, pressed it into his torn palm, and kept sweeping.
