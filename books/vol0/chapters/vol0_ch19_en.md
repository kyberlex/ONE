The mechanical carriage of the Siemens teletype did not pause. It slammed back and forth like a shuttle on an iron loom, spitting perforated zinc-paper ribbon directly into Min-Jun Kang’s trembling hands.

The cold in the vault had teeth today. It gnawed through Min-Jun’s two layers of thermal wool, settling in the joints of his knuckles. For twenty-three days, he had chewed the same corner of his lower lip until the scar tissue formed a hard, bitter ridge. His body was running on forty-minute naps stolen on an empty shipping crate and the stale dregs of chicory root boiled in meltwater. Every sudden scrape of a boot or ping of contracting pipe sent a jagged spike of adrenaline straight into his chest. 

"Stop hovering, Julian," Min-Jun snapped, his voice scraping dry against his throat. 

Julian Mercer did not move. He stood three paces back, his knuckles shoved deep into his coat pockets, the blue-gray circles under his eyes bruised with sleeplessness. Beside him, Miriam Levin held the yellow legal pad to her chest like an infant, her fountain pen capped, the black ink on her fingers faded to gray.

"Is the compilation clean?" Miriam asked.

"It’s not code yet," Min-Jun muttered. He reached into his pocket out of muscle memory, hunting for a pack of cigarettes that had been empty since day four in Tromsø, finding only a shard of unflavored paraffin wax he rolled between his thumb and forefinger. As a boy in the garment alleys of Daegu, he had watched his mother tune industrial sewing machines entirely by ear, adjusting the foot pedal until the rattle of the bobbin synchronized with the hum of the overhead fluorescents. If the tension was off by a millimeter, the silk ripped. If it was too loose, the knot bunched and choked the feed. 

This covenant was no different. Seven hundred pages of moral consensus, labor compromises, and anti-monopoly firewalls, reduced to punched holes in zinc foil. If the logic hitched on a single ambiguous clause, the optical core would tear it to pieces before the first cycle even finished.

"Eva!" Min-Jun called out toward the trench beneath the deck plates. "Give me the optical clock. Bring the manifold to zero."

From the dark pit five meters away came the brutal clank of an iron spanner striking bronze. Dr. Eva Lindqvist hauled herself up the iron ladder, her forearms smudged with lithium grease, her pale hair matted with condensation. She did not shiver; her broad shoulders were locked in that rigid, immovable posture she adopted whenever the facility’s life-support struggled against the permafrost.

"Intake line three has slush," Eva said, wiping sweat and dirty frost from her temple with the back of her wrist. "The geothermal siphon is choking on sub-glacial silt. If you spool that optical mainframe up to full frequency, the batteries will take the entire load. We have four hours of reserve before the catalytic scrubbers starve and we start breathing our own carbon."

"We won't need four hours," Min-Jun said. "The simulator either finds a lethal exploit in the first forty minutes, or the articles hold."

"And if they don't hold?" Nayra Calvimontes spoke from the shadows near the water still. She was winding a length of rough gray alpaca yarn over her fingers, over and under, her breath pluming white in the six-degree air. "If your machine proves that men will always sell the grain stores when winter comes?"

"Then we rewrite the grain clauses," Chen Liang said quietly. The former bureaucrat had not sat down since dawn. His back was straight, his chin lifted, though his wool coat hung loose on his shrinking frame. "Or we freeze in Svalbard knowing we were fools. Feed the tape, Min-Jun."

Min-Jun took a slow, deliberate breath. The air tasted of ozone, damp granite, and old machine oil. He lifted the spool of perforated zinc foil and stepped through the low bulkhead hatch into the cryo-vault alcove.

Here, the temperature plunged instantly. The ambient chill was nothing compared to the deep, unnatural cold radiating from the sealed central cylinder—the optical logic array. Inside that drum of vacuum-jacketed steel, prisms of synthetic sapphire rested in liquid nitrogen, ready to route light through crystalline logic gates at speeds that copper wire could never endure. No external cables left this chamber; no wireless signal could penetrate the two hundred meters of solid Arctic granite and copper mesh above them. Whatever horror they were about to summon would live and die inside this tomb.

Min-Jun set the tape into the mechanical feeder. The bronze teeth engaged with a dry, oily snap.

Beside him, Eva leaned over the analog gauges mounted to the frost-coated bulkhead. Her breath came in short, rhythmic huffs as she watched the needle on the direct-current reserve meter. "She’s cold, Min-Jun. The sapphire block hasn't equalized. If the refractive index drifts by half a micron, the beam will scatter into noise."

"I'm manually biasing the beam splitters," Min-Jun said. He reached into the maintenance collar of the cylinder, his fingers wrapping around five brass micrometer dials. His hands were shaking from caffeine withdrawal, a fine, rhythmic palsy that threatened to strip the threads. 

He closed his eyes. 

*Listen to the bobbin,* he told himself. *Find the thread.*

He remembered the bitter winter of 1998, sitting under the cutting table while his mother worked through the night, the rhythmic *thump-click-thump* of the treadle keeping time against the howling Siberian wind outside their paper-thin window. She had taught him that balance was not stillness; balance was the controlled handling of friction.

His fingers steadied. He turned the first dial three notches clockwise until a faint, high-frequency chime resonated through the steel casing. 

*Vector One: Market Cornering.* The synthetic adversary programmed with every predatory trick of speculative capital—the hoarding of seed grain, the shorting of municipal water bonds, the artificial throttling of supply chains to starve non-compliant communities.

He turned the second dial. A deeper, hollow hum answered.

*Vector Two: Legal Capture.* The bureaucratic parasite—the exploitation of ambiguous language to spawn regulatory priesthoods, gerrymandered sortition pools, and procedural quagmires designed to exhaust ordinary citizens until they surrendered governance to paid administrators.

The third dial resisted, stiff with cold grease. Min-Jun forced it two millimeters. A sharp harmonic whistle pierced the gloom.

*Vector Three: Demagogue Coup.* The psychological infection—the weaponization of local panic, algorithmic grievance, tribal scapegoating, and charismatic falsehoods engineered to persuade a free assembly to vote away its own Class 0 protections.

Dial four clicked into its groove. 

*Vector Four: Kinetic Blockade.* The material strangulation—the severed subsea cables, the naval interdictions in narrow straits, the physical destruction of power transformers, the rail strikes bought with foreign gold to freeze cities in the dark.

And dial five. The master dial.

*Vector Five: Cyber Injection.* The silent poison—the exploitation of sensor latency, poisoned inputs to the hydrological ledger, corrupted consensus timestamps, and the slow, invisible drift of cryptographic trust.

Five hyper-adversarial engines. Five digital warlords, unbound by human ethics, unhampered by pity, programmed for one solitary objective: to find the single hairline fracture in the covenant and tear it wide open until the sortition assemblies collapsed into civil war.

"The clock is locked," Min-Jun whispered, his skin prickling with cold dread. "Eva. Cut the battery bypass."

Eva’s jaw clenched. She seized the massive, twin-pole knife switch mounted to the rock wall and slammed it downward.

An arc of violet fire snapped across the copper contacts. 

The low hum of the station died instantly. The auxiliary light bulbs dimmed to a dull, blood-orange ember as thousands of amperes of direct current surged out of the lithium bank and into the optical drivers. Deep in the granite floor, the tectonic heat pipes groaned, the sudden drop in auxiliary heat causing the sub-glacial ice to expand against the steel jackets.

Inside the sapphire drum, five separate lasers flared into life.

Through the heavy quartz inspection port, Min-Jun saw them: five needles of blinding, coherent light—emerald, violet, ruby, amber, and cobalt—striking the central prism block. The light did not reflect; it refracted through millions of etched microscopic pathways, splitting into an infinite spiderweb of luminous threads that filled the chamber with an eerie, phosphorescent fog.

The mechanical teletype on the outer table did not merely chatter; it screamed.

The carriage slammed against the stops with machine-gun fury. Paper tape spewed across the iron floor, twisting in the draft, white ribbons of paper covered in dense, violent rows of strike-throughs and structural stress vectors.

Julian stepped forward, his boots crunching on the accumulating paper. He knelt, his raw fingers snatching the first foot of tape as it emerged from the feeder. His pale eyes scanned the ink.

"Round One," Julian read, his voice barely audible beneath the roar of the optical cooler. "Adversary Vector One has deployed. It’s not attacking the sortition assemblies."

Miriam leaned over his shoulder, her breath catching. "Then what is it hitting?"

Julian looked back toward the dark cylinder, where the ruby laser was pulsing with erratic, savage violence, heating the quartz port until the frost melted into running drops of water that looked like tears.

"The survival baseline," Julian said, his knuckles turning white as he gripped the tape. "It’s buying every cubic meter of potable water in the southern river basins before our councils even draw their first lot."

Inside the housing, an alarm bell began to hammer. The ruby light flared, drowning out the green and the violet.

The assault on their new world had begun.
