The cold in Station 74-Nord did not arrive from the outside; it seeped upward through the granite floor, a steady, predatory leach that stiffened the joints of Dr. Eva Lindqvist’s fingers until her knuckles felt like dry hinges.

She dragged the flat edge of a carpenter’s chalk down the face of a decommissioned steel distribution panel. The scrape was harsh, setting her teeth on edge. Around the bolted mess table, eight other pairs of lungs rhythmically puffed pale, fragile plumes of condensation into the seven-degree air.

"Without a price mechanism, you are guessing," Julian Mercer said. His voice was too fast, pitched high against the dull thrum of the ventilation shafts. He huddled inside a double-layered wool greatcoat, his bare fingertips white where he gripped a dented tin mug of lukewarm melted pack-ice. The beta-blockers he chewed like mints could dull the tachycardia hammering his ribs, but they did nothing for the shivering. "You eliminate the market, you eliminate the single most efficient distributed computing network humanity ever devised. How do you allocate a ton of steel? How do you route diesel to an orchard instead of a hospital without bids? A commissar decides? A bureaucrat guesses? We’ve seen that graveyard. You get Leningrad. You get mothers trading family silver for rotten cabbages because some central board miscalculated the grain harvest by four percent."

Julian looked around the circle, his bloodshot eyes frantic for an ally. Tariq Al-Fassi was staring into his lap, palms pressed tight against his abdomen to suppress another sour convulsion of bile. Miriam Levin did not look up from her legal pad, the nib of her fountain pen scratching a steady, indifferent rhythm.

Eva lowered the chalk. Her right shoulder burned—a deep, knotting cramp earned from eight years of field surveys dragging core-sampling sleds across the Sarek tundra. 

"The market doesn't compute value, Julian," Eva said, her voice flat, tempered by decades of northern wind. "It computes purchasing power. Those are not the same variable."

"It aggregates scarcity!" Julian retorted, slamming the tin mug down. A splash of gray water hit the zinc tabletop, immediately filming over with a rim of frost. "A price is a signal wrapped in an incentive! If grain is scarce, the price spikes, consumption drops, capital flows to production, and the crisis self-corrects. Break that thermometer, and you blind the whole species."

"The thermometer is plugged into a corpse," Eva said softly. 

She turned back to the steel panel, the memory rising unbidden, sharp and nauseating: a timber boardroom on the top floor of a glass needle in Stockholm, six years ago. The plush wool carpeting, the silent climate control, the sleek slide decks showing negative emissions. She had sat across from men in bespoke cashmere who paid her firm two million kronor to certify a hundred thousand hectares of boreal pine as a permanent carbon sink. She had told them the root systems were suffocating in drought, that the spruce bark beetle was chewing its way north through the warming sap. They had smiled, filed her warnings under an internal appendix, bundled the paper into AAA-rated green bonds, and sold them to European pension funds. Two summers later, a dry lightning storm struck the ridge, and fifty million tons of carbon went into the stratosphere in three days.

The bonds, however, had yielded four point eight percent. On the trading desks in Zurich, the balance sheets were pristine. The pine was dead, the sky was heavier, but the ledger recorded creation of wealth.

"Your price signal didn't see the fire, Julian," Eva said, pressing the chalk hard against the rust-pocked steel until it snapped. "It couldn't. It is blind to the Second Law."

Beside her, Dr. Chen Liang shifted. The low-oxygen air of the submarine vault was carving hollow gray pockets beneath his cheekbones, the low barometric pressure feeding a persistent migraine that made him pinch the bridge of his nose every three minutes. He sat with his spine rigidly straight, an old habit from his years at the Ministry of Water Resources in Beijing—a discipline designed to conceal terror from inspectors.

"In Hebei," Chen murmured, his voice dry as split cane. "In the southern basin beneath Shijiazhuang."

Julian frowned, blinking against the sudden redirection. "What does Hebei have to do with clearing prices?"

"For thirty years," Chen continued, his eyes fixed on the pitted concrete between his boots, "we grew winter wheat to feed four hundred million people. The price of wheat on the Dalian Exchange was stable. The market said everything was fine. Supply matched demand at four hundred yuan per metric ton. Subsidies, futures, transport margins—all balanced to the decimal."

Chen lifted his head. His fingers were trembling slightly, not from cold, but from the memory of the red-stamped dossiers he had locked in steel safes beneath the Third Ring Road. 

"To keep that price stable," Chen whispered, "we sank four million submersible tube wells into the North China Plain. When the shallow water turned to saline mud in 1994, we drilled into the deep confined aquifers. Eight hundred meters down. Fossil water. Rain that fell during the late Pleistocene, when mammoths still grazed the steppe. It takes twenty thousand years to recharge. We pumped it out in twenty-six summers to grow grain to keep a paper price index flat."

The vault fell entirely silent, save for the distant, sucking gurgle of the geothermal loop struggling against sub-glacial slush in the rock beneath them.

"Last year," Chen said, his voice dropping an octave, "the ground sank three meters outside Cangzhou. The casing pipes collapsed. The aquifer did not run low, Julian. The aquifer ceased to exist. The pore spaces in the rock crushed together under the weight of the crust. The water is gone forever. But on the exchange in Shanghai? Wheat moved three cents. The price didn't register the death of an entire hydrological province until the pumps sucked dry silt."

Chen wiped a bead of cold sweat from his lip. "I stamped the hydrological depletion reports *Guojia Jimi*—State Secret. Because if the market knew the deep aquifer was dead, the sovereign credit rating of the provincial development bonds would have crashed. Your price did not aggregate scarcity. It was a camouflage net thrown over physical liquidation."

Eva stepped forward, taking the broken stub of chalk. "Here is the ledger that does not lie," she said.

She drew a massive, thick vertical line down the steel panel, splitting the space in half. 

"On this side, what you call wealth: debt instruments, fiat claims, speculative futures, compounding interest. An infinite mathematical plane with no friction, no gravity, and no borders."

She turned, her boots crunching over iron dust on the floor. 

"On this side: Thermodynamic Exergy. Conserved mass. Physical work potential." 

She wrote three terms in jagged, white block letters across the steel:

`KILOWATT-HOURS OF WORK.`
`CUBIC METERS OF RECOVERABLE WATER.`
`MEGACALORIES OF NUTRITIONAL ENERGY.`

"You cannot discount an electron," Eva said, turning to face Julian. Her gray eyes held no malice, only the cold, unyielding weight of granite. "You cannot leverage a cubic meter of fresh water with a three-to-one margin call. If a community requires ten million calories a day to maintain cellular life, and three million liters of water to keep their crops from wilting, and two gigajoules of thermal work to keep their children from freezing to death in winter—that is a closed physical envelope. You cannot inflate it. You cannot borrow from the twenty-fifth century to settle it."

"And who distributes it?" Julian snapped, his breath billowing thick between them. He surged up from the bench, his wool coat parting, revealing the hollow dip of his collarbones and the desperate agitation in his chest. "You sit there with your chalk, Eva, but you’re dodging the execution! If there is no money, how does a farmer in the Peloponnese know whether to send his harvest to Athens or turn it into olive oil? Who sets the ratio without a common unit of exchange?"

"The work sets the ratio," Eva said.

She moved her hand to the center of the steel. "A unit of account based strictly on dissipative physical work. Not a token printed out of debt, but a clear, non-speculative record of physical throughput. You put three kilowatt-hours of kinetic pumping into moving fresh water through a reverse-osmosis membrane, that physical expenditure is locked into the register. It cannot be lent out at interest. It cannot be hoarded in an offshore shell entity to extract rents from people who are thirsty. When the water is consumed, the exergy is destroyed. It returns to ambient heat. The token vanishes."

Julian opened his mouth to dismiss her, to cite the classics, to invoke the fundamental theorems of welfare economics he had spent twenty years teaching to investment bankers in London. But the words caught in his throat.

His gaze tracked the chalk marks on the steel plate: the mass balances, the inputs, the irreversible flow of physical degradation. 

Energy in. Work extracted. Entropy rejected to the sink.

He stared at the chalk lines. He reached into his coat pocket with a twitching thumb, feeling for the plastic blister pack of propranolol, but his fingers remained still against the foil. 

Money did not measure value. Money was a claim on future work—a claim that had outgrown the physical carrying capacity of the planetary engine by an order of magnitude. It was an exponential phantom demanding that a finite thermodynamic system cannibalize its own biological organs to pay off an arithmetic illusion.

"The interest rate," Julian whispered, his voice cracking against the cold damp of the bunker. His eyes were wide, tracking the stark equations on the rust. "If the ledger tracks only real physical dissipation... compound interest is mathematically impossible. You can't compound an entropic loss."

"No," Eva said gently, dropping the chalk stub into his numb, shivering palm. "You can't."

High above the airlock, the brass teeth of the chronometer groaned, releasing its heavy internal counterweight.

*CLANK.*

The seven-segment mechanical dial snapped:

`528:00:00`.

Twenty-two days. The sub-glacial frost on the ceiling began to drip.
