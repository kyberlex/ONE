The kerosene flame shivered against the cold draft seeping from the air-scrubber ducts, throwing Dr. Chen Liang’s silhouette high onto the damp granite wall.

The silence in Station 74-Nord had become physical over the last forty-eight hours—a heavy, low-pressure ache that pressed against the eardrums like diving too deep in frozen seawater. At the bulkhead, the brass teeth of the chronometer slipped with a dry, mechanical click: *312:00:00*. Halfway through their Arctic exile. Three hundred and twelve hours until the sea ice locked the fjord, sealing the trawler out and entombing them within Spitsbergen’s dead stone.

Chen did not drop his gaze from Tariq Al-Fassi.

A dull, rhythmic spike of pain hammered behind Chen’s right eye, the familiar agony of oxygen-depleted air and fourteen sleepless shifts. In his youth, working the seasonal flood-diversion models along the middle reaches of the Yangtze, he had learned that water never negotiated with the dikes; it simply sought the lowest, weakest point until the earth gave way. Men were no different. Left unchanneled, fear always found the mud.

“The *Keju*,” Tariq murmured. The younger man pulled his coarse woolen scarf tighter around his neck, his dark fingers curled defensively around his tin mug. “You want to resurrect the imperial civil service. You want mandarins in the twenty-first century.”

“I want survival,” Chen said. His voice was quiet, stripped of cadence, carrying the measured finality of a hydraulic seal closing. “The imperial court in Chang’an fell because its examinations tested memory, poetic calligraphy, and loyalty to an emperor who claimed the mandate of heaven. It was literary vanity masquerading as competence. That is not what Julian and I have built.”

Chen gestured with two fingers.

Beside him, Julian Mercer stood. The Englishman’s shoulders twitched under his oversized sheepskin coat, his knuckles swollen red and raw where the skin had split from the bunker’s dry, sub-zero draft. Julian set a heavy, metal-spooled sheaf of perforated paper tape onto the zinc surface, followed by a thick stack of thermal printing charts. His breath billowed white in the dim light, smelling faintly of the bitter, medicinal tea he boiled on the battery casing to suppress his panic-induced heart palpitations.

“We didn't design a test of doctrine,” Julian said, his accent clipped, hollowed out by days of running empirical simulations. He tapped a finger against an intricate neuro-hemodynamic map printed on curling paper. “We designed a filter against human rot. Western cognitive science has spent forty years mapping the cortical pathways of panic, narcissism, and predatory sociopathy. We know precisely which neural structures flare when a man prioritizes tribal vengeance over thermodynamic reality.”

Miriam Levin leaned across the scarred table, her reading spectacles perched crookedly on the bridge of her nose. Her thumbs were black with ink from her fountain pen, her legal pad already filled with scribbled objections. “You’re talking about an ideological litmus test. A psychological gatekeeper. Who writes the questions, Chen? You? The algorithm?”

“No one writes questions,” Chen answered cleanly. He reached out and smoothed the folded parchment with the flat of his palm. His mother had taught him never to crease mulberry paper; even now, surrounded by permafrost and Cold War surveillance relics, the ancient habit governed his hands. “The examination does not ask what a citizen believes. It places them inside an inescapable ethical and operational crucible.”

He let the silence settle, feeling the cold radiate up through the soles of his boots from the concrete floor plates.

“Three distinct tiers,” Chen said. “First: cognitive stress-immersion. The candidate is subjected to dynamic multi-variable resource allocation under acute sensory deprivation and synthetic fatigue. We measure baseline cortisol spikes, executive function stability, and cognitive bias drift. If a candidate demonstrates a predisposition to scapegoat out-groups during simulated famine or hydrological drought, the gate closes. Automatically.”

Ananya Patel shifted on the iron bench, her right leg extended stiffly to ease the ache in her shattered knee. Her dark eyes, usually bright with caustic skepticism, narrowed to needlepoints. “And the second tier?”

“Empathy calibration,” Julian answered for him. Julian’s voice had lost its nervous tremor; in the realm of empirical psychometrics, he moved on solid ice. “Not sentimental pity. Structural empathy. Functional magnetic resonance imaging paired with galvanic and pupillary response while resolving non-zero-sum coordination dilemmas. We test whether the subject can register the material suffering of an anonymous stranger ten thousand kilometers away as an equal weight to their own family’s caloric intake.”

“And the third?” asked Dr. Koffi Adebayo. The trauma surgeon had stopped his habitual pacing. His heavy, bearded face was illuminated by the amber indicator lights of the O-ASIS console. In his left hand, he held his small brass kitchen timer, motionless. “What is the third trial, Liang?”

“Somatic self-abnegation,” Chen said.

The phrase fell into the freezing room like lead shot.

“The candidate is given absolute executive discretion over a simulated regional grid facing total collapse,” Chen continued, stepping forward until his knuckles rested on the table’s edge. “Every incentive is offered to preserve their own status, their own faction, their own life. To pass, the candidate must demonstrate the willingness to sacrifice their own administrative authority the precise moment the statistical equilibrium demands it. Only those who score in the ninety-ninth percentile across all three metrics are certified.”

“Certified for what?” Miriam’s pen hovered.

“For sortition,” Chen said softly.

The room stirred. Kenjiro Sato, who had spent the morning clearing the slush dam from the geothermal intake line, wiped his grease-blackened hands on a rag and looked up, his one good ear cocked toward Chen.

“You don't eliminate the lot,” Sato said, his voice a low rasp from years of foundry dust. “You prune the tree before the wind blows.”

“Exactly,” Chen said, meeting the machinist’s gaze. “We preserve the purity of statistical selection, but we eradicate the catastrophic flaw of uncurated democracy. We do not allow the sociopath, the demagogue, the tribal warlord, or the panic-stricken hoarder to enter the urn. Every legislative seat—from the village irrigation council to the cantonal logistics board—is drawn strictly by lot, but drawn *only* from the pool of certified Sages.”

Julian stepped toward the O-ASIS console, flicking the mechanical toggle switches of the Baudot interface. The machine chattered—a sharp, staccato burst of metal hammers striking carbon paper.

“We ran six hundred iterations of the Demagogue Vector,” Julian announced, holding up the punched tape like an anatomical specimen. “Under pure, uncurated sortition, a charismatic actor leveraging water scarcity takes control of the municipal nodes in seventy-two percent of runs within eighteen months. When we apply the Psychometric Certification filter? Zero. Not a single authoritarian consolidation across ten thousand simulated years. The coordination curves remain flat. The grid survives.”

Silence returned to the vault, but it was no longer heavy with despair. It was the breathless, suspended quiet of miners who had just broken through a granite wall to find an open vein of gold.

Tariq picked up the thermal neuro-map, his fingers tracing the faint red contours of an amygdala response curve. His breath hitched. “No charismatic generals. No party bosses. No backroom financing.”

“No hereditary nobility,” Chen added, his voice dropping to a near whisper, rich with an intellectual pride that had spent generations fermenting in the damp earth of his lineage. “The exam is open to every human being on earth from their eighteenth year onward. A farmer’s daughter from the Sahel has the exact same baseline odds as a university professor from Zurich. The machine does not see skin, or wealth, or dialect. It sees only the clarity of the mind and the capacity to bear the burden of the whole.”

Nayra Calvimontes reached across the table. Her brown hands, rough from childhood spent shearing alpaca fleece in the dry wind of the altiplano, touched the edge of Chen’s calligraphed parchment. She looked up at him, her dark eyes reflecting the yellow kerosene wick.

“If my father had taken this test,” Nayra whispered, “the mining syndicates could never have bought the water council. They could never have bribed the sortition pool with grain sacks.”

“They couldn't touch it,” Julian said, his cracked lips parting in a fragile, triumphant smile. “The code is blind. The psychometrics are invariant.”

Chen felt the cold knot of tension in his neck loosen. For seventeen days, this vault had been an abattoir of competing ideologies—Miriam’s legalist paranoia clashing with Tariq’s populist fury, Koffi’s clinical caution grinding against Sato’s unyielding materialism. They had screamed until their throats bled, thrown rations, threatened to trip the airlock releases.

Now, they leaned inward.

Eva Lindqvist, her shoulders broad and rigid beneath her thermal coveralls, picked up a piece of chalk and drew a clean, perfect circle on the blackboard behind the teletype. Inside it, she wrote two words in Swedish, then crossed them out and wrote in English: *THE MERIT OF SOULS*.

“It bridges everything,” Miriam murmured. She laid her fountain pen down flat on her legal pad—the first time she had set it down since Day Three. Her eyes were unfocused, looking through the granite walls toward a future that suddenly seemed coherent. “It satisfies the Western demand for objective scientific verification, and the Eastern demand for ethical virtue in leadership. It isn't a tyranny of the majority, and it isn't an oligarchy.”

“It is the rule of the best,” Chen said. His migraine seemed to recede, washed away by a cold, luminous surge of vindication. “Not by birth, but by nature.”

Around the zinc table, shoulders relaxed. Koffi set his brass timer down with a gentle, metallic tap. Tariq poured the remaining hot chicory into Julian’s empty cup without a word of mockery. Ananya leaned back against the iron bulkhead, her eyes closing in what looked like the first genuine rest she had allowed herself since Mumbai.

For the first time since the heavy vault doors had sealed behind them, Station 74-Nord felt warm. A profound, intoxicating serenity washed over the ten delegates—the euphoric certainty that after two centuries of democratic decay and mechanical slaughter, they had solved the fatal flaw of human governance.

Chen Liang sat back down against the vertical iron rib of the wall, his hands resting flat upon his knees.

They had built a machine that could crown the righteous.

And for twenty-four hours, not a single soul in the deep Arctic frost thought to ask who would program the mirror that judged the mind.
