The ink from Miriam’s fountain pen did not dry; it congealed. In the eight-degree chill of the mess hall, the dark German pigment thickened on the tooth of the yellow legal pad, dragging against the gold nib until every letter felt like a small trench excavated under duress.

Yet she could not stop writing. For the first time in eighteen days, her hand was not trembling with rage.

"Weight the situational integrity battery at thirty percent," Tariq said, his voice unusually light as he leaned across the zinc mess table, steam from a fresh cup of roasted chicory warming his sallow cheeks. He used a butter knife to pin down the fluttering carbon sheets. "If an administrator faces an asymmetric bribe—say, grain diversion during a drought—the psychometric array has to register systemic empathy over institutional loyalty. We penalize obedience to illegitimate authority."

"Already penned," Miriam murmured, her breath plume rising in the lantern light. "Clause Nine, Subsection B: *Refusal of coercive command constitutes an affirmative ethical signal.*"

Across the table, Eva Lindqvist laughed—a brief, musical sound that had been entirely absent from Station 74-Nord since the heavy vault airlock sealed behind them. Eva was whittling a fresh stick of chalk down to a chisel point with her pocketknife, shavings curling like birch bark onto her knees. "We are actually doing it," she said, looking up at the blackboard where the schematic of the Neo-Confucian sorting matrix was sketched in towering, interlocking arcs. "A civic filter that cannot be flattered, bought, or intimidated by a family name. A civil service chosen by virtue and moral perception."

The mess hall had the frantic, exhausted gaiety of a field hospital after a victorious counter-offensive. Above them, the brass chronometer ticked toward `288:00:00`—the Svalbard freeze-up countdown shaving away their final hours before the fjord hardened into permanent ice—but for once, the red numerals seemed distant, almost irrelevant. Min-Jun Kang was asleep with his forehead pressed against a stack of punched teletype paper, his breathing deep and even for the first time in ninety hours. Julian Mercer sat near the catalytic scrubber vent, sipping chicory, color returning to his hollowed cheeks as he nodded along with Chen Liang’s quiet, methodical cadence.

"It eliminates the demagogue at the gate," Chen was explaining to Julian, his back straighter than it had been all week. "The narcissist fails the empathy battery. The sociopath trips the cognitive dissonance threshold in the scenario simulations. Only those capable of enduring grief without weaponizing it are admitted to the lottery."

Miriam dipped her nib into the well. As a girl in Antwerp, her father—a diamond cutter whose precision was legendary across the Pelikaanstraat—had taught her to examine stones under a ten-power loupe, looking not for brilliance, but for the invisible structural cleavages that would cause a gem to shatter under the lapidary wheel. *The flaw is always quietest where the light gathers brightest,* he had told her, tap-tapping a steel mallet against raw carbon. She had carried that lesson into appellate courtrooms and constitutional draft rooms for twenty-five years, distrusting every consensus that felt too clean, every compromise that brought smiles to exhausted faces.

Yet looking at the rubric taking shape across the yellow pages, she felt an unfamiliar, intoxicating warmth loosen the permanent ache between her shoulder blades. It was elegant. It solved the ancient, bleeding rot of human politics: how to prevent power from selecting for the ruthless. By filtering sortition candidates through an objective, algorithmic crucible of moral discernment, they were not building an aristocracy of blood or wealth, but an aristocracy of conscience.

"Pass the chicory, Tariq," Miriam said, her lips curling into an uncharacteristic, dry grin. "Before Sato drinks the pot dry."

Sato didn't look up from the bench where he was re-seating a copper gasket on the spare kerosene burner, but a faint, approving grunt escaped his throat.

"The Latin is wrong in your preamble, by the way," Tariq teased, sliding the tin pot across the scratched zinc. "You wrote *lex incorruptibilis*. It’s pretentious, Miriam. Even for a Hague litigator."

"It’s precise," she countered, pouring the bitter, scorched brew into her enamel mug. "Precision is the only shield against bureaucrats with creative vocabularies."

Around the circle, chuckles rippled through the cold air. Julian leaned forward, pointing with a cracked fingernail at a sub-clause on Miriam's pad. "And the validation rounds? We randomize the proctors through the optical processor so no single academic cabal can coach candidates?"

"Completely blind," Eva affirmed, dusting chalk from her woolen sleeve. "The machine generates the ethical scenarios in real-time. Ten thousand branches. No two applicants ever face the same dilemma."

The room hummed with that rare, dangerous electricity that comes when brilliant people convince themselves they have outsmarted human tragedy. They were intoxicated by their own relief.

In the far corner, near the unlit auxiliary conduit where the temperature hovered barely above freezing, two women had not spoken for three hours.

Ananya Patel sat on an overturned wooden cable spool, her right leg stretched out stiff to ease the throbbing fluid behind her patella. Her hands were tucked deep into the fleece-lined pockets of her parka, her shoulders drawn up against the granite wall. She had not touched her ration tin. The biscuit and salted lard had gone gray and hard on the lid.

Beside her, perched on a rusted tool chest, Nayra Calvimontes was weaving a strip of undyed alpaca cord through her knuckles, over and under, over and under, her fingers moving with the rhythmic, mechanical speed of an Andean loom. Her dark eyes were fixed not on the blackboard, nor on the bustling legal drafting at the table, but on the floor between Miriam’s boots.

Miriam’s pen slowed. The festive warmth in the center of the mess hall suddenly felt fragile, like an air pocket trapped beneath Arctic ice.

She looked toward Dr. Koffi Adebayo. The trauma surgeon was leaning against the steel bulkhead halfway between the table and the corner, his brass kitchen timer held loosely in his right palm. He wasn't participating in the banter. His gaze shifted back and forth between Tariq’s laughing gestures and the rigid line of Ananya’s jaw.

Koffi caught Miriam’s eye. His face remained an impassive mask, but the subtle downward tilt of his chin was a warning she had learned to read during their worst deadlocks in Week One: *Watch.*

"We need a final provision for the psychological appeal process," Eva said, entirely unaware of the shift in the room's pressure, her voice ringing off the damp iron overhead. "In case an applicant claims somatic distress impaired their ethical testing score. We should establish a three-member review tribunal."

"No tribunals," Tariq insisted, shaking his head. "Tribunals are where favors get traded. The psychometric score must be absolute. The algorithm sees the mind as it is, not as the applicant wishes it to be."

"It has to be final," Julian agreed softly, his voice trembling with the fervor of a convert. "If we allow discretionary appeals, we let the old world slip through the cracks. The rubric is the moral baseline."

"The rubric," a voice cut through the air.

It was not loud, but it possessed the hard, dry weight of a stone dropped into an empty zinc bucket.

The chatter died instantly. Tariq’s laugh hung half-formed in his throat, then vanished.

Ananya had not shifted her position on the cable spool, but her eyes had lifted. They were bloodshot, rimmed with the deep, bruising purple of chronic sleeplessness, but her stare was razor-sharp, pinning Miriam to her chair.

"Read Clause Four aloud, Miriam," Ananya said quietly. Her voice was slightly hoarse from the bunker dust, stripped of all ornament. "Subsection Three. The one you just drafted with Tariq. Read it."

Miriam tightened her grip on her fountain pen, the congealing ink sticky against her index finger. "Ananya, we’re establishing the cognitive stability requirements for sortition eligibility. It ensures—"

"Read the words," Ananya repeated.

Miriam swallowed, the chicory suddenly tasting like ash at the back of her throat. She looked down at the fresh black lines on the yellow pad.

"'Candidates exhibiting acute indicators of behavioral instability, persecutory fixation, or chronic somatic stress reactions following civil disturbance shall be remanded to secondary restorative pools until biometric equilibrium is restored.'"

A silence settled over Station 74-Nord, heavier and colder than the granite pressed against their skulls. The low, thrumming vibration of the geothermal intake lines beneath the floorboards seemed to amplify, rattling the tin cups on the table.

Nayra stopped weaving her cord.

Koffi took half a step backward, his back pressing against the cold steel rib of the wall, his brass timer clicking once as his thumb brushed the winding key.

Ananya pushed her hands against the cable spool. Slowly, using her good left leg to take her weight, she rose. Her right knee let out a sickening, wet pop that echoed clearly in the hushed vault. She did not wince. She did not reach for the bulkhead to steady herself.

She took one limping, measured stride forward, then another, the rubber soles of her work boots dragging against the salt-crusted concrete. She walked directly into the warm yellow circle of the lantern light, stopping at the edge of the zinc table, towering over Miriam’s legal pad.

Her scarred hand reached down. Her fingers, dark and callused from years of street organizing and industrial salt yards, closed around the edge of the draft exam.

She lifted the yellow sheets into the air between them.
