The lining of Tariq Al-Fassi’s stomach felt as though someone had swabbed it with battery acid. He pressed the heel of his thumb hard into the soft dip beneath his ribs, trying to squash the burn, but the bitter sludge of chicory and instant granules he’d gulped down at midnight offered no mercy. Across the zinc table, three kerosene lamps flickered against the damp draft, their yellow light catching the condensation weeping down the granite bulkhead.

The geothermal intake had cleared twenty-four hours ago, and the subterranean freeze had retreated to a sullen, clammy six degrees, but the air was foul. It carried the wet-dog reek of damp wool, the scorched-sugar tang of the analog scrubbers, and the alkaline dust of pulverized stone.

On the table between them lay Miriam Levin’s yellow legal pads, their margins blackened with forensic cross-examinations of dead republics.

“It is never a coup,” Miriam said, her voice dry as dead reeds. Her knuckles were swollen, red-rimmed from cold, her right forefinger permanently stained with Prussian blue ink from her fountain pen. She didn’t look up from her notes; she simply dragged the metal ruler down another column of handwritten dates. “People imagine tanks rolling across the plaza at dawn. They imagine bayonets. It almost never happens that way. In ninety-one percent of systemic collapses, the executioner is invited inside by an absolute legislative majority.”

She tapped the nib against the paper—*tick, tick, tick*—a sharp, mechanical percussion that scraped against Tariq’s raw nerves.

“Weimar, March twenty-third, nineteen thirty-three,” she murmured, turning a page with a violent rustle that sounded like dry leaves. “The Reichstag sat inside the Kroll Opera House because the primary chamber was a charred shell. Four hundred and forty-one votes in favor. Ninety-four against. A four-to-one supermajority of duly elected representatives freely signed the suicide pact of parliamentary democracy. They used the constitution’s own emergency decree mechanism—Article Forty-Eight—to incinerate the constitution itself. They did it with applause.”

“They had brownshirts lined up in the aisles, Miriam,” Min-Jun rasped from the corner. He was curled inside his fleece liner, his knees pulled up to his chest, fingers trembling as he turned an unlit cigarette over and over between his knuckles. He had not slept in two days; his eyelids twitched with involuntary micro-spasms. “There were carbines behind the podium. That is coercion.”

“No,” Tariq interrupted, the word tearing out of his throat before he could swallow it down.

The table went quiet.

Tariq looked down at his own trembling hands. His fingernails were split from hauling lead ballast bricks the day before, gray stone dust ground permanently into the cuticles. As a boy in the Karrada district of Baghdad, when the car bombs shook the soot loose from the plaster ceilings, his father—a man who measured out sulfa drugs and codeine in delicate brass pans—had taught him that terror was rarely an outside invader. Terror was the cousin who knocked on your door at dusk carrying a clipboard.

“Min-Jun is wrong,” Tariq said, his voice flat, stripped of academic ornament. “The guns only keep the doors shut. The slaughter begins when the crowd wants the slaughter. In Sadr City, after the blast at Al-Askari, we didn't have an occupying army forcing us into factional councils. We held neighborhood assemblies. We voted by acclamation in the courtyards under the date palms.”

He closed his eyes, and the ghost was right there, sitting behind his eyelids in the dark.

“There was an engineer,” Tariq whispered. “A man named Kadhim. He had a soft voice, clean fingernails, and a silver ring with an agate stone on his right thumb. He didn’t scream. He didn't carry an assault rifle. He brought three flatbed trucks loaded with purified drinking water and bags of long-grain rice when the municipal pumps failed. He stood on an oil drum and spoke for twenty minutes. Not about theology. About hygiene. About the rot in the water mains. He told us that the cholera taking our babies was not a failure of engineering, but an orchestrated assault by the Sunni families two streets north.”

Tariq opened his eyes. The yellow lamplight threw grotesque, elongated shadows of their ten silhouettes against the raw stone vault.

“The assembly cheered him,” Tariq said, his stomach giving another vicious, burning spasm. “They hoisted him onto their shoulders. Three days later, Kadhim’s militia was hauling fourteen-year-old boys out of bakeries and shooting them in the knee-caps behind the brick kilns. And the mothers who had taken the rice stood on their balconies and ululated in celebration. That was not coercion, Min-Jun. That was an election.”

Silence settled over the table like fine silt.

In the far corner, near the hum of the battery banks, Koffi Adebayo sat with his elbows on his knees, staring into the low flame of his brass kitchen timer. The clockwork mechanism inside it clicked with slow, indifferent precision.

“Majoritarianism,” Koffi said quietly, his deep voice vibrating through the duckboards beneath their feet, “is simply the ritualization of the mob. If fifty-one percent of a sortition council decides that the remaining forty-nine percent are an existential threat to the grain reserves, the mathematics of democracy will happily sanction an extermination camp. Eloquence is a biological weapon. A charismatic speaker can flood a room with oxytocin and tribal panic in eight minutes. We are primates. We are wired to follow the alpha who promises to butcher the wolf at the gate.”

“Then pure sortition is a slaughterhouse waiting for a butcher,” Min-Jun whispered, his voice cracking. He dropped the unlit cigarette into his cold tea. “If you draw three hundred citizens at random from the street, you do not get angels. You get the herd. You get the fearful, the illiterate, the easily terrified. You get people who will hand the master keys to the first silver-tongued sociopath who promises them that their heat won't be cut off.”

Nayra Calvimontes leaned forward, her dark braid pulled over one shoulder. Her thumb worked the rough alpaca cord tied around her wrist, knotting and unknotting the fiber. “In El Alto, when the water tanks ran dry, we did not trust the mayors. We did not trust the parliament. We trusted the water committees because every delegate had to drink from the same cistern as their neighbors. But even there... when the supply trucks were intercepted by the military, the assemblies turned. A man would stand on the cab of a tractor, shout that the miners from Oruro were stealing our rations, and within an hour, people who had shared bread for twenty years were burning tires in front of each other’s houses.”

She looked across at Tariq, her dark eyes hollowed by the vault’s relentless halogen glow. “The fear makes you deaf to reason. It makes you hungry for a master.”

Miriam set her fountain pen down with a sharp click. The heavy gold nib left a single, spreading black blot on the yellow paper.

“Then we have failed before we have begun,” she said, her voice dropping into an icy register that chilled Tariq far more than the Arctic stone. “If an assembly drawn by lot can be captured by a silver tongue—if sixty percent of an unvetted council can suspend the baseline charters in a moment of manufactured terror—then the architecture is rotten at the foundation. A constitution that cannot survive its own citizenry is nothing more than poetry written on wet cardboard.”

Tariq pressed both hands flat against the cold zinc of the table, breathing through his teeth as another hot wave of bile rose in his throat. Across from him, Julian Mercer sat hunched in his grease-stained parka. Julian’s hands, wrapped in strips of clean white linen over raw, blistered knuckles, rested motionless beside a stack of five-level punched tape. His eyes were downcast, bloodshot, his pupils dilated from exhaustion and beta-blockers.

Next to him, Dr. Chen Liang had not spoken for three hours.

Chen sat with his spine pressed flat against the vertical iron rib of the bulkhead, a posture so rigid it looked like an orthopedic brace. A vein pulsed visibly against his right temple, dark and swollen under paper-thin skin—the telltale signature of the low-oxygen migraines that had plagued him since they sealed the airlocks.

Then, Chen moved.

The chair legs shrieked against the concrete deck plates as he stood. The sound was like a bone snapping in the damp confines of the vault.

Every head turned.

Chen reached into the breast pocket of his wool overcoat and withdrew a thick sheaf of folded parchment, densely marked in fine, carbon-black calligraphy. He did not drop it; he placed it squarely in the dead center of the table, right between Miriam’s legal pad and Tariq’s cold, scummed mug of chicory.

“Julian and I have spent the last forty-eight hours stress-testing the O-ASIS demagogue vectors,” Chen said. His voice was clipped, devoid of fatigue, carrying the chilling, surgical clarity of a man who had already buried his own doubts. “A purely statistical assembly will fall to rhetorical capture within four simulation cycles under conditions of hydrological or caloric stress. It is a mathematical certainty. You cannot insulate a mob from charisma through procedural rules alone.”

Miriam’s eyes narrowed. “Then what are you proposing, Liang? A veto council? A military junta?”

“No,” Chen said softly. He stepped around the table, his shadow blotting out the kerosene light across Tariq’s notes. “We do not give power to the mob, and we do not give it to the generals. We filter the pool before the lot is ever drawn.”

He tapped the edge of the parchment with a single, manicured fingernail.

“A tiered, universal psychometric and ethical gauntlet,” Chen announced, his gaze sweeping the room like a cold searchlight. “Julian and I have engineered the invariant counterweight. The governance of the nodes will belong only to those who have demonstrated cognitive immunity to tribalism, rhetorical manipulation, and panic.”

Chen looked directly down into Tariq’s eyes, his expression carved from frozen granite.

“We call it the Exam of the Sages.”
