The iron rim of the sluice wheel bit through Marcus’s sodden work gloves, cold enough to tear skin. He let go, stumbling back a half-step across the wet granite of the quay. His breath tore out of his chest in ragged, white plumes.

“Elena,” he said into the radio, his voice scraped raw from the river air. “Say that again.”

Static chewed at the frequency before her reply punched through, brittle and thin: “Aegis-Apex didn't pull back when the hydro-gate tripped. They bypassed cantonal authority entirely. The Debt Consortium’s legal desk just invoked an emergency admiralty lien over the entire international territory. They’re moving heavy armor up the Avenue de la Paix.”

Carlos Quispe was already beside him, wiping grey river spray from his eyes. The Bolivian miner’s chest heaved in the damp air; his lungs, long accustomed to the thin, biting freeze of four thousand meters, wheezed softly against the heavy moisture of the lake basin. “Armor?” Carlos muttered, his dark eyes fixing on the green column of light stabbing upward into the clouds. “Against an assembly of unarmed civilians?”

“They don't see an assembly,” Marcus said, turning toward the stone stairwell that led up to the street. “They see their ledgers burning.”

They ran.

Every step up the granite incline sent a dull spike of pain through Marcus’s left hip—the old assembly-line torque that had plagued him for twenty years, flared white-hot by thirty hours without sleep. Above them, the city didn't sound like a victory. It sounded like an iron lung struggling to draw breath. The deep, rolling toll of the Saint-Pierre bell had dissolved into the freezing drizzle, replaced by a low, gut-churning rumble that rattled the sash windows of the old stone apartments facing the water.

It was the specific, mechanical vibration of heavy tracked vehicles: twelve-cylinder diesels turning low revolutions, their planetary gearboxes grinding against asphalt not built to bear sixty tons of steel.

Two hundred thousand people stood between the quays and the gates of the Palais des Nations. They were not a mob. They were a dense, living forest of heavy wool coats, reflective rain ponchos, and knitted caps, packed shoulder to shoulder in the freezing fog. Thermoses were passed hand to hand; cardboard cups of hot chicory water moved from strangers to strangers. When Marcus and Carlos shoved through the fringes of the crowd, people yielded with the quiet, stubborn solidarity of those who knew that any sudden panic would crush them against the wrought-iron fences.

At the intersection where the Rue de Lausanne met the Place des Nations, the crowd stopped.

Three hundred meters ahead, blocking the grand boulevard, sat three heavy armored recovery vehicles and two main battle tanks, their hulls painted the matte, slate-grey of the cantonal territorial reserve. Their exhaust louvers vented thick, oily clouds of unburned diesel into the sub-zero air, the sweet, sickening stench of hydrocarbons smothering the clean smell of snow. Behind the armored tracks, fifty private contractors from Aegis-Apex stood in full composite shock-gear, their faceplates mirrored, carbines slung across their chests on single-point harnesses.

Inside the rear compartment of a tracked command carrier fifty yards behind the line, Arthur Perrin did not feel victorious. He felt the cold creeping through the soles of his bespoke leather boots, numbing his toes.

Perrin, senior managing director for the European Sovereign Liquidity Desk, had his gloved fingers clamped around a hard-shell military tablet. On the screen, a red line plummeted straight down into an abyss—the secondary market for continental sovereign debt had ceased to exist the moment the Saint-Pierre bell finished its twelfth stroke.

“Major,” Perrin said, his voice flat, razor-edged with terror. He leaned over the shoulder of the Swiss auxiliary commander sitting in the carrier’s jump seat. “The warrant has Hague emergency clearance. The sortition hall inside that compound is currently hosting an illegal expropriation of sovereign wealth. You have your clearing order. Drive through the south gate.”

Major Lucian Varga did not turn around. He was forty-six years old, with short-cropped iron-grey hair and the leathery, wind-burned skin of a man who spent his winters on high-altitude alpine artillery ranges. His thumb rested lightly on the transmit toggle of his headset.

“The south gate is occupied, Monsieur Perrin,” Varga said. His vocal cords sounded like dry gravel being crushed under a heel.

“By an unlawful assembly!” Perrin spat. A fleck of spit hit the edge of the tactical monitor. “Do you know what happens at eight o’clock tomorrow morning when the Frankfurt clearinghouse opens and finds six trillion euros in debt obligations scrubbed by an anonymous lottery of schoolteachers and bus drivers? The commercial paper market freezes. Supply chains for grain, municipal fuel, insulin—everything halts within seventy-two hours. My mandate is the prevention of systemic social collapse. Push the barricade.”

Varga slowly raised his eyes to the viewport.

Through the multi-spectral thermal screen, the mass of humanity wasn't a tactical obstacle. It was a single, vast lake of ambient body heat—orange and gold against the dead, blue-black frost of the Geneva pavement. There were no barricades of overturned cars. No wire. Just men and women, three deep, sitting cross-legged on the frozen ground before the iron gates, their arms linked through the sleeves of their winter coats.

Directly in front of the lead tank’s steel dozer blade, an elderly woman in an emerald-green knitted scarf was drinking tea from a steel camping flask. She hadn't even looked up when the tank’s turbine had spooled up two minutes ago.

“Look at your display, Major,” Perrin hissed, leaning closer, the smell of expensive coffee and sour panic rolling off his breath. “The federal council signed the cantonal requisition ten minutes ago under Article Fifty-Eight. Aegis has tactical lead. Your unit is legally indemnified.”

“Article Fifty-Eight applies to foreign military invasion or armed insurrection,” Varga said softly.

“This *is* an insurrection!”

“No,” Varga said, his gaze fixed on the woman with the thermos. “An insurrection shoots back. Those are cantonal taxpayers holding library cards.”

The Aegis liaison officer standing by the open hatchway racked the bolt of his sidearm with a sharp, ugly *clack*. “Major, if your crews won't depress the throttle, my contractors will operate the forward vehicles under field requisition protocols. Step aside.”

Varga’s hand didn't move toward his service holster. He didn't raise his voice. He simply keyed his company-wide net, the open channel connecting the five armored hulls idling on the boulevard.

“All stations, this is Iron Seven,” Varga said, his voice dropping into the steady, mechanical cadence of standard field doctrine. “Status check on auxiliary hydraulic systems.”

Over the crackling tactical radio, the voice of a twenty-two-year-old sergeant from Valais answered from the lead tank: “Iron One... line pressure nominal, sir. But... Commander, the crowd isn't breaking. We revved the plant to nineteen hundred RPM. They’re singing. I don't know what it is. Church music, maybe.”

“Sergeant,” Varga said, ignoring Perrin’s sudden, sharp intake of breath. “What is your engine block temperature?”

“Running hot, sir. Low-speed idle in freezing sleet is choking the radiator intakes. If we drop the plow and push against dead weight, the torque converters will shear within fifty meters.”

It was a lie. A competent mechanic knew a sixty-ton transmission wouldn't shear against human bone and down coats. But it was a technical assertion logged on a military black box.

Perrin grabbed Varga’s shoulder. “I will have your commission stripped before dawn, you coward! You have an order from the Federal Department of Finance!”

Varga turned his head. His grey eyes were entirely dead. He reached up, peeled Perrin’s manicured fingers off his combat jacket, and pushed the director back into the steel bulkhead with a single, firm thrust of his forearm.

“The Department of Finance does not hold the firing keys to a tank, Monsieur,” Varga said.

He leaned back toward the radio. “All stations. This is Seven. Neutral gear. Cut main drives. Lock track brakes. Disembark crews to secure vehicle perimeters with non-lethal side-arms only. Nobody moves forward.”

Outside on the avenue, the deafening scream of the turbine engines suddenly died.

The silence that followed was terrifying. It rushed into the vacuum like cold water pouring into an empty hull. The huge steel monsters shuddered as their fuel pumps cut out, the smell of cooling metal rising into the mist.

Marcus reached the front rank of the crowd just as the heavy steel hatches atop the lead vehicle clanged open.

A young corporal stood up in the turret. He wasn't wearing his helmet. His hair was matted with sweat despite the cold, his face pale, his eyes darting across the sea of faces staring back at him in utter silence.

For four seconds, nobody breathed. Two hundred thousand people held their collective wind in their throats.

Then, from the pocket of his combat smock, the young corporal pulled out a personal smartphone. Its screen glowed in the dark—a live broadcast feed showing the assembly floor inside the Palais. On the screen, an anonymous sortition delegate from an agricultural cooperative in Kenya was dropping a wooden token into a bronze urn, the cryptographic hash resolving on eight billion consumer screens simultaneously.

The soldier looked down at the old woman with the thermos. He looked at Marcus, whose hands were still black with grease from the sluice gate.

The soldier did not draw his weapon. He slowly lowered himself back down onto the cold steel hull, pulled his collar up against the sleet, and sat there, his boots dangling over the unmoving tracks.

A breath broke from the crowd—a low, seismic murmur of absolute relief that seemed to shake the very frost from the plane trees.

Marcus felt his knees buckle. Carlos caught him by the elbow, hauling his weight back up.

“It held,” Carlos whispered, his voice cracking. “Marcus... the soldiers held.”

Marcus pulled his radio up, his thumb shaking against the button. “Elena. The avenue is blocked. The tanks shut down. The line is holding.”

There was no cheer from the receiver.

Only the frantic, rapid-fire clatter of mechanical keys, and Elena’s voice, hollowed out by pure horror:

“Marcus... get inside the compound now.”

“Elena, what is it? The military stood down—”

“The Consortium anticipated Varga’s refusal,” she gasped, her words tumbling over each other in an avalanche of static. “Perrin wasn't the main play. While we were watching the avenue, three automated cargo barges flagged under Panamanian maritime registry cut their transponders and ran dark through the harbor lock. They’ve just beached themselves against the north water intake of the Palais.”

Marcus froze, his heart slamming against his ribs. “What are they carrying?”

“They’re not carrying troops, Marcus,” Elena whispered. Her voice broke entirely. “They’re carrying industrial liquid nitrogen from the chemical docks. They're flooding the Palais’s sub-basement cooling tunnels right now. In four minutes, the cryogenic thermal shock will shatter the fiber-optic trunks housing the entire master ledger.”
