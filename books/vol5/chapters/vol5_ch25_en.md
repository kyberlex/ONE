The ascent up the Avenue de la Paix was not a march; it was the slow, tectonic displacement of a quarter-million bodies moving through freezing slush.

Marcus kept his hands loose at his sides, his woolen gloves stiff with road-salt. Beside him, Carlos walked with an awkward, heavy-heeled gait, his boots sliding on the glazed asphalt. The Chilean took short, ragged sips of the thick lake air, his chest heaving under a borrowed corduroy jacket. After twenty-six years in the thin, dry vacuum of the Atacama, Geneva’s sea-level pressure felt like wet flannel pressed against his mouth, bringing on a dizzying rush of oxygen that left him coughing in low, dry spasms.

Ahead, the high iron gates of the Pregny entrance hung open, abandoned by the cantonal gendarmes who had parked their blue-and-white vans bumper-to-bumper across the southbound lanes. But five hundred paces beyond, where the gravel swept up to the monumental granite terrace of the Palais des Nations, the grand bronze entrance was dead.

A cordon of Aegis-Apex contractors in matte-slate ballistic plate stood three deep along the terrace stairs. They carried no riot shields or clubs. They didn't need them. Behind their line, across the three double-leaf bronze doors of the Assembly Hall, two heavy hydraulic trench-jacks were braced horizontally between the stone pilasters. The jacks were industrial excavation shoring units, their twin steel pistons pressurized to forty tons, locking the five-ton bronze leaves so rigidly into their jambs that the surrounding masonry showed hairline fractures in the mortar.

A single contractor stood on the top landing, apart from the squad. He wore an unadorned Gore-Tex field coat over a dark suit, an encrypted ear-piece looped behind his left ear. He was fifty, maybe fifty-five, with close-cropped salt-and-pepper hair and the calm, posture-perfect stance of a former maritime logistics officer.

"Commander Brandt," Elena said quietly, her breath clouding Marcus’s shoulder. Her tablet was dead, tucked into her inner coat pocket to save the remaining battery cell, but her memory held the entire clearinghouse dossier. "Head of sovereign security underwriting for the Basel debt syndicate. He’s not a mercenary. He’s a bonded trustee of the bondholders’ liquidation committee."

Brandt did not raise a weapon. He held a laminated cantonal requisition order against his chest like a ledger.

"Mr. Hayes," Brandt’s voice was amplified through a small directional megaphone clipped to his lapel, dry and devoid of theatrical malice. "The grounds of the League are extraterritorial assets under protective receivership. By decree of the G20 Financial Stabilization Board, all assembly spaces are sealed pending liquidation of sovereign debt defaults. Your gathering is unlawful under cantonal emergency code four."

From the front rank of the marchers, four dockworkers from Marseille lunged forward onto the lower steps. One of them, a broad-shouldered man named Renard whose knuckles were stained with marine diesel, swung a five-foot hexagonal track-jack off his shoulder. He slammed the forged chisel-tip into the granite riser, the metal ringing like a church bell.

"Ten seconds, Marcus," Renard growled, his jaw clamped tight, spittle freezing on his scarf. "We set this under the lower ram, catch the bypass valve, and drop the pressure. The seals pop. We’re in."

Two more men stepped up, hauling an eighteen-pound forged splitting bar. Their eyes were wild, bloodshot with two days of sleepless driving from the coast. Behind them, the crowd surged forward a half-step, a collective intake of breath that sounded like surf dragging across shale.

"Back off," Marcus said.

Renard didn’t move. His boots dug into the iced gravel. "There are a thousand people freezing to death on this hill, Hayes. The heat is on inside that glass. We take the door."

"Renard. Step back."

Marcus didn't raise his voice, but the flat, gravelly bite in it stopped the younger man’s hands on the bar. Marcus knew the look in Brandt’s eyes. He had seen it fourteen years earlier on a cold November morning in Detroit, when the plant receiver had chained the emergency turnstiles on the axle-stamping floor. Marcus had stood with forty wildcat strikers holding oxyacetylene torches, ready to cut the links, until he had spotted the private insurance adjusters parked across the boulevard in an unmarked sedan, their telephoto lenses trained on the hinges. The moment an iron tooth bit into that steel, the entire pension strike had ceased to be an industrial dispute; it had become an uninsurable act of trespass and domestic property destruction, legally forfeiting forty million dollars in survivor benefits with the stroke of a bailiff’s pen.

The law of the old ledger was never a wall. It was a tripwire.

"Look at the telemetry cameras under the portico cornice," Marcus said, his voice dropping into the cold air between them. "The line is tethered to the sovereign default clearinghouse in Zurich. The millisecond you strike that hydraulic housing with an iron tool, it registers as an armed kinetic breach of sovereign territory. Brandt doesn't even have to give an order. The automated clearing protocol triggers an immediate default forfeiture. The Hague Perpetual Trust is declared null. The sortition charter becomes a riot docket."

Renard’s chest hitched. He looked at the heavy hexagonal bar in his gloved hands, then up at Brandt.

Brandt had not flinched. The megaphone at his chest remained level, his face as detached as an actuary calculating the mortality curve of a sinking tanker.

"He's right, son," Captain Jin Wei said from Marcus's flank. The old mariner leaned heavily on a stout ash walking stick, his sea-worn knees trembling slightly against the sub-zero chill. "You don't ram a closed lock-gate when the canal authority has the flood-sluices chained from the tower. You only wreck your own bow, and the clerk charges you for the masonry."

Renard swore through his teeth, a short, ugly curse that plumed in the frost, but he dragged the iron bar back three feet and grounded it in the gravel.

Marcus turned his back on the sealed doors. He faced the terrace, looking down across the sweeping lawn toward the lake.

The front rank of the crowd had halted fifty paces back, holding their small glass jars. Inside each, a candle burned, the collective glow painting the snow an amber, living gold. Further back, rising in dense, quiet tiers down to the water’s edge, two hundred thousand people stood in the rising fog. High on the rail gantry at the foot of the hill, the digital public transit clock showed the local time in sharp green diodes: *06:41:12*. The temperature was minus three. The fuel in the city’s municipal backup generators was scheduled to drop below operating pressure by noon.

"Carlos," Marcus said.

The miner stepped up, his breath rattling in his throat as he unzipped his woolen parka. From inside, he drew the heavy, milled-aluminum token of the Class 0 Sortition Assembly—the master seal of the 1,001 delegates, cold and unpolished, bearing no crest or seal, only the laser-etched parity coordinates of four continents.

"No levers," Marcus said to the crowd, his voice carrying clearly across the silent terrace. "No iron. We didn't cross the ocean to break into our own house."

He walked to the bottom step of the grand granite staircase, three paces below the lowest line of Aegis contractors. He took off his woolen gloves, shoved them into his pockets, and sat down directly on the frozen stone.

Carlos looked at him for a second, a faint, wry twitch touching the corner of his wind-burned lips. He sat down beside him, crossing his heavy legs in the slush and resting the aluminum token squarely on his knees.

Giulia was next, her winter coat sweeping over the wet gravel as she took her place to Marcus’s left, her face pale under the streetlights, followed by Elena, then Jin Wei, who eased himself down with a low grunt, resting both hands over the head of his ash stick.

Behind them, the 1,001 sortition delegates—farmers from the Rhône basin, machinists from Turin, rail switchers from the Rhine valley, nurses from Lisbon and Valparaíso—began to sit. They moved without a signal or a chant. In less than three minutes, the entire monumental terrace of the Palais des Nations was covered in a vast, silent amphitheater of seated human bodies, five hundred yards wide, their knees touching, their coats forming a dark, unbroken terrace of wool and canvas against the white stone.

Above them on the portico, Commander Brandt’s hand dropped slightly. The megaphone clipped to his chest let out a single burst of static.

For thirty seconds, the only sound was the low, rhythmic thrum of the lake’s water curtain generators half a mile away and the faint hiss of two hundred thousand candles burning inside glass jars.

Then, from deep within the hollow, cavernous shell of the locked building behind the bronze doors, came a dull, metallic shudder.

It was not the sound of an iron jack failing. It was the internal air-handling system of the Assembly Hall, its dampers suddenly swinging wide, venting forty thousand cubic meters of stale, heated air through the bronze transoms.

High on the portico ceiling, a maintenance relay clicked.

Brandt’s ear-piece squawked, a harsh, rapid squall of cantonal French that cut through the silence of the terrace. He pressed his fingers against his left ear, his gaze flicking away from Marcus toward the high windows of the secretariat tower above.

The hydraulic pressure gauge on the lower trench-jack gave a sharp, wet cough. A drop of amber fluid seeped from the piston seal and fell onto the stone.
