The amber drop of hydraulic fluid struck the granite step between Marcus’s boots with a tiny, greasy click.

On the portico above, Commander Brandt did not move his fingers from his ear-piece. His face, shadowed beneath the brim of a sodden tactical cap, remained set in the hard, pale lines of a man tallying accounts that refused to balance. Cantonal police channels were crackling through his headset—flat, dry voices from the Geneva Department of Security refusing municipal clearance for tear-gas dispersal, citing public health statutes from 1931, while Aegis logistics reported that two of their three mobile diesel generators near the Botanical Gardens had seized up after taking on contaminated paraffin.

Below him, five paces down, Jin Wei shifted his weight off his stiff right knee. The wet wool of his pea coat smelled of salt brine and stale chicory, a stubborn maritime reek that twenty hours on Swiss rail had failed to scour clean. His knuckles were raw from the lake wind, the skin around his cuticles split into tiny red fissures that stung every time the sub-zero breeze whipped off the water.

"They aren't going to fire," Jin muttered under his breath, his voice like gravel rolling in an iron drum. "They don't have the paperwork, and Brandt knows his pension clears through Zurich. But they don't have to shoot. Cold does the work for nothing."

Marcus didn't turn his head. His breath rose in thin, rhythmic plumes against the stone. Beside him, Carlos let out a short, rattling cough—the high-altitude dryness of the Atacama still rasping in his bronchi, struggling against the heavy, damp chill of Lake Geneva.

"The crowd's freezing, Captain," Marcus said quietly. "If we stay seated on this wet granite for two more hours, hypothermia takes three hundred people. If they stand up and press forward, someone slips on the sleet, panic hits the terrace, and Brandt’s barricade turns into a crush pen."

Jin spat onto the gravel. His eyes drifted past the line of Aegis contractors, past the hydraulic trench-jacks wedged into the monumental bronze doors, out toward the eastern perimeter where the Palais gardens sloped steeply toward the municipal docks.

There, tethered against the icy stone quay of the Parc de l'Ariana, sat two yellow harbor service cranes—stout, tracked mobile rigs with eighty-foot lattice booms, brought in three weeks earlier by the city for dredging dead reeds before the freeze set in. Between them lay a half-dozen modular floating pontoons of marine-grade spruce and corrugated steel decking, stacked like neglected rafts on the gravel apron.

"We don't need to force the stairs," Jin said.

Marcus glanced at him. "The retaining wall drops twenty feet into the sunken courtyard. It's an anti-vehicle trench."

"On a container slip, twenty feet is an afternoon coffee break," Jin said. He pushed himself upright, his knee joint grinding audibly in the quiet. He didn't rush. He limped deliberately toward the edge of the terrace where Matteo was already kneeling by a cluster of portable battery packs, wrapping exposed terminal lugs in greasy vulcanized tape to stop the freezing drizzle from shorting their inverter circuits.

Matteo looked up, his face smudged with graphite lubricant. A streak of gray motor grease marked the bridge of his nose, an old habit from his night shifts on the Turin milling lines where he used to wipe sweat with his forearm before remembering the rag in his back pocket. His eyes were bloodshot, pupils contracted against the glare of the municipal sodium lights.

"The winch drums on the harbor cranes," Jin said, pointing with the tip of his ash stick through the bare lime trees. "Are the engines locked?"

Matteo didn't blink. He reached into his canvas tool roll, his fingers finding a six-millimeter hex key by pure touch. "Cantonal port authority uses standard mechanical interlocks on the hydraulic valve blocks. Master keys are universal Swiss industrial pattern. I pulled four blanks from the maintenance depot behind the railway yard an hour ago."

"How much counterweight on the rear skirts?"

"Twelve tons of cast pig iron each," Matteo said, standing so quickly his damp trousers cracked with frost. "They’re balanced for harbor dredge buckets. Why?"

"The trench," Jin said, turning toward the sheer granite wall separating the upper promenade from the inner Court of Honor. Aegis had concentrated its non-lethal deterrents—steel razor wedges, water curtains, and interlocking trench-jacks—along the central grand staircase. But eighty paces to the left, the sunken flagged apron of the courtyard ran flush against the old garden revetment, blocked only by a sheer drop and four rows of concrete jersey barriers. "We don't use engines under load. Diesel's dead or monitored. We drop the booms using the gravity-release friction brakes. We swing the pontoons across as a counterweighted incline. A non-lethal gravity bridge."

Matteo’s jaw tightened. He wasn't thinking in ideals; his mind was instantly running the shear strength of four-inch marine timber against forty tons of static civilian foot traffic. "The pontoons are six meters long. If we string three together with hoisting slings, the angle of descent is twenty-two degrees. That's wheelchair accessible. But the sleet—"

"Grit," Carlos said, having walked up behind them with his heavy woolen poncho pulled tight over his chest. He held up a red plastic bucket full of coarse road salt and crushed basalt gravel from a cantonal maintenance bin. "We lace the planks with basalt. Even wet rubber boots won't slide."

"Go," Marcus told them. He hadn't stood up. He remained seated on the freezing step, his broad shoulders forming a quiet anchor at the center of the terrace, drawing Brandt's gaze like an iron post. "No crowbars. No alarms. If a single Aegis man reaches for a stun baton, we halt."

Jin and Matteo cut across the terrace, slipping down the grass embankment where the sleet had turned the frozen lawn into slick mud. The air here smelled of dead lake weeds, diesel exhaust, and the sharp, clean bite of wet iron.

At the quay, the yellow mobile crane stood motionless, its yellow paint peeling in long scabs along the jib. Matteo hoisted himself onto the steel tracks, the cold metal biting through the leather palms of his work gloves. He shoved the hex key into the bypass port beneath the cab's hydraulic manifold. His knuckles strained against the frozen valve, muscles in his forearms bunching until the brass plug gave way with a sharp *clink*.

"Hydraulic pilot is open," Matteo hissed down. "Manual friction release on the drum brake is stiff. Jin, grab the cheater pipe!"

Jin hauled a two-meter length of galvanized scaffolding pipe off the pontoon stack, wedging it over the crane's manual drum release lever. His boots slipped on the iced decking; he cursed between clenched teeth, his breath coming in white, ragged gasps. "On three. Don't touch the throttle—let the counterweight fall on the dog-clutch!"

"One. Two. Pull!"

Both men threw their weight onto the steel pipe. For a second, nothing happened. The cable drum was frozen in its housing, locked by fifty miles of lake frost. Then, with a dry, screaming shriek of steel on bronze, the brake bands separated.

The boom groaned. The massive lattice arm dipped three feet under its own dead weight, the steel hoisting cables singing a high, harmonic whine like a cello string tuned to snapping point.

Below on the gravel apron, Carlos and four rail switchers from Basel hitched the heavy nylon rigging slings around the steel eyelets of the first marine pontoon. They didn't shout; they used the low, rhythmic hand signals of shunting yards.

*Up-slack. Swing-left. Drop-pin.*

The mobile crane’s lattice jib leaned forward across the lawn. Matteo, his hands bleeding where the brass petcock had gouged his thumb, rode the manual brake lever with his entire body weight, feathering the descending boom inch by inch. There was no engine roar—only the heavy, groaning sigh of displacement oil pushing through narrow bypass orifices and the dry scrape of steel cables paying out over the sheaves.

On the portico above, Commander Brandt suddenly stiffened.

Through his binoculars, the Aegis commander saw the long lattice arm of the lakeside crane bowing over the lime trees, carrying not a wrecking ball or an explosive charge, but a vast, flat platform of spruce decking suspended from four green nylon slings.

"Perimeter three!" Brandt barked into his lapel microphone, his voice sharp with professional alarm. "They’re bypassing the grand portico! Shift squad two to the western revetment!"

"Sir," the radio sputtered back, the voice of his tactical sergeant crackling with thin, static-laced panic. "Cantonal police have parked two water tenders across the service road. They say our fuel trucks don't have Swiss environmental transit permits. We're on foot. We can't haul the barricade wedges eighty meters through the mud in time!"

The first pontoon struck the granite rim of the sunken courtyard with a massive, hollow *thud* that echoed off the high limestone facade of the Palais.

Matteo didn't pause. He slammed the friction dog home, locking the cable with an iron pin, then dropped from the track to the frozen grass. Carlos was already there, his weathered hands scattering dark basalt gravel across the spruce boards, the crushed stone biting into the wet timber with the sound of grinding teeth.

A second pontoon swung down, guided by Jin’s ash stick wedged as a pivot against the curb. It seated flush against the first with a clean, heavy snap of interlocking tongue-and-groove steel brackets.

The gap was bridged.

A broad, gentle incline—six yards wide, ribbed with salt and crushed stone, banked securely against the granite parapet—now led directly from the crowded terrace down into the empty, unblocked Court of Honor, completely circumventing the razor wire and the hydraulic jacks on the main stairs.

Marcus Hayes stood up from the bottom step of the grand portico. His knees were stiff, his long overcoat dark with slush, but his eyes were completely clear.

He turned toward the 1,001 delegates seated on the freezing terrace. He didn't make a speech. He simply raised his right hand, open-palmed, and pointed toward the timber ramp.

"Move," he said.

The delegates rose. Not in a rush, not in a surge, but with the slow, disciplined rustle of a thousand people finding their feet together after an alpine vigil. They moved in pairs, supporting the elderly, their boots crunching on the gravel, turning away from the fortified bronze doors and channeling smoothly toward the wooden ramp.

Brandt lowered his binoculars. His tactical line was intact, his orders technically unviolated, and his position entirely irrelevant. The barricade on the stairs guarded nothing now except empty air and frozen stone.

Marcus walked toward the base of the ramp, stepping beside Matteo, who was leaning against the crane track, sucking the blood from his cut thumb.

"Good iron," Marcus said softly.

"Swiss pine," Matteo corrected, his chest heaving as he wiped his face with his sleeve. "Tougher than it looks."

Jin Wei was the first to step onto the ramp, testing the decking with the iron ferrule of his stick. The spruce held firm under the weight of the advancing crowd, the basalt grit crackling cleanly beneath their boots as the first fifty delegates filed down into the vast, flagged arena of the Court of Honor.

Above them, the gray clouds over the Jura mountains began to break, revealing a thin sliver of pale winter dawn.

Then, from somewhere deep beneath the flagstones of the courtyard floor—not from the doors, not from the barricades, but from the municipal water utility vaults five meters beneath their feet—came a dull, rhythmic hammering.

It was three rapid metallic strikes, followed by the deep, resonant hiss of pressurized steam venting through an old iron intake.

Jin froze, his stick planted midway down the incline.

Through the heavy cast-iron storm grate at the center of the courtyard, a plume of pale vapor rose into the freezing air, carrying with it the unmistakable, sulfurous reek of industrial cutting torches biting into high-tensile copper conduit.
