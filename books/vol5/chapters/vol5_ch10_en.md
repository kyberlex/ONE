The auxiliary diesels beneath the floor of the Cointrin terminal took six seconds to catch. In those six seconds of complete dead-eye black, before the emergency switchgear clunked shut and bathed the Approach Radar suite in dull amber low-voltage light, Beat Meyer did not reach for a telephone or curse. He kept his palms flat against the laminate rim of the primary console, feeling the subsonic thrum of the ventilation dying and the sudden, heavy silence of thirty air-traffic controllers inhaling at once.

When the cathode screens pulsed back up, the primary sweep repainted the lake basin in phosphor greens and grays. 

"Loss of main municipal tie," Sophie said. Her headset boom swung an inch from her lips as she leaned into the secondary terminal, her right thumb clicking through the cantonal emergency backup bus. "Pumping stations and shore grid went down together. We’re on terminal battery and isolated diesel. Radar head at La Dôle is holding on local solar-storage."

"Identify," Meyer said. His voice was clipped, stripped of inflection, dry with the grit of eight hours breathing recycled filter air. He pointed a blunt, nicotine-stained forefinger at the northern quadrant of the scope.

Four high-speed targets had crossed the ridgeline of the Jura, dropping out of French airspace at four thousand feet. They carried no civilian transponder squawks, only the encrypted four-digit burst codes typical of private tactical transport. They were banking south-southeast, riding the cold alpine down-drafts directly toward the blind pocket between the United Nations complex and the water intake jetty.

"Contract flight out of Annecy," Sophie muttered, checking the strip printer as it spewed blank yellow cardstock. "No flight plan on file with Eurocontrol. Call-sign Aegis Three-Zero flight. They’re running low-level formation."

The tactical VHF channel crackled. The voice that came through was flat, mid-Atlantic, and scrubbed of panic.

*"Geneva Approach, Aegis Three-Zero lead. Flight of four heavy rotorcraft inbound Palais perimeter under G20 Financial Stabilization Board emergency enforcement mandate. We have operational priority. Clear Class D airspace from surface to six thousand feet within five nautical miles of the lake basin. Divert all civilian traffic immediately."*

Sophie’s eyes flicked to the left edge of the sweep. An Airbus A320 out of Vienna, holding fifty tons of passengers and sixty minutes of remaining holding fuel, was already banked into the standard descent pattern over Saint-Prex, turning inbound for Runway 22. Behind it, a regional turboprop from Lugano was five miles out from the glideslope intercept.

"They’re cutting the localizer," Sophie whispered. "If they punch into the basin at four thousand, they’re inside the Austrian’s missed-approach lane."

Meyer did not ask the gunships who gave them their orders. He did not ask about their mandate, nor did he lecture the contractor on international law. He adjusted his headset, keyed the master cantonal transmission toggle with a sharp, downward snap of his wrist, and spoke directly into the teeth of the incoming flight.

"Aegis Three-Zero flight, Geneva Approach. You are operating unidentified inside Swiss federal civilian airspace without an approved flight plan, without secondary radar transponder altitude reporting, and without cantonal clearance. Squawk seven-seven-zero-zero or turn north immediately to heading zero-four-zero."

A pause on the frequency. Then the contractor lead returned, the rotor beat bleeding through his open mic like the slow ripping of sheet metal.

*"Geneva, repeat: this is an active stabilization operation authorized under multilateral debt preservation protocols. We are establishing an airborne security cordon around the Palais des Nations and the quayside. We are not turning. Clear your civilian stack or assume liability for tactical interdiction."*

"He thinks this is an airfield in Basra," Sophie said under her breath. Her fingers moved rapidly across the touch-strip, locking the Austrian flight into an immediate climb. "Austrian Seven-Six-Two, Geneva, stop descent at six thousand feet, turn left heading two-eight-zero, hold as published."

Meyer remained entirely still, his shoulders locked, his eyes tracking the four blips as they breached the three-mile ring. The gunships were not climbing; they were descending into the thermal inversion layer that clung to the lake, masking their heat signatures against the cold water to blind any handheld optical sensors while their side-mounted jamming pods went to work on the municipal mesh below.

"Sophie," Meyer said, his tone dropping into the cold, rhythmic cadence of a controller handling a twin-engine flameout over granite. "Open the open-access air safety relay. Link terminal radar telemetry directly to the European public broadcast stream and the Swiss Federal Office of Civil Aviation in Bern."

"Sir?"

"Route the raw primary radar echo, uncompressed, unencrypted, to the live civic internet feed Lin Zhang is broadcasting from the boatyard. If they cross the shore, eight million people will watch their flight vectors superimpose over the international hospital and the civilian promenade."

Sophie’s eyes narrowed, then her knuckles hit the routing macro. "Telemetry bridged. Signal is live on the open civic channel."

Meyer keyed his microphone again. He did not raise his voice by a single decibel.

"Aegis Three-Zero, you are now transmitting your raw primary track to public civil air telemetry across thirty-eight cantonal and international ground stations. Furthermore, you have two hundred meters before you enter the wake-turbulence corridor of Austrian Seven-Six-Two. If you continue descent on your present vector, you will breach civil separation minimums by twelve hundred feet. Under Swiss federal aviation statute, that constitutes reckless endangerment of civil air navigation. Your airframes are tracked, your tail numbers are registered with the Bern registry, and your aviation insurance under Lloyd’s of London is formally voided as of this transmission."

The radio went completely dead for four long seconds.

On the radar sweep, the four targets hovered on the boundary of the inner harbor. Meyer could see the physical hesitation in their telemetry—the tight, nervous micro-adjustments of cyclic sticks held by men who were paid twenty thousand dollars a week to be invisible, suddenly realizing they were illuminated in thirty-meter phosphor clarity for every maritime reinsurer and cantonal prosecutor on the continent.

A new voice broke through the channel—rougher, older, carrying the distinct cadence of a private military operations officer speaking from a command center outside Zurich.

*"Geneva Approach, this is Aegis Command. We have clear jurisdiction to disperse an unlawful municipal gathering endangering the sovereign debt clearinghouse."*

"Aegis Command," Meyer said, his eyes never leaving the sweep. "You are in Class D civilian airspace. You do not have an operational waiver from the Federal Council. If your skids pass inside the perimeter of the Quai Wilson, our automated ground-proximity warning systems will flag your flight lead as an uncontrolled aerial obstacle. The cantonal police wing at Dübendorf has already been alerted for emergency airspace sovereignty enforcement. You have ninety seconds of fuel burn before you enter an unrecoverable civil airspace violation. Vector zero-four-zero, climb to eight thousand feet, or report your ditching intentions on this frequency."

Sophie glanced at Meyer. A bead of sweat had rolled down the deep furrow alongside his nose, hanging at the corner of his mustache, but his hand on the frequency gain was steady as a vise.

On the glass, the lead blip twitched. It drifted twenty yards closer to the Palais, then violently banked thirty degrees to the north, pulling its three wingmen into a steep climbing turn back toward the cloud deck over the Jura.

*"Geneva Approach,"* the flight lead spat, the tactical coolness cracking into sheer operational disgust. *"Aegis Three-Zero breaking formation. Diverting to alternate holding fix at Saint-Cergue. We will hold outside your boundary, but your ground perimeter is already blown. Look at your harbor camera."*

The frequency snapped off with a sharp burst of static.

Sophie let out her breath in a long, shuddering hiss. "They broke off. The Austrian is clear to intercept the localizer."

"Hold him high for another three miles," Meyer said, his fingers finally dropping from the console edge. "Clear the Lugano flight behind him."

"Beat," Sophie said, her voice dropping an octave as she leaned toward the auxiliary surveillance feed patching through from the lake police pier. "Look at the water."

Meyer shifted his gaze to the low-resolution optical camera mounted atop the Jet d'Eau pumping tower. 

The lake was pitch black under the winter drizzle, the waterfront streetlamps completely dead from the grid collapse. But slicing through the freezing surface haze, moving at thirty knots from the direction of the French border near Hermance, was a fifth signature—low, narrow, throwing almost no radar cross-section because it was running three feet above the water.

It wasn't an aircraft. It was a high-speed littoral assault skiff, totally unlit, its composite hull riding the trough of the waves as it headed directly toward the municipal boatyard where Lin Zhang and Elena Vance were nursing the last optical transmitter.

Meyer reached for the cantonal direct line, his thumb jamming into the red toggle. "Marine police, Approach. We have an unflagged surface interceptor running the south shore at thirty knots. They’re under the radar floor. They’re thirty seconds from the boatyard slipway."

Across the glass of the main terminal display, the tiny blip of the skiff hit the mouth of the harbor, cutting straight through the black water toward the dark, fragile silhouette of the municipal pumping shed.
