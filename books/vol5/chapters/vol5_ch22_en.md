The impact rattled the spruce framework of the boatyard slipway, sending a shower of dry rust and powdered lime down into the trench.

"Wedge hold!" Jin Wei bellowed from the slipway edge. He was not looking at the grey lake mist, but down at the hand-cranked ratchet dogs anchoring the drydock timbers. His left knee, thick and permanently stiff from thirty years on bulk-carrier iron, locked against the hemlock timbering. "Counter-weight drops in three! Carlos, trip the dog!"

Carlos did not shout back. His chest heaved with the thin, sharp air of low altitude—rich with an unaccustomed soup of marsh damp and salt that made his Andean lungs feel drowned. He hammered a cold chisel into the release pawl with a rusty two-pound maul. The six-ton ballast wedge of municipal granite, balanced over the slipway throat since the late fifties, dropped three feet into the silt. The boatyard shuddered again. The hydrofoil’s aluminum hull shrieked across the submerged stone ridge, tearing away its starboard foil with the sound of a freight car jumping a switch. The engine died in a choking cough of fouled intake water and diesel foam.

"Clear!" Carlos wheezed, wiping black bile and lake spray from his cracked lip. "Hull's broached on the rock. They're hung up."

High above on the gantry catwalk, Giulia did not lower her spotlight. The beam remained fixed on the listing hydrofoil, pinning its three-man cantonal security crew in a glare so flat and clinical it turned their tactical drysuits to chalk. Below them, thirty yards back behind the slipway bulkhead, the first two hundred jurors of the Global Commons Assembly stood in unbroken ranks. They did not retreat. They did not run. They pulled their woolen scarves tighter against the freezing drizzle, their boots rooted in the gravel, watching the water churn.

The green beam overhead hummed—a solid, thumb-thick cord of coherent light cutting through the sleet toward the relay station on the Mont Salève. It was completely silent, carrying no radio signature for cantonal jammers to smother, transmitting nothing more than the physical weight of verified wooden tokens and the unedited camera eye of the yard floor.

***

Six miles away, across the black water in the soundproofed fourth-floor studio of Continental Financial Telemetry on the Quai du Mont-Blanc, David Ward peeled his earpiece loose and let it dangle against his collar.

The room smelled of overheated copper chassis, burnt chicory, and the damp wool of eight production assistants who had not left their monitors in seventy hours. David was fifty-two. His neck carried the red chafing of stiff poplin shirts starched by Geneva hotel laundries, and his right hand had a persistent tremor that only ceased when he wrapped his fingers around the brass barrel of his fountain pen. For nineteen years, his baritone voice had anchored the midday European clearinghouse index—a voice deliberately pitched to reassure sovereign bondholders, to smooth over currency devaluations, to make industrial liquidations sound like the rational pruning of an orchard.

In his left ear, the direct line from the Emergency Stabilization Board in Basel was barking.

"David, prompt is green. Read the update," the executive producer’s voice rasped through the desk intercom. "Read the wire verbatim. Geneva police report armed saboteurs occupying the municipal water intake. Contamination risk at level four. Repeat the shelter-in-place directive for the canton."

David looked down at the glass prompter below the lens of Camera One.

The white letters scrolled up smoothly, synchronized to the cadence of his breath: *UNAUTHORIZED MILITANTS PENETRATE SECTOR SEVEN INTAKE. SWISS CANTONAL AUTHORITIES DECLARE WATER SAFETY EMERGENCY. COMMONS GATHERING COMPROMISED BY FOREIGN EXTREMISTS.*

Beside the prompt monitor, on a small, uncalibrated field screen patched into the open optical relay from Mont Salève, another image played.

There were no weapons. There were no masks. There was a middle-aged woman in rabbit-fur mittens, her face streaked with black machine grease, using a pipe wrench to seal a weeping water flange while an old ship’s captain in an oilskin coat held a flashlight for her. Behind them, an old man in a threadbare brown wool overcoat dropped a turned-beechwood cylinder into an antique copper balance scale. The pan tilted with a soft, distinct *clack* that came through the studio’s raw audio monitor like dry firewood snapping in the dark.

"Three hundred and forty-two," the woman at the ledger table muttered in the raw feed. Her voice was flat, frayed, carrying the unmistakable cadence of an administrative clerk who had missed three meals.

"David," the producer barked, louder now, the sharp pop of studio feedback biting the line. "Read the board memo. Cut the field patch. Now."

David did not touch the prompter pedal. He reached beneath the curved mahogany desk, felt the rough plastic loom of the primary video-bus patch cable, and unseated the locking ring with his thumb.

"David, what are you doing with the board?"

He looked straight into the lens. The little tally lamp above the glass hooded eye was a deep, unblinking red. Forty million television receivers across the Eurozone were currently slaved to this transponder. In train stations from Munich to Marseille, behind the plate-glass windows of closed retail banks, on airport waiting-room monitors where stranded travelers sat upon their luggage, the studio’s feed was the only signal not frozen by the cantonal data embargo.

"This is David Ward," he said. His voice was not the measured, oiled baritone of the market summary. It was thin, dry, and entirely steady. "For nineteen years, I have read you the prices of your water, your wheat, your debt, and your children’s futures from slips of paper handed to me through that glass booth."

In the control booth, hands slammed against the soundproof acrylic. The executive producer was on his feet, spitting into his headset boom, signaling frantically to the master control switchboard in Frankfurt to cut the microwave link.

"The wire copy currently running on my prompter is an absolute fabrication," David said. He picked up the three-page official release from the Stabilization Board, folded it once lengthwise, and laid it quietly beside his water glass. "There are no armed militants at the Seujet slipway. There is no contamination. The men and women standing on the dock are the sortition jurors of the Global Commons Assembly. They are your neighbors. One of them is a retired track inspector from the yard at Annemasse. I recognize him because he used to fix the switches on the commuter rail I rode to this desk every morning."

The audio console behind the glass flashed red. A technician’s arm reached past the producer, hesitating over the master emergency kill-switch—the red guarded toggle designed to drop the station to black in the event of military occupation.

"Don't pull it, Marc," David said, his eyes shifting for half a second toward the booth. "If you drop this carrier, the clearinghouses take the default automatic. The pension funds vanish into the stabilization pool at midnight anyway. You know that. Your father's pension is in that tranche."

The technician froze. His hand hung four inches above the toggle, his knuckles white against the dark console.

David looked back into the center of the lens.

"We are going to switch your screens now to the unencrypted optical feed coming off the Lake Geneva basin," David said. He took the brass fountain pen from his breast pocket, wedged it firmly into the manual override socket of the desk switcher, and shoved the lever forward until the steel linkage snapped against its mechanical stop. "Look at them. See what they are doing with the weight of their own hands. And decide for yourselves whether you are going to stay inside your houses."

The studio monitor went gray for half a second, then snapped into crystalline clarity.

The screen did not show graphics or ticker bars. It showed the boatyard floor under the harsh, cold glare of the floodlights. The listing hydrofoil lay wedged in the mud, its crew sitting unarmed on its tilted gunwales, accepting paper cups of hot chicory handed down to them by an elderly Swiss woman in a knit cap. In the center of the floor, the copper scale dipped again.

*Clack.*

"Three hundred and forty-three," Sophie Althaus’s voice carried through eight million television speakers, dry as salt, echoed by the rain falling on the corrugated iron roof.

Behind David, the door to the studio burst open. Two cantonal bailiffs in yellow visibility vests stepped onto the carpet, their faces tight with administrative dread. One of them held an official sequestration notice on heavy blue cantonal parchment, stamped with the seal of the Grand Council.

David did not rise. He took his earpiece, dropped it into the cold dregs of his coffee cup, and leaned his chin on his knuckles.

"You're late, gentlemen," he said, nodding toward the red lamp on the camera snout. "The master loop is hard-wired to the transmission mast on the Dole. It has three hours of fuel in the diesel generator at the summit, and there isn't a switch between here and Frankfurt that can pull it back."

On the field monitor, the camera swung toward the water-gate.

Out on the dark surface of the lake, through the drifting sheets of freezing mist, a sound was rising. It was not the high-pitched shriek of maritime turbines or the siren of police tenders.

It was the low, rhythmic churning of hundreds of small diesel engines.

Across the water, emerging from the dark perimeter of the French shoreline, the first of the local fishing skiffs, timber tenders, and municipal gravel barges were entering the roadstead, their running lights cutting amber paths across the sleet.

Giulia’s voice cracked over the open yard radio, breathless and hoarse:

*"Marcus. Look at the water. They aren't the consortium's boats."*
