The sleet turned to needles against Marcus’s cheek, but the chill didn't register. What struck him was the dead silence where a radio carrier should have been hummed out into the alpine air.

"How thick are those shutters?" Marcus asked, his breath misting grey against the damp collar of his canvas coat.

"Ten-gauge cold-rolled plate," Elena said, her thumb swiping across the frost-crusted glass of her slate. The screen flickered, struggling against the sub-zero bite that was eating her auxiliary lithium cells percent by percent. "Fire isolation system from the mid-seventies. Gravity drops. Once the counterweights trip, they don't rise without the hydraulic mains in the basement."

"Basement’s dead," Jin Wei rumbled behind them. The old sailor shifted his stance on the frozen gravel, his left knee giving a sharp, dry pop that Marcus had never heard during their weeks aboard the battered tugs of the southern straits. Jin Wei had an old copper washer tucked inside his cheek—a lifelong habit from his engine-room apprenticeships, sucking on cold metal to keep from grinding his molars down to the pulp under tension. "District power cut the auxiliary turbines ten minutes ago. We’re on local battery."

"Then we don't go through the basement," Marcus said. "We go through the Grand Rotunda."

Carlos Quispe stumbled slightly beside them, catching his balance against a stone balustrade. The thin, high-altitude air of the Altiplano had sustained him for fifty years; down here at lake level, the thick, heavy oxygen of the Geneva basin made him perpetually lightheaded, as if his blood were too rich for his lungs. He pressed a hand to his chest, coughing a dry, raspy bark that left flecks of grey phlegm on the slush.

"The doors," Carlos rasped, his dark eyes watering from the raw wind off the water. "There are men behind the bronze. I hear the boots. Steel plates on heels."

"Consortium interior cordon," Giulia murmured, appearing at Marcus’s elbow. Her dark hair was plastered flat against her temples by the freezing rain, her skin pale beneath the bruises left by the border crossing. She held no weapon; her hands were jammed into the deep pockets of an oversized railway coat she’d traded for dry socks three checkpoints ago. "They aren't cantonal police. Cantonal stood down at the gate. These are the private security details from the clearinghouse towers in Zurich. Salaried retainers. They have wives with mortgages in Lausanne."

"Which means they're frightened," Marcus said. "Frightened men shoot before they listen."

He took the stone steps three at a time, his wet soles slipping twice on the rime of black ice coating the limestone. Behind him, the low murmur of the two hundred thousand ceased at the threshold of the courtyard. They did not push past the iron gates; they gathered in an immense, dark crescent across the snow-covered lawn, steam rising off their wet coats like mist off an autumn river. The absolute discipline of that quiet was more terrifying than any war cry.

At the top of the terrace, the grand bronze portals of the Palais stood bolted, but the narrow flanking service wicket—used for midnight diplomatic couriers—was cracked four inches, held on a steel tension chain.

Marcus didn't knock. He put his shoulder against the oak jamb and shoved.

The chain snapped taut with a sound like a rifle shot.

Through the gap, the muzzle of an assault rifle didn't appear. Instead, the burnished rim of a riot shield slammed hard into the oak, followed by the dull, flat clatter of composite armor.

"Clear the threshold!" a voice barked from the dry, heated air of the interior. It was a flat, educated voice, Swiss-German vowels clipped short with military precision. "Cantonal neutrality does not permit unauthorized assembly within the Grand Assembly perimeter. Step back down the stairs or we deploy aerosol dispersal."

"The dispersal compressors failed an hour ago when the line-drop hit the north grid," Elena called out, her voice dangerously calm as she stepped up beside Marcus, holding her slate at waist height. "You're running on emergency capacitor banks. You have three minutes of halogen lighting in that lobby before your primary relays trip, Major."

A pause fell inside the vestibule. The heavy breathing of men encased in nylon and ceramic plate rasped through the four-inch gap.

"We aren't breaking anything," Marcus said. He kept his hands open, palms out, chest level. When panic flared in his sternum—that old ghost from the cold concrete floor of his youth, the animal urge to run or swing wild—he forced himself to count the brass rivets holding the wicket's iron strapwork. One, two, three, four. Sarah had taught him that during the bitter months after the first raid on the river workshop. *Count the joins, Marcus. Men built it with finite hours. It has an end.* "I'm Marcus Hayes. I was summoned by the sortition lottery drawn at dawn."

"The lottery is contested," the voice behind the shield replied. "The Sovereign Debt Consortium filed an emergency injunction with the commercial tribunal in Basel at zero-four-hundred. The assembly cannot sit while insolvency actions are pending against the guarantor accounts."

"The assembly doesn't belong to the banks," Carlos called out, his voice thin but cutting cleanly through the draft. "The thousand names were drawn from the parish rolls of six continents. My sister’s boy in Potosí is sitting in that hall. You have locked an apprentice welder in the dark with no water."

Inside, a bolt slid back.

The heavy door creaked open three feet into the amber glare of the Rotunda vestibule.

A tall officer in unmarked slate-grey tactical wool stepped into the threshold. He wore no helmet, only a high-collared garrison tunic stripped of national insignia, his hair cropped silver at the temples. A thick scar notched his left eyebrow, pulling the lid down into a perpetual squint. In his right hand, he carried a heavy polycarbonate baton, held low against his thigh. Behind him, eight armored men stood in a staggered wedge across the marble checkerboard floor, their non-lethal kinetic carbines held at low-port.

"Major Keller," the officer said softly, his breath smelling faintly of peppermint and cold tea. "Executive security, Bank for International Settlements security liaison."

"Marcus Hayes," Marcus said again.

Keller looked past Marcus’s shoulder, out at the vast sea of damp wool, canvas, and upturned faces filling the terrace under the driving sleet. The sheer weight of humanity made the air outside seem dense, almost pressurized.

"If that mob breaches these doors," Keller said, his tone devoid of anger, weighed down by an immense, calculated exhaustion, "the clearinghouse settlements in Frankfurt fail by noon. The pension trust reserves for twenty-two sovereign nations evaporate before the London market opens. You think you are inaugurating a commonwealth, Hayes. You are pulling the pins on the life support of four hundred million people who rely on those daily overnight loans for their tap water and flour."

"The flour stopped moving three days ago, Major," Jin Wei said, stepping forward until his wide, barrel chest was inches from Keller's baton. The old captain pointed a thick, grease-stained thumb toward the lake. "The grain ships are anchored off Rotterdam because your clearinghouses won't honor the fuel bills. My first mate hasn't bought diesel for a towboat with paper credit in six months. It’s already dead. You’re just guarding the corpse so nobody sees the skin turning blue."

Keller didn't strike. His grip on the baton didn't tighten. He looked at Jin Wei’s hands—the blunt, scarred fingers of a man who worked winches in zero-degree gale winds—and then back at Marcus.

"The Sortition Chamber has one thousand and one chairs," Keller said, his voice dropping an octave. "Right now, seven hundred of them are sitting in their overcoats in the dark because the air handlers have shut down. The Consortium delegates are at the high dais. They have the diplomatic microphones on an isolated battery loop. They are preparing to declare a state of planetary insolvency under the Hague Emergency Clauses."

"Then let me in," Marcus said simply.

Keller looked down at Marcus’s hands. Marcus’s right knuckle was swollen, split open from a frozen winch cable three nights ago on the crossing from France, the skin crusted with dried brown salt.

"You have no credential," Keller said.

"I have the same credential Sarah had when she walked into the Detroit city council in ninety-eight," Marcus said. "I'm a citizen who pays for his bread with sweat."

Keller stood motionless for five beats of a heart. Around them, the halogen lights in the high ceiling gave an audible shudder, the white glare dimming to a jaundiced, tea-colored amber as the building’s emergency capacitors dumped another five percent of their finite reserves.

"My men will not fire on the crowd," Keller said quietly. "We have children in Neuchâtel. But inside that rotunda, you are under the cameras of two hundred global syndicates. If you falter, if you threaten them, they will cut the master trunk line and tell the world you came with guns."

"We left the iron in the snow," Marcus said.

Keller stepped back. With a single, sharp gesture of his left hand, the eight armored guards split their wedge, moving back against the marble pillars like grey statuary.

The heavy inner doors of the Grand Rotunda swung open.

A wave of trapped air rolled over them—smelling of old paper, hundreds of woolen overcoats drying on human bodies, and the sharp, metallic tang of ozone from the television camera arrays mounted on the upper galleries.

The silence inside was absolute.

Under the massive, domed ceiling of the assembly hall, a thousand ordinary men and women sat in the curved tiers of the amphitheater. They were wrapped in blankets, scarves, and working jackets—farmers from the Nile Delta, dockers from Busan, schoolteachers from the Ohio Valley—sitting in odd-parity rows beneath the frozen flags of a dying century. At the elevated center dais, beneath the bright glare of battery-powered floodlights, six men in charcoal-grey bespoke suits stood around the master council table, their faces taut, their hands resting on stacks of vellum debt covenants.

At the center of the low floor, fifty paces away across the cold green marble, stood the lonely bronze stem of the sortition podium.

Marcus stepped across the threshold alone.

His boots, caked with Geneva slush, left wet, grey impressions on the polished stone. Every squeak of his rubber soles echoed off the limestone dome like a hammer hitting an anvil.

Past the silent ranks of armored private guards whose carbines remained slung, Marcus walked steadily forward. He did not look at the cameras humming in the upper loges. He kept his eyes on the small, unadorned black steel microphone mounted on the walnut lectern.

As he reached the podium and placed his swollen, salt-crusted fingers on the wood, the primary halogen lights overhead gave a violent, dying snap—plunging the vast dome into the grey, freezing dawn filtering through the glass lanterns above.

At the high dais, the senior Consortium delegate rose, his mouth open to shout into his live transceiver.

Marcus tapped the microphone once. The thump reverberated through the chests of a thousand delegates like the first stroke of a bell.
