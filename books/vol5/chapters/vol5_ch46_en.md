The heavy bronze chandeliers of the Palais des Nations did not flicker; they shuddered. A low, gut-churning vibration rose out of the flagstones, humming through the soles of Marcus’s boots like a subterranean engine breaking its mounts. Overhead, the glass in the ceiling ribs groaned against their lead cappings.

"They aren't trying to reverse the verdict," Lin whispered, her hands already locked onto the terminal’s mechanical toggle switches. Her breath came in shallow, ragged bursts. "They’re tripping the transmission corridors. Every automated breaker between Frankfurt and Lyon is dumping secondary load directly into the valley lines. If the regional transformers melt, the local carrier signal dies before the air-gap clears the cantonal boundary."

"How long?" Marcus barked. He didn't look at the bench of judges. Alvarez was already standing, her hand raised to hold the sortition assembly in their seats, her voice cutting through the rising panic of a thousand delegates with steady, rhythmic authority.

"Four minutes," Lin said, throwing a rotary disconnect. A shower of dry, orange sparks bit into the cuff of her woolen sweater. She didn't flinch. "Five, if the lake hydro plant holds its dampening sluices. But the optic dish on the roof isn't locking. The tracking gimbal is fouled."

Marcus turned on his heel. "Elena. Stay with Alvarez. Don't let them break the parity."

Elena didn't waste breath on an answer. She simply clamped her air-gap token between both palms, her knuckles white, and planted herself at the center lectern like an oak stump driven into granite.

Marcus threw his weight against the heavy side door, bursting out onto the exposed stone colonnade. The Swiss winter hit him like a wet canvas sack. Freezing drizzle blew sideways off the lake, coating the balustrades in an oily skin of black ice. Below, in the parkland stretching down to the water, the encampment of two hundred thousand people had gone dead silent, staring up at the city's perimeter pylons. Across the water, on the French shore, a substation flared violet once, twice, and died, dropping an entire district into blackness.

He sprinted down the service stairs toward the decommissioned municipal pumping station. His left boot slipped on the third tread, his hip slamming against the iron rail with a dull, sickening crack that drove the air from his lungs. He caught himself by the fingers of his torn hand, the raw split skin searing as it dragged against rusted paint. He didn't stop. He pushed off the iron and took the remaining steps three at a time.

Inside the pumping station, the air smelled of ozone, scorched varnish, and stagnant bilge.

Carlos Quispe was bent double over the optical driver assembly, his heavy miner's frame silhouetted against the emerald pilot array. He was coughing—a dry, hacking rattle that seemed to pull from the bottom of his dust-scarred lungs. At high altitude, Carlos moved like a mountain cat; down here at lake level, the thick, oxygen-dense air sat in his chest like wet mortar, making his balance sluggish.

"The sleet froze the azimuth collar!" Carlos shouted over the roar of the backup fuel cell. "The servo is stripping its teeth! We’re off the Detroit vector by three degrees!"

Captain Jin Wei stood braced against the gantry ladder, one hand pressed firmly against his bad right knee to keep the joint from buckling under the torque of the wind whistling through the open roof hatch. Decades on rolling iron decks had given him an unnatural stillness in a storm, but the damp cold of the lake was seeping into his marrow.

"The Malacca packet is already holding its station," Jin said, his voice flat, stripped of all maritime formality. He held up a salt-crusted field transceiver. "Atacama just checked in. They've punched through the coastal cloud bank. But they can't see us. If we don't return the pilot pulse, the global loop stays broken. The ledger will fragment into four regional partitions."

"Where's the manual crank?" Marcus demanded, clearing the bottom rung of the ladder.

"Upper deck," Jin said, pointing up through the hatch. "Exposed to the draft. Carlos tried to take a pipe wrench to the dog-clutch, but the housing is iced over."

Marcus reached into his coat pocket. His fingers found no ancestral talisman, no secret heirloom—only a flat steel pry-bar he had lifted from the dockside maintenance shed an hour earlier, its black oxide finish pitted with machine grease.

"Up," Marcus said.

They scrambled through the hatch onto the flat roof of the old brick station. The wind off Lake Geneva was vicious, screaming through the lattice of the optical tower. Thirty feet above them, the main emitter assembly—a four-ton cluster of mirrors, copper heatsinks, and shrouded lenses—hung suspended on its gimbal, shuddering violently as the electric drive motor whined in high-pitched agony, slipping against an inch of clear rime ice.

Marcus grabbed the frozen rungs of the tower ladder. As a boy in the rust-belt winters, he used to test if his tongue would stick to the freezer door; now, the bare palm of his hand stuck to the sub-zero iron, ripping a layer of skin when he hauled his weight upward. He didn't feel the pain, only the deadening cold that turned his fingers into wooden pegs.

Behind him, Carlos followed, his boots ringing dull on the metal. "The teeth are jammed on the western sector gear!" Carlos yelled into the gale. "If you crack the casting, the whole array falls!"

"Hold my belt!" Marcus shouted back.

He swung out over the open drop, his boots skittering on the icy flange of the primary ring. The wind tried to rip him off the frame, tearing at his coat. Below, the dark, roiling water of the lake looked like a slab of cold slate waiting to shatter him.

Marcus jammed the tip of the steel pry-bar into the junction between the slewing ring and the drive pinion. Ice splintered, sharp shards stinging his eyes.

"Again!" Carlos roared, lunging forward with both hands and grabbing the back of Marcus’s canvas coat, anchoring his boots against a cross-strut.

Marcus leaned his entire body weight into the lever. His breath came in ragged, burning gasps. In his mind, he wasn't thinking of planetary constitutions, or sortition counts, or the grand sweep of history. He was thinking of the stripped threads on an alternator bracket he and Sarah had fought for six hours in a freezing garage on an October night long ago, neither of them speaking, just passing the torch and the breaker bar until the bolt finally gave with a sharp, metallic *crack*.

*Crack.*

The dog-clutch slammed home. The drive gear bit into the ring.

With a deep, mechanical groan, the massive emitter pivoted three degrees to the west, locking onto its coordinate with a heavy hydraulic hiss.

Down in the pumping station, Lin Zhang screamed through the radio: "Link confirmed! We have line-of-sight! All four points are synced!"

Marcus looked up into the winter murk.

From the roof of the pumping station, the Geneva node fired.

It was not a beam of blinding violence, but a pillar of coherent, deep-sapphire light, three feet thick, silent and terrifyingly pure. It tore through the freezing drizzle, punching a clean, steam-rimmed tunnel through the low cloud ceiling.

A fraction of a second later, another beam broke over the western horizon—the Detroit node, relayed across the Atlantic atmospheric bridge, a piercing cyan that lit the undersides of the alpine peaks like stage footlights. From the south, punching over the snow-bound passes of the Alps from the Mediterranean relay, came the burning copper pulse of the Atacama uplink. And from the east, carried through the Indian Ocean optical chain, came the dense, emerald-green carrier wave of the Malacca Strait.

The four beams did not cross. They did not battle for dominance.

At eighty thousand feet, high in the thin, dead calm of the stratosphere, they struck the passive orbital refraction layer precisely at the four corners of the pre-set geometric envelope.

For a heartbeat, nothing happened. The clouds over Europe hung dark and bruised, the hum of the dying electrical grid vibrating like an open wound through the soil.

Then, the four distinct colors vanished.

High above the weather, where the air ceased to resist, the coherent light merged. It spread outward in a frictionless, instantaneous ripple—a blinding, incandescent canopy of pure, brilliant white that stretched from the Jura mountains to the distant rim of the Mediterranean, turning the dead-of-night winter sky into a dome of absolute midday.

There was no sound. No thunder. Only a vast, terrifying illumination that cast the shadows of two hundred thousand people long and sharp across the lawns of the Palais des Nations.

On the gantry tower, Marcus slowly lowered his arm, his eyes watering against the unbearable, shadowless brilliance above. Beside him, Carlos let out a breath that seemed to carry the weight of three generations of underground darkness, his scarred hands trembling against the iron rail.

Then, from the speaker pinned to Marcus’s shoulder, Lin Zhang’s voice cut through the stillness—not with triumph, but with a flat, chilling urgency.

"Marcus," she breathed, the radio crackling with static that wasn't terrestrial. "Look at the telemetry. The canopy isn't just reflecting the ledger back down. Something else in the upper atmosphere is reflecting back down into us."
