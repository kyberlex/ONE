The breath of one thousand and one people rose in pale, fraying plumes toward the vaulted ceiling of the Palais, where the unlit crystal chandeliers hung like frozen clusters of grapes. Outside, the cantonal winter pressed hard against the glass, coating the tall arched mullions in a thin membrane of wet, grey frost. The auxiliary dynamos down in the boatyard thumped rhythmically through the limestone floor—a dull, heavy heartbeat that rattled the empty water glasses on the perimeter tables.

Marcus Hayes stood at the foot of the dais, his coat pulled tight across his chest. His hands were tucked deep into his pockets, fingers curled to hide the split, swollen knuckles that throbbed with every pulse of the lake-water pump beneath them. Across the room, the emergency amber lamps threw long, amber shadows across the circular benches. Nobody spoke. Even the reporters along the mezzanine railing had stopped typing; the dead batteries in their handheld relays were freezing up in the draft that swept through the unheated corridors.

Beside him, Carlos Quispe shifted his weight from boot to boot. At four hundred meters above sea level, the dense, moisture-laden air felt thick as wet fleece in the miner’s lungs. He cleared his throat, a dry, sawing rasp that he caught in the crook of an alpaca-wool cuff. When he was eleven, working a rogue seam before the state inspectors came through, he had learned to chew coca leaves to trick his diaphragm into holding still; now, he only had the sour taste of stale black tea and the bitter cold of a continent locking its knees.

"They cut the border wires," Carlos said under his breath. "Just like the salt flats in July. When the trucks stop, the company men always think hunger makes people polite."

"It won't be polite," Captain Jin Wei said. Jin’s back was straight, but Marcus saw the slight tremor in the older man’s left leg. Decades of riding rolling steel across the Singapore shoals had worn Jin’s cartilage down to bone on bone; on level, motionless marble, his body kept trying to compensate for swells that weren't there. Jin reached into the pocket of his dark wool pea coat, his thick thumb turning over a dry, shriveled plum stone he had kept tucked in the lining since his first crossing through the Malacca narrows—a sailor’s superstitious ballast against dry rot. "The supply ships waiting off the hook at Hoek van Holland have eight days of heavy oil left for their auxiliaries. If the automated lock switches stay dead in default mode, those skippers will drop anchor in the mud and burn their dunnage to keep the galley stoves lit."

"Then we don't give them eight days," Giulia Bellini said.

She stood to Jin’s left, her hair tied back with a length of frayed electrical tape she’d pulled from a sound monitor an hour ago. A bruise, yellow-green and edged with dark purple, sat high on her temple beneath the hairline—the stubborn physical receipt of a falling gantry back at the railhead. To steady the persistent, low-frequency hum of post-concussive nausea, she was counting the decorative iron rivets along the base of the podium balustrade: forty-two on the left, forty-two on the right, perfectly balanced, completely blind to the fact that the grid outside was flatlining.

Lin Zhang appeared at the edge of the stage, her boots wet with slush from the lakeside terrace. She was carrying no papers, only a flat, rectangular slab of slate-grey optical glass mounted in an ash-wood frame, salvaged that morning from a broken navigation table in the cantonal hydro-office. On its surface, beneath the unpowered dark crystals, lay the physical vellum—a hand-scraped sheet of sheepskin cured with chalk, resting directly over an air-gapped matrix of silver-threaded traces.

"The air-gap relays are grounded to the lake bed," Lin said, her voice dry, stripped of everything but logistics. "No satellites. No sovereign debt server can write over it once the carbon settles into the thread. But once you seal it, the 168-hour master clock starts running. The moment this circuit closes, the optical towers on the ridge will broadcast the default release to every municipal depot between here and the Black Sea. There's no back door. There's no appeal to an emergency committee."

Renard remained in the front row, his elbows on his knees, his silk necktie loose and crooked. The former diplomat looked up at the four of them, his eyes sunken into grey pockets of sleeplessness. "You think you are freeing them," Renard murmured, his voice barely carrying past the third row of empty press benches. "You are simply turning off the only machine that knows how to feed forty million people before the bread goes moldy. London will watch this valley freeze until you beg for the interties to hum again."

Marcus didn't look down at him. Instead, he looked past the podium toward the high, curved tiers where the sortition delegates sat. Nine hundred and forty-one men and women, chosen not by wealth or party list, but by the blind, unpurchasable roll of the civic lottery. There was a retired rail brakeman from Lyon with a grease stain on his lapel; a primary school teacher from Bern whose spectacles were held together with a tiny copper wire; an apprentice machinist from Turin with grease still ingrained in the creases of his palms. None of them looked heroic. They looked cold. They had wrapped their coats around their shoulders and tucked their wool scarves into their collars, their eyes fixed on the flat slab of wood and glass.

"Come on," Marcus said softly.

He mounted the three steps of the dais. The wood groaned beneath his boots—dry spruce, cut fifty years ago from the Jura slopes.

Carlos followed, his breath hitching once, a sharp catch in his ribs before he smoothed his face into the flat, immovable expression of a man who had spent thirty years watching rock faces for the hairline fracture that preceded a collapse. He stepped up to the table first.

Lin held out an unpainted clay dish containing a thick, dark paste—lampblack mixed with cold-pressed linseed oil and the pulverized graphite dust they’d scraped from the brushes of an old subway alternator.

Carlos dipped the flat of his right thumb into the black grease. His skin was rough as horn, scored by decades of granite dust and carbide drills. He hovered over the vellum for a single, unhurried breath, his gaze drifting for a split second to the high windows, remembering the bitter smell of burning yareta roots outside his sister’s stone kitchen when the company shut the water lines.

He pressed down.

The air-gapped graphite beneath the skin yielded with a dry, mechanical *click*—the contact of pure silver points meeting through the grain. A dull green filament on the ash frame flared once, catching the carbon impression of his whorls.

"For the water," Carlos whispered in the quiet hall.

Captain Jin stepped forward next. His knees made an audible click as he took the weight off his right heel. He didn't hesitate. He thrust his broad, square thumb into the black ink with the swift, decisive movement of a helmsman throwing a rudder hard over to clear a shoal. He had buried two cousins who died of heat stroke in the unventilated engine rooms of flags-of-convenience rust-buckets; he had seen three different container lines vanish into bankruptcy courts while their crews starved on hardtack at the anchorage.

Jin brought his thumb down hard on the vellum, next to Carlos’s mark. His print was broad, crisscrossed by the white, faded scars of old cable burns.

"For the crews," Jin said, his voice deep and steady as a low-speed diesel.

Giulia was already there. She didn't look at the ink; she looked at the assembly. Her gaze caught the young machinist from Turin in the fourth row, who was watching her with his breath held, his fingers nervously knotting a piece of hemp twine. She remembered the sound of the stamping press in the dark, the way the corporate managers had bolted padlock chains across the circuit breakers before fleeing in their black sedans.

She pressed her thumb into the grease, then down onto the vellum with a deliberate, trembling intensity that made the ash frame creak against the table. The graphite contacts below chimed—a sharp, clear ring of metal on metal.

"For the hands that make the world," Giulia said.

Marcus was left alone before the glass.

He looked down at his own thumb. It was the hand of a man who had spent thirty years setting dies, replacing bronze bearings, and tightening the tension rods on aging industrial presses that rattled his teeth in their sockets. On the inside of his wrist, a tiny, puckered star marked the spot where a drop of molten solder had burned through his sleeve during his second winter in the assembly plants. Sarah had cleaned that burn with witch hazel in their kitchen, blowing on the raw red skin while the kettle whistled on the stove.

*Don't build something they can take away with a wire,* she had told him once, sitting on the porch steps while the streetlights of Detroit hummed their tired, yellow hymn into the evening.

Marcus dipped his thumb into the paste. The cold grease bit into the split skin near the nail.

He stepped to the plinth, set his thumb squarely in the center of the vellum between the three other marks, and leaned his whole weight into the wood.

The fourth contact closed.

Deep within the table, the mechanical latch tripped with a sound like a rifle bolt sliding home. The silver wires embedded in the frame caught the current from the lake turbines, and from the four corners of the Palais, the high-frequency optical emitters mounted on the cornice hummed to life. Through the frosty glass of the arched windows, four razor-thin spears of pale, blue-white light shot out through the alpine mist—striking north toward the Jura, west toward the Rhone gap, south toward the passes, and east across the dark water of the lake.

In the assembly, someone let out a long, ragged breath that sounded like a sob.

Then, through the heavy double doors at the back of the hall, a young runner from the telegraph station burst in, his coat half-buttoned, snow clinging to his bare ears. He held a grease-penciled slate above his head, his face white against the amber glare.

"Marcus!" the boy shouted, his voice cracking against the limestone walls. "The French cantons just acknowledged the beacon! But London didn't wait for the morning—they’ve ordered the North Sea fleet to lay magnetic mines across the mouth of the Scheldt!"
