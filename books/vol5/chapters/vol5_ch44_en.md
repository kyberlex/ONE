The wind off Lake Geneva tasted of wet slate and raw galvanized iron. 

Marcus let the cream-colored parchment drop onto the scarred workbench. It did not flutter away; the damp air had already softened the paper, weighting it down against the spilled transformer fluid and grease. 

"They aren't bluffing," Elena said. Her breath came in short, gray plumes, her lips cracked at the corners from five days of alpine damp. She leaned over the table, her wool scarf tucked tight into the collar of her oilskin coat. "The central clearinghouse code in Basel doesn't care about the sortition assembly. It’s an automated collateral sweep. If the speculative registers are zeroed at twelve hundred hours without verified bills of lading substituted in the clearing ledger, the maritime insurers pull their underwriters. The grain elevators in Alexandria and Tunis stay shut. The silo gates don't lift for an unsigned cargo."

Captain Jin Wei shifted his weight against the iron doorframe, his joints popping with a dry, wooden snap. Decades on rolling container decks had left him with an asymmetric, wide-legged stance, his right knee permanently thickened by old salt-water falls. "Three bulk carriers are anchored outside the breakwater at Port Said right now," he muttered, his voice gravel-rough. "Thirty thousand tons of hard red winter wheat in the holds of the *Amira*. She’s burning bunker fuel just to keep her auxiliary pumps turning. If her discharge bond isn't authenticated through the lakeside node before noon, the port captain won't even drop the pilot ladder."

"Then we don't just clear the debt," Marcus said. His voice was gravelly, the muscles along his jaw aching from a habit he’d had since he was twelve—grinding his molars whenever an engine seized. "We hand them the physical cargo manifest. Direct peer-to-peer line-of-sight to the Mediterranean relay."

"We can't," a voice cracked from the iron gantry above.

Marcus looked up into the dripping gloom of the boatyard rafters.

Florian Meier was hanging halfway out of the maintenance cradle beneath the cupola. He was nineteen, a cantonal waterworks apprentice who had walked off his shift at the municipal pumping station three days ago when the sortition call went out over the cantonal radio. He had broad, shovel-shaped hands, bitten raw at the cuticles, and an unzipped work jacket that showed a moth-eaten gray pullover. His eyes were wide, rimmed with the twitchy red of someone who hadn't slept since Tuesday, and when he spoke, his Swiss-accented French tripped over a nervous, childhood stammer on hard consonants.

"The... the tracking mount," Florian stammered, pointing a black-greased wrench toward the roof hatch. "The optical transceiver gimbal. It’s frozen at forty-two degrees. The wind shear off the water threw the gear train out of true."

Marcus was on the iron rungs before the boy finished speaking. The cold metal bit through his canvas work gloves, leaching the residual heat straight out of his palms. Carlos followed two paces behind, his breathing shallow, the thin mountain rasp in his chest giving way to a wet, heavy rattle in the dense lakeside air.

The rooftop platform was an open grating suspended over the slate roofs of the Quai Wilson. Sleet drove horizontally through the open mesh, stinging Marcus’s face like thrown gravel. 

Mounted on a welded tripod of salvaged railway steel was the optical transceiver—a heavy brass-and-aluminum housing that focused the laser array toward the alpine relay station twenty miles south on the shoulder of the Salève. If that beam wandered by even half a milliradian, the packet stream carrying the grain manifests to the coastal relay stations would scatter into the snow.

The housing was dead still. Its tracking arm was cocked at an unnatural, twisted cant, locked hard against the limit stop.

Florian pulled himself onto the grating, shivering so violently his knees rattled against the guardrail. "The replacement drive rod we salvaged from the container rig—it isn't matching up. The lead screw is binding. I tried to force it with the cheater bar. It jammed."

Marcus knelt in the slush. His knee pads were soaked through, freezing water soaking through his trousers to the scarred skin of his kneecaps. He didn't swear. Swearing was fuel wasted on friction. 

He leaned in close, shielding the assembly with his body. The problem was instantly, brutally clear. The replacement drive screw was an eleven-threads-per-inch Unified National coarse pitch, dragged out of an American-manufactured hydraulic lift on the docks; the gimbal’s bronze split-nut was tapped for a metric two-millimeter lead. 

"You forced it three turns," Marcus said, his voice dropping into the flat, mechanical calm he had used for thirty years when a block cracked on the floor.

Florian swallowed hard, his throat bobbing. "The... the assembly was drifting out of alignment. The screen was flashing drop-outs. I thought the threads would take if I gave it leverage."

"Metal doesn't negotiate, Florian," Marcus said quietly. "You force an imperial pitch into a metric sleeve, you don't make a thread. You make a wedge. You cold-weld the bronze to the steel."

From the courtyard below, the deep, copper resonance of the cathedral clock struck the quarter-hour. Eleven-fifteen. Forty-five minutes until the clearinghouse sweep at the Bank for International Settlements triggered the automatic liquidation hedge.

"Can we bypass the gimbal?" Carlos asked, leaning his shoulder against the windbreak to steady his coughing. "Lock it mechanically with timber shims? Aim it by eye?"

"Beam spread is eight inches wide at twenty miles," Marcus said. He pulled off his wet gloves with his teeth, exposing fingers that were already turning the dull, waxy yellow of early frostbite. "A timber shim contracts when it freezes. You'd hold the lock for ninety seconds, then the beam would dance into the clouds. We cut it out and chase the thread."

"We don't have a lathe," Florian whispered, staring at the ruined mechanism. "The machine shops across the bridge are locked down by the cantonal gendarmerie. We don't have a replacement bronze sleeve."

"We have an oil stone," Marcus said. "A triangular needle file. And a kerosene wash."

He reached into the heavy canvas tool roll Carlos had hauled up the ladder. No magic. No pristine antique from a forgotten drawer that miraculously held the exact proprietary pitch. Just cold, hardened carbon steel tools with chipped plastic handles, greasy from a hundred nameless repair jobs.

Marcus unpinned a laminated, grease-smudged conversion chart from the lid of the toolbox—an open-source shop printout dog-eared from years in damp pockets. 

"Look here," Marcus told the boy, pulling Florian down beside him into the slush. He didn't raise his voice against the howling wind. "The pitch difference between eleven threads per inch and two-millimeter metric is three-tenths of a millimeter over five threads. The steel lead screw didn't shear. It galloped the bronze lead. The bronze is softer. It bunched into a burr right at the third crest."

Florian leaned in, his shivering slowing as his eyes locked onto the metal. The panic in his expression flickered, replaced by the narrow, forensic focus of an apprentice who suddenly saw the physical cause behind the disaster.

"The burr is jamming the lead flank," Florian murmured, pointing a blackened thumb.

"Right," Marcus said. He handed the boy the triangular file. "You don't push forward. If you push, you cut the pitch deeper and ruin the bearing surface. You draw-file. You lay the flat of the needle file against the trailing flank, drag it backward, and shave only the pushed metal. Feel the bite. When the file stops chattering and slides smooth, you've cleared the burr."

Florian’s hand shook as he took the slim piece of steel. "My hands... I can't feel the rake."

Marcus reached out, wrapping his thick, calloused palm over the boy’s frozen knuckles. The boy’s skin was ice; Marcus’s was scarred leather, radiating the stubborn, sullen heat of a body that refused to yield. 

"Breathe out," Marcus instructed. "Don't fight the cold. Let your shoulder take the weight. Feel the bronze through the steel. It’s softer than you think."

Together, in the freezing horizontal sleet, they worked the file. 

*Skritch. Skritch. Skritch.*

A ribbon of pale bronze swarf, no thicker than a human hair, peeled away from the third thread crest. 

Marcus let go. Florian continued alone, his jaw clenched, his eyes narrowed against the stinging ice. He dragged the file three more times, reversed the angle, and cleared the root.

"Kerosene," Florian snapped, his stutter gone, consumed entirely by the mechanical rhythm.

Carlos tilted the brass squirt can. The clear, sharp-smelling oil washed away the filings, leaving the dull gold of the bronze threads clean and re-profiled. 

Marcus took the lead screw, turned it counter-clockwise by hand until he felt the subtle, tactile *thunk* of the first thread finding its mating seat. "Now," he whispered.

He gave it a quarter turn clockwise. It slid in. No binding. No metal screaming against metal. 

Florian grabbed the retaining collar, torquing the lock nuts down with two open-ended wrenches until the threads bit into the lock washers. The electric stepper motor clicked once, found its home position, and whirred—a low, clean, harmonic hum that cut straight through the drone of the storm.

On the masthead, the red status LED blinked twice, turned a hard, unyielding green, and stabilized.

Below in the boatyard, Jin Wei’s voice crackled through the battered handheld radio: *"The link is solid! Alexandria port authority has the handshake. The bills of lading for the Amira are verifying in the open ledger right now. The clearinghouse hedge just choked on its own code. The wheat is clearing."*

A ragged, half-strangled laugh tore from Florian’s throat. He slumped back against the grating, his face streaked with soot, engine grease, and melted snow, staring at his trembling fingers.

Marcus stood up slowly, his lower back screaming as the vertebrae uncompressed. The cathedral bells in the distance began their slow, rhythmic toll for noon. Twelve strikes. The clearinghouse deadline had passed, and the silos of the Mediterranean were opening to the dockworkers.

Marcus reached deep into the pocket of his oilskin coat. His fingers closed around an old steel drafting compass—not an ornamental relic, not a family heirloom with sacred inscriptions, but the scarred, utilitarian layout tool Toby Morales had ground out of high-speed tool steel behind the maintenance shed in Detroit during the first weeks of the occupation. Its legs were pitted with rust, its knurled thumbwheel worn smooth by ten thousand adjustments, but its hardened points were still true to a thousandth of an inch.

Marcus held it out across the workbench.

Florian looked up, bewildered. "What is this?"

"A layout divider," Marcus said, placing the cold steel into the boy's greasy palm. "Morales ground the tips out of a broken broach bit. It doesn't care about borders. It doesn't care about clearinghouse debt. It measures what’s actually there. Keep the points sharp, Florian. The assemblies belong to the people who know how to keep them turning."

The apprentice closed his fingers around the steel, his eyes shining in the gray winter light.

Then, from deep within the flooded foundations beneath the boatyard, came a dull, sickening *crump*.

The iron grating under their boots shuddered violently. 

Inside the glass sight-tube mounted against the brick pumping wall, the lake-level column suddenly plummeted two feet, sucking a frantic, whistling vortex of air into the high-pressure cooling circuit. 

Down in the basin, the intake pumps began to cavitate with a high-pitched, metallic shriek.
