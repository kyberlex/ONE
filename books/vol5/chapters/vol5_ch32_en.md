The sleet had turned the cobblestones of the Avenue de la Paix into black grease.

Marcus Hayes leaned into the grade, his thighs burning beneath damp wool trousers. Beside him, Carlos Quispe stumbled once, his boots skidding on a slick seam of granite before Jin Wei’s thick forearm caught him by the elbow. Carlos caught his breath with a rattling, shallow wheeze; the thick, sea-level air of the lake basin sat in his high-altitude lungs like cold cream, heavy and intoxicating.

"Too much air," Carlos muttered, pressing a mitten against his ribs. "My chest doesn't know what to burn."

"Keep your chin tucked," Jin Wei said, his own knee joint clicking with every third step—the familiar rhythmic protest of cartilage ground down over forty years of damp steel decks. "Salt mist is coming off the water behind us. Once that rime gets into the weave of your coat, you don't get warm until May."

Above them, through the skeletal canopy of the Ariana park oaks, the four-node optical laser cut the low-hanging alpine clouds. It wasn't a pristine beam. The freezing drizzle scattered the coherent light into a shimmering, amber halo that pulsed and wavered as atmospheric shears rolled off the Jura mountains. Down on the pier, the old water-works crew was still cursing over lead seals and wax joints, manually adjusting the prism mounts to keep Detroit, Turin, and the Atacama tied into Geneva’s air-gap ledger.

Around them, the city had ceased to be an audience and had become a wall.

They weren't screaming. That was the thing that pressed against Marcus’s eardrums like altitude sickness—the sheer, terrifying density of quiet. More than two hundred thousand men, women, and children stood shoulder to shoulder along the iron palisades of the United Nations compound. They wore whatever had been hanging by their front doors: padded parkas, cantonal railway high-vis vests, sheepskin coats smelling of woodsmoke and sheep dip, wet trench coats pulled tight over business suits. Thermos flasks were passed hand to hand, steam venting from tin cups in brief, ghost-like plumes.

Fifty paces ahead, the monumental wrought-iron gates of the Palais des Nations were sealed shut.

Behind the bars stood the line.

They were not abstract silhouettes. Marcus saw the condensation dripping from the matte-black polycarbonate visors of the Aegis-Apex tactical contract detachment, and behind them, the gray-green parkas of the Swiss Federal territorial battalion. Three armored crowd-control carriers idled behind the ranks, their exhaust pipes belching blue, sulfurous diesel fumes that curled low over the icy tarmac. But the engines were stuttering. The winter grade fuel in their secondary tanks was beginning to paraffin in the sub-zero chill, their alternators whining under the dead-weight draw of high-intensity searchlight arrays.

Elena Vance marched three paces behind Marcus, her eyes locked to an open-frame diagnostic slate cradled in the crook of her arm. Her fingers, red and raw from hours of typing in an unheated boatyard, tapped a rhythm against the cold frame.

"Their tactical telemetry just dropped off the Zurich clearinghouse exchange," Elena said quietly, her voice hoarse, scraped clean of inflection. "Look at their transceivers."

Marcus glanced up. The high-gain omnidirectional whips mounted to the tactical carriers were dead, their indicator beacons flashing amber: *Carrier Lost. No Route to Host.*

"The payroll servers?" Sofia asked, her hand clutching the canvas portfolio containing the printed physical articles.

"Worse," Elena said. "The fuel routing contracts. The Swiss federal fuel card network suspended their fleet authorizations thirty minutes ago when the cantonal council refused to sign the federal emergency policing warrant. Those trucks are burning whatever is left in their primary filters. When the tanks suck air, the hydraulics lock up. They can’t even raise the plow blades."

At the center of the gate, framed by two idling armored wedges, stood Colonel Christian Brandt.

Brandt was fifty-two years old, a career artilleryman from the Bernese Oberland who had spent the last eight years managing logistics for joint cantonal security. His face was gray with forty-eight hours without sleep, his field jacket stiff with rime along the shoulder seams. He wasn't holding a bullhorn. He was holding an analog clipboard under an oilskin flap, his bare thumb compulsively working the tension screw on his sidearm holster—not drawing it, but feeling the physical presence of the latch, like a rosary.

Brandt’s eyes tracked Marcus as the small cluster of engineers and port workers reached the perimeter marker—the white municipal paint stripe frozen into the asphalt twenty meters from the gate.

"Hayes," Brandt called out. His voice was flat, dry, carrying through the dead damp air with the clarity of cracked slate. "Halt your party at the line."

Marcus stopped. Jin Wei halted half a step ahead of him, his shoulder angled to block the wind.

"Colonel," Marcus said. His throat burned from lake damp, but his voice did not lift into an orator's pitch. It stayed level, the cadence of a shop-floor foreman explaining a sheared pin to a shift supervisor. "The Great Hall seated the sortition five minutes ago. One thousand and one delegates. Parity confirmed. The line needs to pass through."

Brandt didn't look at the crowd. He looked down at his clipboard, where four different printouts from the Federal Department of Justice and Police were stapled together, each contradicting the one below it.

"I have an interdiction order from the Federal Council dated zero-two-hundred," Brandt said. He didn't raise his chin. "The perimeter is legally quarantined. No unrecognized assemblies within five hundred meters of the treaty grounds. That order has not been rescinded."

"Your auxiliary power grid failed twenty-two minutes ago, Colonel," Elena said from Marcus’s shoulder. She held up her slate, its monochrome LCD screen glowing pale green. "Your command post at the Pregny Gate is running on lead-acid batteries with thirty percent life left. The canton cut the line from the Verbois hydro station because your command failed to remit the municipal transit clearing fee. It wasn't an attack. The city accounting office simply ran out of clearing credits for your unit code."

One of the Aegis-Apex contractors—a young man with a scarred jaw and the tactical patch of a Baltic private security firm—turned his head sharply toward Brandt. His weapon, a heavy compressed-gas kinetic launcher loaded with hard-rubber baton rings, was slung across his chest, but his gloved fingers were twitching against the foregrip.

"Sir," the contractor muttered over his radio collar, his accent clipped, Baltic. "Base radio is dark. Zurich clearing network says my token is expired. My insurance indemnity is voided if the contract carrier goes unverified for sixty minutes."

"Quiet in the rank," Brandt snapped without turning.

"Colonel," Marcus stepped forward, one boot crossing the white municipal line. The crunch of road salt was loud in the frozen air. "Look behind you. The delegates inside haven't had heat since midnight because your dispatchers tried to freeze them out. The water engineers down at the basin bypassed the municipal valves ten minutes ago. There is steam in the Palais pipes right now, but there is no diesel coming up the hill for your carriers. The highway from Lausanne is blocked by milk trucks and timber haulers who ran out of electronic toll tokens at dawn."

Brandt’s gaze flicked from Marcus to the crowd behind him.

Two hundred thousand people were not advancing. They were simply standing there, breathing into the sleet. A grandmother in an apron over a shearling coat reached down, pulled a vacuum bottle from a wicker basket, and poured a stream of chicory coffee into a dented metal cup. She stepped out of the front rank, walked three paces to the iron fence, and held the cup through the ornamental bars toward an eighteen-year-old Swiss conscript whose teeth were audibly chattering behind his helmet strap.

The young soldier didn't raise his rifle. He looked down at the steaming cup, then looked at his squad sergeant.

The sergeant looked at Brandt.

The wind sheared down from the park, carrying the low, tectonic hum of the Palais’s steam radiators venting into the cold courtyard—the sound of old cast-iron coming back to life under the hands of municipal boiler men.

Brandt looked at Marcus Hayes. He didn't look angry; he looked like a man who had done the ledger math in his head and found himself standing in front of an empty vault.

"If I open this carriage gate," Brandt said, his voice dropping so low only Marcus and Jin Wei could hear, "the Federal Council will issue warrants before noon."

"There is no one in the clearinghouse to process the warrants, Christian," Marcus said, using the man’s first name without emphasis or triumph. "The clerks in Bern went home at four when the payroll packets didn't clear. Look at your slate. The debt jubilee protocol just hit the cantonal registers. Your mortgage in Thun was settled three minutes ago along with mine in Detroit. You don't owe the Consortium your uniform."

Brandt stared at Marcus for three long seconds. His bare thumb finally slipped away from his sidearm holster. He reached up, took off his wet woolen garrison cap, shook the heavy slush from the crown, and pulled it back down over his ears.

He turned toward the armored vehicles.

"Driver," Brandt said into his collar microphone. "Cut the main turbines. Conserve secondary battery reserve."

The deep, diesel rumble of the carriers sputtered and died, leaving only the sound of sleet hissing against the hot manifolds.

Brandt walked to the central security post, inserted a brass master key into the manual gate release box, and turned the tumbler with both hands. The heavy iron latch dropped with a dull, subterranean *clack*.

"Clear the carriage line," Brandt shouted, his voice cracking slightly on the cold air. "Rifles at low-port. Fall back to the portico steps. Let the delegates pass."

The young conscript by the fence reached through the bars, took the steaming cup from the old woman’s hand, and drank.

The iron gates began to swing outward into the snow, groaning on their frozen hinges.

Marcus felt Jin Wei’s heavy hand clap onto his shoulder, the old mariner letting out a long, shuddering breath that smelled of stale tobacco and salt water. "Gate's open, boy," Jin Wei murmured. "Walk inside before your knees lock."

Marcus took his first step through the portal, his boots crunching into the virgin slush of the inner courtyard. Behind him, the two hundred thousand did not rush; they moved forward in a steady, unbroken tide, their boots sounding like a distant river breaking through an ice jam.

Elena was three steps behind him when her slate let out a sharp, high-pitched double chirp.

Marcus turned.

Elena wasn't looking at the crowd or the open gates. Her face had drained of all its remaining color, her jaw clenched tight.

"Marcus," she whispered, her voice barely cutting through the crunch of footsteps. "The Great Hall. The Sortition Assembly didn't call for Article Six."

Marcus stopped on the wet gravel. "What do you mean? We heard the carrier."

Elena turned the slate toward him. The green phosphor display was covered in red parity-error flags, the line-of-sight laser feed from Detroit pulsing erratically like a dying heartbeat.

"That wasn't our broadcast on the podium carrier," Elena said, her finger trembling against the glass. "Someone inside the diplomatic gallery just cut the manual release to the emergency fire shutters. The Great Hall is sealed from the inside."
