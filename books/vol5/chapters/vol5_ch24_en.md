The steel roll-up doors on the freight cars groaned as they hit their ceiling stops, the rattle vibrating through the wet ballast of the rail embankment. 

Marcus did not run toward the noise; he backed toward the workshop threshold, his boots sliding half an inch through slush before finding purchase on the crushed granite of the yard. Down the line of dark boxcars, yellow floodlights snapped on—not sweeping searchlights, but fixed industrial halogen bars mounted to portable generator carts. Men in slate-gray foul-weather gear dropped from the car beds to the gravel. They carried no rifles. Instead, their gloved hands hauled heavy steel jacks, interlocking crowd-control wedges, and spools of coiled concertina wire. 

Aegis-Apex logistical support, working under cantonal emergency transit decrees. They were not here to shoot; they were here to build a cage. If they sealed the rail crossing and the lakeside arterial within twenty minutes, the sortition delegates inside the decommissioned pumping station would be legally and physically quarantined from the Palais des Nations before dawn.

"Marcus!" Elena hissed from the open door, grabbing his wool sleeve and hauling him across the sill. "Inside. Now."

The boatyard shop smelled of paraffin heaters, wet sheepskin, and the ozone tang of overtaxed inverters. On the central worktable, cleared of its lathe fittings, rested the primary hardware token: a scarred slab of milled aluminum with two amber indicator diodes pulsing in staggered rhythm. 

Beside it stood Carlos Quispe, leaning heavily against the zinc-topped bench. The sea-level atmosphere of the lake basin sat thick and wet in his chest; he had rolled a dried bay leaf under his tongue—an old habit from his boyhood sorting raw ore in the cold sheds of Potosí, the bitter oil keeping the dry, hacking spasms at bay. His brown skin looked paper-thin under the fluorescent tubes, but his eyes were clear, fixed entirely on the small liquid-crystal display beside the key slot.

"The third canton is in," Carlos muttered around the leaf, his voice gravel-scraped. "Neuchâtel cleared twelve seconds before they dropped the rail signal. We have five hundred and one verified hardware handshakes."

"They're dropping mobile barricades across the north crossing," Marcus said, peeling off his soaked leather gloves. His knuckles were raw and split, the skin around the nails swollen purple from three days of handling frozen hemp line and wet copper. "Eight freight cars of modular steel fencing. If those interlocks drop, we can't move the assembly up the hill to the gates."

Jin Wei sat on an overturned cable spool near the back wall, systematically taping a fresh strip of friction grip around the shaft of an aluminum boat hook. His knees cracked with the sound of snapping kindling as he stood up. Decades on rolling steel freighters had given his gait a wide, heavy pendulum swing, completely unsuited to the slick, unyielding concrete of dry land. 

"They won't need to finish the fence," Jin Wei said, his flat southern-dialect cadence steady as an anchor chain. "The harbor patrol has already laid an oil boom across the jetty mouth. They aren't trying to storm us. They're letting the frost do the work. By four in the morning, the diesel in our generator tanks will gel. Then the screens go dark."

Giulia Bellini didn't look up from the optical alignment terminal mounted on a surveyor’s tripod by the lake-facing window. A blue bruise colored the hollow beneath her left cheekbone, a souvenir from the train station scramble in Domodossola, but her hands were motionless on the brass micrometer dials. 

"Laser canopy is holding," Giulia whispered, squinting through the crosshairs at the far shore of the lake. "Turin's feed is jittering two milliradians because of the sleet over the water, but the packets are crossing. Detroit and Malacca are slaved to our carrier wave. If we lose the local junction, the ledger stays alive out there—in the clouds, in the hulls, in the salt. But it won't mean a thing here if the delegates can't walk through those Palais doors."

Sophie came out of the back office, carrying a galvanized bucket full of steaming tap water and three cracked mugs. She had an unlit Gauloise tucked behind her ear—a vice she had abandoned twelve years ago and only retrieved from a corner grocer an hour before the lake froze. She set the bucket down hard, water sloshing over the lip.

"The police prefect just signed the transit refusal," she said without preamble. "He cited civil unrest and the risk of hypothermia. No group larger than twelve persons is permitted to traverse the park grounds after midnight."

"How many delegates are in the hall?" Marcus asked.

"Five hundred and one sortition jurors from forty-two nations," Sophie replied. "And another five hundred alternates shivering in the municipal gymnasium behind the station. Plus us."

"A thousand and one," Carlos whispered, his thumb lightly brushing the edge of the aluminum token. "Odd parity. The global assembly. Just as the covenant wrote it."

"It doesn't matter what the covenant wrote if there's five hundred yards of razor-wire between the roadstead and the glass pavilion," Jin Wei said, testing the point of the boat hook against the concrete floor. "We have twenty crowbars and four pallet jacks. We don't have a bulldozer."

A deep, low rumble resonated through the building’s timber foundation. 

It was not the freight train. The train’s engines were idling down, the rhythmic *chug-chug* of the compressors fading into a high-pitched hiss. This sound was continuous, vast, and muffled, like the roll of heavy surf breaking across a shingle beach miles wide.

Marcus pushed past Giulia, wiping a circle of condensation from the lower pane of the clerestory window with his forearm.

The northern rail embankment was no longer dark. 

Down the Rue de Lausanne, pouring out from the narrow alleys of the Pâquis, crossing the rail footbridges, and surging along the lakeside promenades, a line of light was moving through the freezing mist. It was not the cold, piercing white of police strobes or private security floodlights. It was the soft, flickering orange of hundreds, then thousands, then tens of thousands of naked flames.

"What is that?" Giulia breathed, stepping away from her scope.

"The neighborhood councils," Sophie said. Her voice dropped, all the bureaucratic sharpness stripped out of it. She took the bent cigarette from behind her ear and crumpled it into her palm. "They were listening to the mesh broadcast from the boatyard. When the prefect issued the curfew, the canton’s postal workers didn’t strike. They just left their sorting depots and walked into the streets."

Marcus opened the side door. The cold lake air hit him like a wet sheet, but the wind had died. The silence that had descended over the basin was uncanny, broken only by the trudge of wet boots on slush and the faint, dry crackle of burning wax.

They were coming from every quarter of the city. Swiss railway machinists in high-visibility vests over heavy mackinaws. Hospital orderlies from the cantonal clinic still wearing their paper surgical caps under wool beanies. Students from the university, bank tellers from the private institutions across the Rhône who had walked out of their ledger offices when the foreign clearinghouses froze the regional payrolls. Old women with wool shawls pinned under their chins, carrying tea lights inside emptied glass jam jars to shield them from the sleet.

They were not shouting slogans. There were no banners, no amplified megaphones, no clenched fists raised against the night. 

They simply moved into the gap.

Between the idling diesel locomotives of the transport train and the roadstead gates of the Palais, the crowd formed an unbroken, living carpet. They filled the roadway, four ranks deep, then ten, then thirty. When the Aegis-Apex logistics crew attempted to roll the first concertina rack off the flatbed, the crowd did not surge forward or throw stones; they simply linked arms, stepped forward six inches into the slush, and stood still.

The contractor at the crane controls hesitated, his hand hovering over the pneumatic release. Looking down through his cab window, he wasn't looking at an insurgent cell or a line of masked agitators. He was looking directly into the face of a gray-haired woman holding a church taper inside an upside-down water carafe—a woman who lived two floors above his mother’s flat in Meyrin.

The crane motor sputtered, then clicked into neutral. The steel barrier remained suspended three inches above the flatbed, swaying gently in the rising steam of two hundred thousand bodies.

Marcus stepped out onto the gravel yard, Carlos and Elena falling in behind him. The air was warming. The sheer thermodynamic output of a quarter-million human beings crowded into the lake basin had begun to turn the freezing sleet into a fine, drifting vapor, softening the harsh edges of the granite buildings.

A middle-aged man in a soaked postman's cape separated himself from the front rank of the crowd, stepped through the boatyard gate, and approached Marcus. He held out an extra wax taper, its wick already burning with a steady, yellow point of light.

"The road to the pavilion is clear," the man said simply, his French accented with the flat vowels of the Jura valley. "The police have parked their vans across the avenue to block the ministerial convoys. They told us to tell you that the hall is unlocked."

Marcus took the candle. The wax was warm against his stiff, salt-chapped thumb.

He turned toward the open door of the workshop. Carlos emerged, the heavy milled-aluminum token cradled against his chest like an infant, followed by Giulia carrying the master optical transceiver, and Jin Wei, who had laid his boat hook aside to take the arm of an elderly sortition delegate from the Valais.

Behind them, five hundred men and women filed out of the shadows of the boat slips into the light.

Around the entire perimeter of the Palais des Nations, stretching from the water's edge to the high iron gates of the Pregny entrance, the human ring closed—two hundred thousand points of fire standing immovable between the dying architecture of the debt ledger and the assembly within.

High on the gantry above the halted train, the master signal clock clicked over. 

Hour ninety-six died. The red numbers dissolved, re-forming into the sharp, stark glyphs of Hour ninety-seven. 

The siege had broken. The trial of the world had begun.
