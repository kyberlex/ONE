The three hulls did not run running lights. They moved with the low, sullen displacement of working vessels burdened by iron ballast, their diesel exhaust muffled beneath the waterline so that the lake sounded only like a heavy tongue lapping against wet limestone.

"Tugs," Jin Wei muttered, his hand dropping to the frozen rail of the slipway winch. The wool of his watch cap was dusted white with frozen lake mist. "Heavy bow fendering. They aren't trying to shoot their way into the cradle. They're going to shoulder the gates off their tracks and drop mooring lines."

"How long?" Marcus asked. His knuckles were cracked from the dry winter drafts off the Jura, split along the creases where the grease of the Detroit railhead had settled years ago.

"Four minutes at that draft," Jin Wei said. "Look at the bow waves. They’re running on harbor electric auxiliaries to keep the decibels down. Heavy batteries in the bilges. That means once they hit the timber pilings, they don't bounce—they crush."

Behind them, inside the cavernous ribbing of the boatyard, the smell of burnt rye flour was instantly overtaken by the sharp, ozone reek of high-voltage switches snapping closed. Giulia was already herding the neighborhood women and their children toward the rear dry-storage bays, her voice pitched low and flat, stripped of urgency so the panic wouldn't catch. She did not yell; she simply pointed toward the tool cribs beneath the concrete mezzanine, her shoulders hunched against the migraine that had shadowed her left eye since the tear gas on the Italian border.

Carlos Quispe came jogging down the cedar slipway, his rubber work boots slipping once on the skim of black ice before he caught a staysail turnbuckle to steady himself. He was breathing through his teeth, his chest rattling with that dry, gravelly whistle he’d brought down from the four-thousand-meter pans of the Atacama.

"The culvert bypass," Carlos said, slapping a rolled linen schematic against Marcus’s forearm. "The cantonal drainage map. The city didn't disconnect the high-pressure storm overflows when they decommissioned the dock. The pneumatic pumps are still tied into the municipal hydro-flume from the upper weir."

Marcus didn't unroll the paper. There was no light to read it by anyway, save the sickly, diffused emerald shimmer overhead, where the optical laser feed from the mountain station was dying in the manufactured fog. "Can you hold the gate seals without burning the municipal transformer?"

"We don't touch the transformer," Carlos said, coughing into his fist until his eyes watered. "It’s gravity. Six hundred feet of head from the alpine reservoir behind the ridge. The valve is mechanical—four turns on a wheel down in the sludge pit."

"Go," Marcus said.

The transceiver pinned to Marcus's collar hissed violently. Elena’s voice was sheared by static, the packet-loss audible in the broken syllables of her delivery.

"They're thirty yards off the outer pilings," Marcus said into the mesh grill. "Clear the airwaves, Vance. Carlos is on the sluice."

A sharp metallic crack rang across the water.

The lead tug had nudged the outer wooden dolphins guarding the boatyard’s mouth. The seasoned oak timbers, submerged since the late nineteenth century, groaned in their iron hoops, shedding flakes of black creosote into the gray slush. On the forward deck of the nearest craft, three figures clad in drysuits and matte-gray tactical flotation vests stepped to the gunwale. They carried no rifles—the doctrine of the Swiss cantons was rigid enough that an open firearm would bring the federal police helicopters out of Bern within ten minutes. Instead, they carried hydraulic cable-launchers and pneumatic wedge-guns designed to breach industrial roller gates without generating ballistic forensics.

"Hold the threshold!" Jin Wei bellowed.

He didn't reach for an iron bar. He reached for the heavy brass sea-cock lever on the yard’s bilge-wash manifold—a contemporary bronze ball valve installed two seasons ago to flush tourist sloops. With a grunt that brought a sharp wheeze from his saltwater-ruined lungs, he hauled the lever upward ninety degrees.

Beneath the slipway floor, two thousand gallons of stagnant, lake-bottom wash water surged through the brass nozzle array usually reserved for scraping zebra mussels off fiberglass hulls. A vertical fan of brown, icy foam erupted from the threshold stones, hammering straight up into the freezing night air.

The lead tug’s bow struck the water curtain at eight knots.

The pilot’s forward view vanished in a blinding screen of pressurized lake spray. The high-pressure nozzles weren't lethal, but sixty pounds of pressure across a three-inch nozzle tore away the forward deck-line thrown by the lead operator, sending the braided synthetic hawser snaking harmlessly back into the churning screw.

"Now!" Marcus shouted, his voice tearing against the roaring spray.

Deep in the boatyard's subterranean sump, forty feet beneath the frozen sawdust, Carlos Quispe threw his entire weight against the cast-iron spokes of the primary gravity valve. The wheel was cold enough to tear the skin from an unwary palm; he had wrapped his hands in strips of burlap torn from flour sacks. With a metallic shriek that vibrated through the foundation pilings, the bronze gate of the alpine weir feeder slid upward two inches.

The hydrostatic physics took over.

Six hundred vertical feet of water pressure behind the cantonal dam found its escape through the boatyard's pneumatic bubbler lines—the underwater perforations designed decades ago to keep winter ice from crushing yachts against the granite piers.

The lake did not merely splash; it boiled.

A violent, frothing wall of pneumatic upwelling tore across the slipway entrance. Millions of cubic feet of pressurized air and ice-cold meltwater erupted beneath the three black hulls. The lead vessel, deprived of solid water for its twin props to bite, began to cavitate violently, its engine scream rising to a shrill, unloaded whine as the screws whipped through froth. The hull listed twelve degrees to port, slammed sideways against the granite quay, and was pinned there by its own deadweight as the aerated water killed its buoyancy.

Through the roaring geyser of spray, Marcus hauled on the secondary chain hoist.

From the roof trusses above the slipway, two theatrical fog generators—dragged that afternoon from the municipal opera house by four student volunteers—ignited with a hollow *thump*. Thick, glycol-heavy vapor, heated over electric ceramic elements and propelled by three-phase ventilation blowers, cascaded down through the open water-gate like an avalanche of warm, white cotton.

The artificial lake fog manufactured by the tugs collided with the dense, thermal theater vapor rolling out from the drydock.

The result was an impenetrable, milk-white wall.

Marcus stepped to the manual junction box by the door. His left shoulder burned with the dull ache of a torn rotator cuff that had never set right after a crane cable snapped in Toledo seven winters back. He flipped the bank of agricultural quartz work-lights—salvaged from the vegetable greenhouses outside Versoix—directly into the fog.

Sixteen thousand lumens of raw, unshielded tungsten light hit the water curtain.

The effect was instantaneous and absolute. To the men aboard the tugs, looking through night-vision optics and thermal goggles, the universe ceased to have contours, depth, or horizon. The quartz lamps turned the suspended droplets into a solid, blinding mirror of white phosphor. Marcus heard the sharp, frantic clatter of boots slipping on composite decks, the mechanical thump of a pneumatic launcher discharging blind into the lake stones, and the hoarse shout of a tactical commander calling for reverse pitch.

"They're fouling their wheels!" Jin Wei laughed, a short, barking sound from deep in his barrel chest. "The aerator’s pulling their stern down! Back off, you bastards, or sink on your own anchors!"

The second tug tried to sheer off toward the open basin, its rudder thrown hard over. But the aerated channel offered no purchase; the vessel skidded crabwise across the boil, its steel flank groaning against the rotting timber fenders of the breakwater before its captain cut the throttles to keep from rolling the craft into the surf.

Through the howling mist and the hiss of thirty tons of displaced water, Marcus stood at the water's edge, soaking wet from the knees down, the hem of his canvas coat stiffening with instantaneous frost. The cold penetrated his boots, finding the old fracture in his right heel, but he did not step back.

The lead tug was reversing now, its diesel revving with a smoky, choking cough as it backed out of the aeration field, retreating into the deeper gray of the lake. The raid had lasted four minutes. Not a single shot had been fired; not a single hand had been laid on an officer of the consortium. The ledger tokens remained air-gapped in the dry cedar hold behind them, humming their silent mathematical proofs to the mountain mirrors.

Then the concrete under Marcus's boots gave a heavy, sickening shudder.

It was not the tugs. They were already fifty yards out, their wakes dying into the fog.

Carlos burst up through the sludge-pit hatch, his face gray with silt, his fingers bleeding through the burlap wraps. He didn't look at the lake. He looked straight down at the foundation floor.

"The culvert," Carlos gasped, his voice shredded by stone dust. "The backflow didn't vent to the lake. The main municipal return pipe under the boatyard floor… someone welded an iron sluice shut from the street side three hours ago. The pressure has nowhere to go."

Marcus looked down.

Through the cracks in the century-old concrete apron, thick, black lake-water was beginning to pulse upward in rhythmic, violent jets, carrying with it the smell of stagnant mountain clay. The entire slipway floor was rising on a hydraulic cushion, lifting the foundation toward the timber roof like a cork in a bottle.
