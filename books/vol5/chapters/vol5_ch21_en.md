The cold came in through the boatyard’s shattered clerestories like an axe blade. 

Two hundred thousand people packed along the Quai du Mont-Blanc were suddenly murmuring in the gloom, their collective breath rising into the alpine twilight as a vast, pale cloud. Below the gantry, the one thousand and one sortition jurors were silhouettes against the zinc water-gates. Then the lake answered: the twin-screw whine of fast-displacement patrol hydrofoils cutting through the chop off the Jet d'Eau, their searchlights slicing yellow wedges across the ice-slick corrugated roof.

"Grid failure is absolute from the substation at Cornavin down to the harbor mouth," Lin Zhang shouted, her boots skidding on the slush-rimmed steel stairs of the mezzanine. She didn't have a screen; the digital monitors were dead glass. She carried a three-ring binder clamped under her elbow, her wool fingerless mittens—lined with rabbit fur her sister had stitched in Harbin thirty years ago—stiff with grease and salt spray. "Primary feeder cables are cut at the transformer vault. We’re on the localized hydrogen cells, and the busbar won’t carry both loads!"

"The sortition air-gap stays closed, Lin!" Elena Vance’s voice cut through the dark, hoarse and serrated by four straight days of filings, injunctions, and three hours of sleep on an oilcloth cot. She stood over the low pine rostrum on the dry-dock floor, her small brass plumb-bob—a surveyor’s relic she rolled between her palms to hide the tremor in her thumbs—clicking rhythmically against the wood. "If you drain the voting registry's internal cell to feed the rooftop transmitter, the physical cryptographic tokens lose parity. If the parity drops below odd-count verification, every cantonal court from here to Zurich will classify the debt cancellation as an arbitrary seizure! The charter becomes a scrap of wet paper!"

"If I don’t power the roof laser array within ninety seconds," Lin fired back, hitting the bottom step with a jarring thud that sent a spasm up her spine, "the atmospheric canopy over the Alps folds. Turin goes dark. Detroit loses the return-carrier. If the world outside this shed can’t witness the tally in real time, the clearinghouses will announce that this entire assembly was evacuated at gunpoint! They’re already spinning the blackout as a terror incident on the wire feeds!"

Outside, the hydrofoils reversed their screws. The roar of back-churning water hit the steel water-gates with the concussive rattle of gravel fired from a cannon.

"We have seven hundred and fifty-one votes to verify!" Elena stepped forward, her coat collar turned up against the draft, her chapped lips pale. "That is seventy-five percent of a global sortition body drawn by lot under Class Zero law! You do not strip the legal agency of one thousand and one human beings to maintain your broadcast vanity!"

"It isn’t vanity, you idiot, it’s survival!" Lin lunged toward the breaker cabinet mounted on the damp concrete footing. "Without the light, we’re a rumor in a dark barn!"

"Touch that manual bypass," Elena snapped, stepping directly between Lin and the safety-taped switchgear, "and I will enter a formal point of criminal obstruction on the perpetual record before this floor!"

Around them, the sortition floor was fracturing. A delegate from Mendoza was on his feet, his hands raised in panic as lake water began to slosh under the warped timber seals of the lower slipway. The Hokkaido woman gripped her wool skein, her eyes darting between the dark water-gates and the armed patrol boats idling fifty yards out on the black swell. Panic was a grease fire: odorless, cold, and spreading along the edges of the room.

Marcus Hayes did not raise his voice. He reached past both women, caught the heavy bronze lanyard of the boatyard’s mechanical shift whistle—a counterweighted brass valve salvaged from the old steam slipway—and pulled it down with the full weight of his shoulder.

The whistle didn't shriek; without high-pressure steam, it emitted a deep, pneumatic *groan* that resonated through the timber deck and vibrated in the soles of twelve hundred pairs of boots.

Silence snapped shut over the hall. Even the hydrofoils outside seemed to pause, their muffled diesels idling in the low chop.

"Covenant Article Eight, Section One," Marcus said. His voice was cracked, raw from breathing Detroit coal soot and Geneva frost, but it was anchored by the stubborn mass of a man who had spent forty years prying rusted pipe out of frozen clay. "Mandatory five-minute direct session. No motions. No theoretical filings. No touching the switches."

Elena’s jaw tightened. "Marcus, the legal quorum—"

"Five minutes," Marcus said, not looking at her. He didn't check an heirloom watch. He didn't count the seconds aloud. He reached down, took Lin by the elbow of her sheepskin coat, and took Elena by the sleeve of her salt-stained wool mantle. "You two want to litigate thermodynamics? You do it down in the bilge where the copper is actually sweating."

He hauled them down the narrow wooden ramp into the lower mechanics’ pit beneath the dry-dock platform. Down here, the smell of grease, rotten lake weed, and stagnant ballast water was thick enough to choke on. A single, low-wattage yellow emergency filament, fed by an auxiliary lead-acid truck battery, flickered over the iron junction manifolds. 

Carlos Quispe was already down there, his breath rattling wetly in his chest as he struggled to seal an auxiliary water line with an iron wrench. The sudden descent from four thousand meters in the Atacama to the thick, oxygen-dense air of the Swiss basin had kept his head swimming for two days, but his hands were steady on the pipe collar.

"The hydrogen cell isn't the bottleneck," Marcus said, pointing a thick, scarred finger at the manifold. His early days salvaging tugs on Lake Huron had taught him that when men argued about policy, the machinery was usually just fouled by silt. "Look at the return pressure gauge on the cooling loop. Lin, your roof laser isn't dying from lack of juice. The heat exchanger for the optical emitter is choked with frozen slush from the lake intake."

Lin dropped to her knees, heedless of the greasy bilge water soaking through her canvas trousers. She pressed her bare palm against the bronze pipe. It was stone-cold. "The intake strainer... the surface ice on the slipway is jamming the mesh."

"And your cryptographic token rack," Marcus turned to Elena, "runs on twenty-four volts direct current. It doesn't need the main three-phase busbar. Lin's people wired the voting consoles into the primary inverter rack because it was convenient during the Tuesday setup, not because the protocol requires it."

Elena knelt beside Lin, the brass plumb-bob swinging still on its twine. Her legal training had spent twenty years in high-ceilinged appellate chambers with radiant heat and oak paneling; down here, surrounded by weeping rivets and cold iron, the neat geometry of administrative code vanished into raw mass and kinetic friction. She looked at the copper terminal blocks, her eyes narrowing as she traced the thick rubber-jacketed cables.

"The air-gap only needs local continuity," Elena whispered. Her breath puffed white against the rusty cast-iron casing. "The sortition tokens can store their cryptographic parity on internal capacitive memory for forty minutes without external draw. As long as the physical loop between the thousand and one seats isn't broken, the odd-parity count remains forensically sound under the Hague standard."

"Which leaves twelve kilowatts of hydrogen reserve," Lin said, her voice dropping from a shout to a fierce, rapid calculation. She didn't look at Elena; she looked at the pipe. "If Carlos and I blow the backwash valve on the intake using the hand-pump, we clear the ice from the heat exchanger. The laser stays lit at sixty percent luminosity—enough to punch through the mountain fog to the relay on the Salève."

"And the sortition floor?" Marcus asked Elena.

Elena wiped a smear of cold grease from her knuckle against her trousers. "We don't light the overhead hall. We don't need the monitors. We use the mechanical tally drums on the pine tables. Every delegate drops their wooden token into the copper scale. The weight is self-proving. One thousand and one tokens. If the balance tips past seven hundred and fifty-one, the supermajority is verified by gravity, not by an algorithm the cantonal police can claim was spoofed by our servers."

Lin looked up from the bilge. Her rabbit-fur mittens were black with machine grease. For three seconds, the two women stared at each other across the narrow trench, the icy lake water lapping at the soles of their boots.

"Four minutes," Lin said, pulling an adjustable spud wrench from Carlos's canvas roll. "Carlos, give me the cheater bar on this flange. We have sixty seconds to crack this valve before the array drops offline."

"Elena," Marcus said, his voice flat. "Get up on the floor. Tell the jurors to keep their coats on and get their tokens in hand. No speeches."

Elena didn't nod; she simply turned and hauled herself up the timber ladder, her boots pounding on the rungs. 

Marcus stayed in the trench, his shoulder braced against Lin’s back as she threw her weight against the rusted iron handle of the backwash valve. The metal screeched, resisting, locked by decades of lime-scale and sub-zero frost.

"Pull," Marcus grunted, his ribs aching from the old steel-mill break that always flared in the damp. "Don't jerk it. Lean into the iron."

With a wet, violent *thwack*, the valve seat gave way. A geyser of black, freezing lake sludge erupted from the bypass nipple, splashing Lin’s cheek and soaking Marcus’s chest. Above them, in the rafters, a low, singing hum began—the optical laser array's cooling pump clearing its throat as clean water surged through the copper coils.

High overhead, through the open clerestories, a pale green beam of coherent light cut through the freezing lake mist, shooting skyward toward the gray limestone cliffs of the Mont Salève.

Outside on the water, the hydrofoil turbines suddenly screamed—not in retreat, but surging forward at full throttle.

A violent shudder ran through the entire boatyard as the first vessel’s bow struck the outer timber pilings of the slipway barrier. 

From the gantry above, Giulia’s voice cut through the dark, cold and sharp as broken glass:

*"They aren't trying to cut the power anymore. They’re running the water-gate."*
