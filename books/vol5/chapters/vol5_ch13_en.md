The damp of Lake Geneva did not fall; it hung in the air like suspended grease, penetrating the wool of Marcus Hayes’s coat until the fabric weighed twice what it had on the bridge. Beneath his boots, the slipway cobbles were slick with a sheen of crushed river-silt and half-frozen drizzle. 

Beside him, Carlos Quispe stumbled, his hand shooting out to grab an iron mooring bollard. The Andean miner’s breath came in ragged, shallow gasps. At sea level, with nearly four thousand meters of atmospheric pressure suddenly resting on his chest, the air felt thick enough to drown in. Carlos reached inside his frayed collar, fishing out a sliver of dried bitter peel he habitually pressed against his gum when descending into deep shafts—a private trick to keep the nausea of heavy air from turning his stomach.

“Too much soup in the throat,” Carlos wheezed, his eyes watering against the emerald glare that still bathed the mist above the harbor. “The air here has no bone in it.”

“Keep moving,” Marcus murmured. He rubbed the base of his thumb, where the cold had settled into the old calcified crack from an industrial press accident thirty years ago. As a young apprentice, long before Sarah took her first breath, he had learned to gauge the rhythm of a failing die by timing the interval between his own pulse and the shudder of the floorboards. Right now, his pulse was too fast, erratic against the rhythmic, low-frequency roar of the lake pumps behind them.

They slipped through a gap in the corrugated iron fence of the Société Nautique boatyard. The yard was cavernous, smelling of creosote, rotting bilge-water, and the wet sawdust of dry-docked racing hulls. Suspended from the rafters overhead, wooden skiffs hung in the shadows like the ribcages of dead whales. 

In the center of the concrete floor, huddled around a single three-burner propane heater salvaged from a caterer’s van, were the others.

Giulia Bellini stood with her back to an overturned dinghy. Her face was gray with post-concussive exhaustion, her dark curls plastered to her forehead by sleet. From her days rigging tower cranes above the Fiat yards in Turin, she had carried the compulsive habit of testing any tensioned wire within reach; even now, her thumb snapped rhythmically against the stay-cable of an upturned mast, testing its pitch against the ringing in her damaged left ear. 

When she saw Marcus, the hard line of her jaw softened, just for a fraction of a second, before settling back into iron.

“You’re late, Detroit,” she said, her voice raspy from smoke and alpine wind.

“The bridge was packed five deep with students and nurses,” Marcus replied, stepping into the yellow circle of the heater’s warmth. “The cantonal police have their vans parked across the Rue du Rhône, but they aren't swinging batons. They’re just watching the water plume.”

Footsteps crunched on the gravel outside the slipway doors. Jin Wei entered first, moving with a wide, pitching gait. Decades on the iron decks of container haulers had ruined his knees for cobblestones; on dry land, his inner ear still braced for a five-degree roll, making him look like a man trying to cross a skating rink in heavy work boots. He paused inside the threshold, spitting out a small wedge of raw ginger he kept tucked in his cheek to stave off land-sickness, and looked at Marcus.

Behind Jin came Elena Vance and Dr. Lin Zhang. Elena looked as though she had been dragged through a thresher. Her fingernails were stained with violet ink from the manual spirit duplicators they had used when the digital networks were jammed in Marseille, and she carried a heavy, waterproof canvas satchel pinned tight against her ribs like a cracked bone.

For a long moment, no one spoke. 

These were people who had known each other only as encrypted packet signatures, dry logistical manifests, and desperate voices crackling through shortwave relays across ten thousand miles of hostility. They were strangers who had gambled their lives on the assumption that the person on the other end of the line was not a coward.

Jin was the first to cross the floor. He extended a hand—thick-fingered, scarred across the knuckles from a snapped winch wire in the Malacca Strait. Marcus took it. The grip was dry, cold, and unrelenting.

“Hayes,” Jin said. It was neither a question nor an honorific. It was a receipt of delivery.

“Captain,” Marcus acknowledged. 

Carlos stepped forward, extending his hand to Lin Zhang. The economist’s fingers were pale and trembling from seventy-two hours without proper sleep, but she held the miner’s calloused palm with both of hers, bowing her head slightly in an unscripted gesture of deference that silenced the room.

“The water column is holding,” Elena said, her voice flat and urgent as she dropped the canvas satchel onto an empty cable drum. “The optical laser canopy is locked. Eight billion screens are receiving the feed from Detroit, Turin, Atacama, and the Strait through the Jet d’Eau. But we are burning borrowed time.”

She unbuckled the satchel and pulled out a stack of physical continuous-feed printer paper, the green-and-white margins torn and smudged with oil. 

“The Swiss cantonal authorities won't send tactical squads into the crowd,” Elena continued, looking around the circle. “They aren't stupid. Two hundred thousand people between the Cornavin station and the lakeshore means any kinetic action becomes an immediate municipal riot. But the Sovereign Debt Consortium found the valve that matters.”

Lin Zhang leaned over the drum, pointing a short, blunt pencil at a column of ledger entries. “It’s not a military blockade. It’s an administrative quarantine. An hour ago, the Emergency Stabilization Board in Basel invoked the clearinghouse insolvency statutes. They’ve placed the Geneva municipal utility under federal asset receivership.”

Giulia stopped snapping the mast wire. “What does that mean for the pumps?”

“It means,” Lin Zhang said quietly, “that as of midnight, the electrical feed to the lake pumps isn't being cut by a switch. It’s being cleared through a bankruptcy receiver. The cantonal grid controllers have been served with personal criminal warrants. If they keep routing municipal hydro-power to the harbor pumps to sustain the optical fountain, they face immediate personal seizure of their pension funds and thirty years in solitary confinement under federal treason clauses.”

“Paper,” Carlos muttered, spitting into the dust. “They fight with paper while our people freeze on the ridges.”

“Paper moves the water, Carlos,” Marcus said, his voice low. “Without the current, the pumps die. If the Jet d'Eau drops, the four beams lose their prism. The global broadcast breaks up in the low clouds, and the assembly becomes an unverified riot in an unheated shed.”

“The first sortition delegates are already inside the perimeter,” Elena said. She glanced at an old Swiss railway pocket watch she had laid on the cable drum—its glass face spider-webbed with fine cracks. “Three hundred from the European bioregions arrived by regional rail before the lines were choked. Another two hundred crossed the Jura on foot with the snow-plow crews. By morning, all one thousand and one will be within the cordon. But they cannot vote on an invisible ledger. The air-gapped tokens need the optical link to verify the debt jubilee protocol against the global nodes.”

A cold draft swept through the shed as the corrugated iron walls groaned under a fresh squall off the lake. Above them, the dull, hydraulic thunder of the five-hundred-foot geyser seemed to stutter—a minuscule hesitation in the air, like an engine missing a single stroke.

Jin Wei’s head snapped toward the ceiling. “Did you hear that?”

“The intake pressure,” Marcus said, his stomach tightening.

Before Elena could answer, the wooden wicket door at the far end of the slipway was kicked inward. 

A young man in the grease-streaked orange coveralls of the Geneva water-works stood in the opening, chest heaving, his face white under the sodium dock lights. He carried an adjustable pipe wrench in one hand, the jaw of the tool wrapped in blue electrician’s tape.

“They aren't waiting for midnight,” the mechanic gasped, leaning against the doorframe to keep from falling. “The federal receivers just arrived at the Saint-Jean hydro-substation. They didn't bring troops. They brought six private bailiffs from Aegis-Apex and a cantonal magistrate with an emergency court injunction.”

Marcus stepped toward him. “Are they shutting the gates?”

“Worse,” the boy said, his voice cracking with panic. “They’re padlocking the manual bus-tie breakers under commercial salvage law. The municipal operators are refusing to touch the levers, but the bailiffs brought their own certified electrical engineers. If they trip the primary breakers at Saint-Jean, the entire lakefront grid drops in four minutes. The pumps will stall, the fountain will collapse, and the optical canopy will tear itself to pieces.”

Giulia reached down, grabbed a heavy iron pry-bar leaning against the dry-docked boat, and turned toward the door. 

“No,” Marcus barked, his voice cutting through the shed like a dry branch snapping in the frost. “No iron. No weapons. If one bailiff bleeds, the cantonal police have their pretext to clear the entire basin with armored water sweeps.”

“Then how do we hold the breakers, Marcus?” Giulia whispered, her eyes wild with the cold and the pain behind her temples. “They have the law, they have the court seals, and they have the keys.”

Outside, across the black water, the emerald pillar of light flickered again—a long, agonizing shudder that sent a ripple of darkness through the clouds above the city.
