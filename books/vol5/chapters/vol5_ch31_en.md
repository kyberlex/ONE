The slipway smelled of dead mussels, river silt, and frozen grease.

"Drop the cradle," Jin Wei barked, his boots skidding on the slush-caked timber of the boatyard ramp. "Not the winch cable—the counterweight dog! Drop it now!"

Pavel didn't hesitate. Whatever conditioned obedience had kept the young contractor bound to his private security muster rolled over into the simple, brute reflex of survival. He lunged across the rusted iron gantry, seized the five-foot iron release lever with both calloused hands, and threw his full weight against the pawl.

The rusted pawl groaned, sheared through three inches of brittle surface ice, and slammed back into its housing.

With a deafening shriek of ungreased cast iron, three tons of oak-and-steel drydock cradle slid down the greased incline. It plunged into the black swell of the Petit-Lac fifty feet ahead of the approaching tug, throwing up a five-yard curtain of churning, freezing spray.

Through the mist, the low, blunt silhouette of the cantonal tug surged through the dark water. Its deck-mounted hydraulic lance—built to blast winter pack-ice from the locks of the Rhône—was already spitting a high-pressure jet that tore white trenches through the chop. But the boatyard cradle hit the lake bottom with the dull, shuddering thump of ancient ballast stone, its submerged oak timbers jamming tight between the riprap boulders.

The tug’s twin bronze screws bit directly into the submerged tangle of cradle cables.

The sound that followed was purely mechanical agony: the sharp, metallic scream of twin diesel drive shafts seizing against knotted steel wire, followed by the deep, resonant *thunk* of engine governor cutoffs tripping in the engine room. The vessel’s bow slewed hard to starboard, its wake collapsing into greasy foam as the current carried it against the timber pilings of the municipal jetty. It rocked there, immobilized, thirty feet short of the concrete conduit where the deep-lake fiber casing vanished into the silt.

From the wheelhouse, a single searchlight flickered twice, blinded by the dense theatrical fog drifting from the yard's low-pressure steam vents, and then died. The diesels fell silent. Only the lake remained, lapping against the stone.

"Shafts fouled," Jin Wei exhaled. His breath bloomed in the sub-zero air, ragged and gray. His knuckles, swollen from forty years of salt damp, unclenched slowly from the handrail. "They’re dead in the water. No steerage."

Inside the drafting shed, the optical reception monitor didn’t flicker. The phosphor trace held unbroken.

Elena Vance leaned her forehead against the edge of the aluminum chassis. A single bead of sweat, cold as well-water, rolled down the hollow beneath her cheekbone. She didn't cheer. She didn't speak. She simply reached out with a grease-stained thumb and tightened the knurled lock-ring on the horizontal prism mount.

Across the room, near the potbelly stove that smelled of burning pine resin and damp birch bark, Sofia stood watching the silent men.

She was forty-one, with the straight, unyielding spine of someone who had spent her adolescence hauling crates of salted cod along the docks of Vigo before winning a municipal scholarship to the law faculty at Salamanca. Her hands were broad, her skin weathered by the Atlantic wind she had left behind two decades ago, but her fingers were meticulously clean. She wore a heavy wool overcoat with mismatched horn buttons, the collar pulled high to cover a thick, pale surgical scar that ran from the hinge of her jaw to her clavicle—the somatic souvenir of an administrative arrest in Madrid eight years earlier, when private bailiffs had cleared a tenement block with kinetic batons and zip-ties.

She looked at Pavel. The young contractor was trembling, not from cowardice, but from the sudden, nauseating plunge of adrenaline. His military-grade tactical jacket was torn at the elbow, revealing gray thermal weave smeared with rust from the drydock lever. He looked down at his own palms, raw and bleeding where the frozen iron had torn away the skin.

Van Houten was staring at him too, but with the hollow, terrifying gaze of an old bureaucrat who knows the execution order has already been filed.

"The police boats will be at the mole in twenty minutes," Van Houten murmured, his voice cracking like dry reed. He pulled his cashmere scarf tight against his throat, as if he could already feel the zip-tie. "You broke cantonal maritime quarantine. You harbored us. When the G20 stabilization board files the extradition warrants under the mutual assistance treaties, there are no civil courts open. They take you to the federal bunker in Sion. They take Pavel to Frankfurt. They take me..." His jaw twitched. "They don't take me anywhere. They settle the accounts on the tarmac."

Sofia walked slowly toward the center of the room. Her boots made no sound on the packed gravel floor. From the pocket of her coat, she did not draw a document, a seal, or an encrypted tablet. She drew a flat piece of slate, four inches square, its edges beveled by hand with an ordinary wood rasp.

She laid it on the scarred surface of the trestle table between Pavel’s bleeding hands and Van Houten’s trembling fingers.

"No one is going to Sion," Sofia said. Her voice was surprisingly quiet, pitched in the low, resonant cadence of a Galician harbor master reading an evening squall warning. "And no one is going to Frankfurt."

Van Houten let out a dry, bitter bark of laughter. "You think you have the authority to grant asylum? You hold an abandoned boatyard and a mirror on a tripod. The Sovereign Debt Consortium holds the penal codes of seventy-four nations."

"They hold statutes of debt," Sofia corrected him. She didn't raise her voice. She pulled out a wooden stool, its legs uneven on the stone, and sat down. "A statute is an agreement to inflict physical distress on a body until a ledger balance is restored. That was the old grammar. It ended forty minutes ago."

Pavel looked up, his lips parted. "I cut the cantonal line yesterday at St. Prex. I placed the acoustic charges on the municipal intake. If they put me in an interrogation cell—"

"There are no interrogation cells," Sofia said, looking directly into the young man’s bloodshot eyes. "Not for you. Not for Van Houten. Not for the men in that tug whose propellers are currently wrapped in ten-gauge steel wire."

Carlos Quispe stepped in from the slipway door, stamping the crusted snow from his felt boots. He carried a heavy cast-iron kettle, steam whistling softly from its brass spout, and set it on the iron rim of the stove. The rich, earthy smell of roasted barley and bitter chicory drifted into the damp chill of the room. He poured three tin mugs without a word, sliding one toward Pavel, one toward the former clearinghouse director, and keeping the third between his own scarred, calloused palms.

"Article Six," Sofia said, tapping the flat slate with her index finger. "It is the sixth invariant. It cannot be suspended by an assembly of three, or an assembly of three hundred, or the thousand who sit in the Palais tonight. It cannot be traded away for an electrical grid, or a grain shipment, or peace."

She leaned forward, her dark eyes reflecting the amber flicker of the draft vents.

"Absolute bodily autonomy. Zero retaliatory punishment."

Van Houten blinked. "That's absurd. That is sentimental anarchy. If there is no punishment for breach of contract, if there is no physical deterrence against sabotage, the social mechanism collapses in seventy-two hours. Men like Pavel—men like I was—will take whatever is not bolted to the bedrock."

"You took because the ledger told you that scarcity was the only condition under which you were allowed to eat," Sofia answered, her tone devoid of malice, carrying only the hard, unyielding weight of granite. "The old courts needed your skin because your skin was the final collateral on an unpayable interest rate. When the money failed, they took your liberty. When your liberty wasn't enough, they broke your jaw."

She touched the scar on her neck with the tip of one finger—an unconscious, mechanical gesture, old as habit.

"Article Six establishes that human tissue is not property. It cannot be pledged, seized, quarantined for vengeance, or compelled to labor through physical deprivation. If a man breaks a machine, we repair the machine. If a man cuts a cable, we lay another line. If a man cannot live beside his neighbors without violence, we walk him to the perimeter, we give him sixty days of dry rations, his clothing, his tools, and his unencumbered name, and we show him the road."

Pavel’s hands tightened around the warm tin mug. The chicory was bitter, but the heat seeped into the cracked skin of his thumbs. "You don't lock me up?"

"Where would we build the cage, Pavel?" Marcus Hayes asked from the dark doorway of the battery annex.

He stepped into the yellow light, his heavy canvas coat dusted with hoarfrost. His shoulders carried the immense, dragging fatigue of forty years of struggle, but his eyes were extraordinarily clear.

"Every prison requires three men to watch one," Marcus said quietly. "It requires a wall that must be guarded, an iron door that must be forged, and a ration that must be withheld to enforce obedience. We don't have the thermodynamic margin to feed jailers. We have work to do."

A sudden, sharp metallic ping resonated through the concrete footing beneath their feet.

Elena didn't look up from her screen, but her voice cut through the room like cold glass. "That wasn't the tug. That was the primary cantonal distribution ring under the Place des Nations."

Marcus moved to the table in two strides. "Telemetry?"

"Not an interdiction," Elena whispered, her fingers flying across the mechanical keyboard. "The Swiss cantonal guards at the perimeter gates. They just opened the hydraulic bypass valves on the main heating conduit to the assembly hall."

Van Houten stiffened. "Why would they do that? That conduit heats the entire diplomatic quarter. If they dump the steam pressure into the lake—"

"They aren't dumping it," Elena said, her eyes tracking the green phosphor vectors rising across the map of the city. "They're routing it into our heat exchanger. The cantonal engineers just disconnected from the consortium’s central thermal supervisory loop."

Outside, through the shattered transom windows of the boatyard, the deep, mournful horn of a lake barge sounded once—not an alarm, but the long, solitary signal for a vessel clearing the channel under full helm.

Then, from the dark lane behind the slipway, came the crunch of gravel under heavy, measured footsteps.

Jin Wei’s hand dropped instinctively to the heavy brass dogging wrench at his hip. "Company on the road. Four men. Unarmed. They're carrying something white."

Marcus didn't move. "Let them walk in."

The timber door swung inward on its iron hinges, admitting a blast of sub-zero wind that sent the stove's embers flaring bright orange.

Framed in the opening stood an elderly man in the gray woolen uniform of the Geneva Municipal Water Works, his white mustache caked with rime. Behind him, two younger technicians in yellow reflective bibs carried a heavy wooden crate between them, its stenciled Swiss cantonal crest faded with age.

The old man looked past Jin Wei, past the cables snaking across the gravel floor, and stopped his eyes on Marcus Hayes.

"The Palais des Nations has three hundred delegates inside who are shivering," the old engineer said, his voice flat, dry, and thick with the accent of the Vaudois hills. "Our dispatchers in Bern told us to shut the boilers down at midnight to force the hall to clear. We took a vote in the boiler room fifteen minutes ago. Seven of us."

He stepped aside as his men set the crate down on the floor with a hollow wooden thud. He reached down and pried open the lid with a cold-chisel, revealing twelve heavy, wax-sealed lead containers of spare optical junction prisms.

"We told Bern the valve was frozen," the old man said, pulling off his damp woolen mittens. "Now show us where to splice these into your laser line before the frost gets into the mirrors."

Sofia looked at Pavel, then at Van Houten. Neither man spoke.

Marcus walked to the old engineer, reached out his hand, and gripped the man's frozen sleeve. "The line runs under the pier," Marcus said. "We have twenty minutes before the frost sets in."

Before the old man could answer, the high-frequency monitor on Elena's desk emitted a flat, continuous tone. Not a telemetry alert.

A live, uncompressed voice carrier from the podium inside the Great Hall of the Palais des Nations, three kilometers up the hill.

A woman's voice, shaking with exhaustion but perfectly intelligible, cut through the quiet:

*"The Assembly of Sortition is seated. Parity verified at one thousand and one. We are entering the constitutional readings. Where is the draft for Article Six?"*

Sofia picked up the slate from the table, turned toward the open lake, and pulled her collar tight against the rising wind.
