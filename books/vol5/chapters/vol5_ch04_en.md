The telescope dome atop the grain elevator shook as thirty-knot gusts barrelled across the frozen expanse of the Detroit River. Through the slit in the rusted corrugated steel, the night was an ocean of black glass, pricked only by the red navigation beacons of freighters locked in the ice near Belle Isle.

Marcus Hayes wiped grease and freezing condensation from his goggles with the cuff of his mackinaw. His hands were stiff, the joints in his index fingers swollen to the size of walnuts from three days of tightening turnbuckles in the sub-zero draft. He reached into his breast pocket and tapped the flat edge of an old brass seamstress gauge he had carried for twelve winters—a notched strip of metal he used to gauge tolerances when his eyes grew too bloodshot to read a ruler. The cold brass bit through his flannel shirt, grounding him against the nausea of sheer fatigue.

Beside him on the wood-planked platform, the optical emitter hummed. It was not the clean, silent beam of a laboratory bench. The primary mirror—salvaged from an astronomical observatory in Ann Arbor and repolished by hand with jeweller’s rouge—groaned on its worm gear every time the wind hit the housing. Downward, through the floorboards, thick bundles of optical glass wrapped in heavy vulcanized rubber snake-coiled through the grain chutes into the building's belly.

"Beam drift is three arc-seconds," Toby called out from the access hatch. Toby's face was chalky under the yellow glare of an unshielded work lamp, his lips chapped and bleeding where he had gnawed at them. He wasn't wearing gloves; he had tucked his fingers into his armpits to keep the sensation alive enough to adjust the fine micrometers. "The stratospheric balloon over Newfoundland is shedding altitude. Sleet on the envelope. She’s sagging four hundred meters every ten minutes."

"Trim the counter-weights," Marcus said, his voice raw from breathing dry frost. "Keep the gimbal wet. If the grease freezes in the gear teeth, we lose the horizon."

A sharp, violent click echoed through the casing. The blue carrier indicator on the receiver panel flickered, dimmed to a sullen amber, and then flared white.

Across three thousand miles of salt-spray fog, low-pressure squalls, and the freezing North Atlantic dark, the beam had struck the passive optical reflector drifting eight miles above the Grand Banks, skipping down the curvature of the earth toward the Azorean relay station. On the oscilloscope face, the wave form was ragged—chewed at the edges by atmospheric scatter and thermal bloom—but the pulse was unbroken.

Detroit Node 1 was locked to Europe.

Marcus let out a breath that formed an instant cloud inside his collar. "Hold that alignment with the manual friction wheels. Don't let the servos hunt. The motors will burn out if they fight the gale."

He didn't wait for Toby’s nod. He scrambled down the iron rung ladder, his work boots ringing on the frozen rungs, descending fifty feet into the warm, smelling core of the former engine works.

Below, the atmosphere was suffocating. Three kerosene space heaters chattered in the corners, filling the low-ceilinged room with the sweet, oily stink of burning paraffin. The floor was a mosaic of plywood sheets covering drainage trenches.

Elena Vance was sitting on an upturned wooden cable spool, her knees pulled tight against the rim of an iron brazier. She was chewing on a dried clove stem to cut the metallic taste of sleeplessness, her dark hair pinned back with a bent bicycle spoke. On the drafting table in front of her lay no screens, but three thick pads of manifold carbon paper, each sheet scribbled with tight, furious lines of jurisdictional precedent and maritime salvage law. Elena never trusted digital registers when the stakes turned sovereign; years ago, during an eviction injunction in an unheated Cook County basement, she had seen five years of public housing trust records erased by a single remote database wipe. Since that winter, her true law lived on paper that smelled of wood pulp and carbon black.

She didn't look up when Marcus entered. She was watching a needle on a mechanical barograph scratch a jagged valley into a paper drum.

"Eurocontrol just dropped the hammer," Elena said, her voice dry as dead grass. "The Emergency Stabilization Board declared the North Atlantic flight track an exclusion zone. Mandatory grounding for all non-scheduled civilian traffic. They’re claiming unregistered optical telemetry constitutes an unregulated navigational hazard."

Marcus unbuttoned his coat, his cracked knuckles throbbing as the room's heat hit his skin. "What’s waiting at Willow Run?"

"Nothing," Elena replied, stabbing her pencil into the cable spool. "The county sheriff sealed the hangars an hour ago under an emergency writ of attachment from the clearinghouse banks. Fuel farm is locked. Power to the runway lights was cut at nine."

Marcus walked to the kettle sitting on the brazier, poured two fingers of lukewarm chicory into a tin mug, and swallowed it in a gulp. "And Coleman Young?"

"Runway three-three is clear of snow, but the municipal workers walked off the tower when the regional grid started rolling blackouts," she said. She reached into her canvas satchel and pulled out two heavy, lead-lined aluminum cylinders, each no larger than a thermos flask. Inside each cylinder sat the physical, air-gapped cryptographic tokens—the sortition seed keys forged in the machine shop forty-eight hours ago, containing the non-invertible physical entropy of Lake Huron’s wave noise. Without them, the one-thousand-and-one seats in Geneva could not be validated. "Perez has the twin-engine turboprop sitting on the apron behind the old municipal maintenance shed. He ran three hundred gallons of filtered vegetable oil and low-grade kerosene through the pre-heaters. It’ll run dirty, but it will turn."

"Has the weather front cleared the lake?"

"No," Elena said. She stood up, her frame slight inside an oversized sheepskin coat that had lost half its horn toggles. She slung the satchel across her chest, the heavy aluminum canisters clanking against her hip. "The blizzard is rolling in from Superior. Zero ceiling within the hour. If we don’t clear the deck before the sleet turns to sheet ice, the wings will carry too much weight to climb through the soup."

A floorboard creaked above them. Toby’s boots came down the ladder, three rungs at a time.

"Marcus," Toby gasped, his fingers red and curled into hooks. "The Valais relay. The data stream from the alpine line just hiccuped."

Marcus was at the telemetry bench in two strides. The paper tape punch was chattering out an irregular rhythm of perforations, the needle jerking erratically.

"What is it?" Elena asked, her hand instinctively closing over the leather flap of her satchel.

"Giulia's train," Marcus murmured, his eyes tracking the mechanical punch. The symbols were plain-language status flags, encoded via simple pulse-width modulation to bypass the jammed public packet networks. *TRACTION ZERO... DESCENT ON GRAVITY... VALAIS BLAST GATE CLOSED... FRICTION SHOES DEPLETED.*

The room went completely silent except for the low hiss of the kerosene heater and the rhythmic clacking of the tape.

"They're running blind down the rack into Martigny," Marcus said. His voice was entirely flat, stripped of terror by the sheer density of the crisis. "The cantonal authorities dropped the mountain avalanche barrier across the rail throat. If Giulia doesn't clear the gallery, Geneva is cut off from the southern passes. The Italian sortition delegates will be stranded in the snow at Domodossola."

Elena reached out, her fingers catching Marcus’s heavy wool sleeve. She didn't offer comfort; her grip was hard, an anchor. "We cannot steer that train from Detroit, Marcus. We have three hours before the Atlantic window collapses under the storm, and four sovereign intercept orders waiting for us if our transponder blips over Newfoundland. Do we move, or do we freeze?"

Marcus looked at the paper tape as it pooled on the grease-stained floorboards. He felt the cold weight of the brass gauge against his ribs. Every weld in the network had been laid with sweat and debt; every mile of line was held by people whose names would never appear on a bank charter.

"Pack the spare optical tubes," Marcus said to Toby. "Drain the cooling lines on the roof emitter so the jackets don't burst when the pump stops. We run Node One on auto-steer until the fuel cells starve."

He grabbed his canvas kit-bag from under the bench. Inside were two spare pairs of wool socks, a brass compass, three spools of safety wire, and Sarah's battered notebook, its oilcloth cover worn down to the grey thread at the corners.

They took the service stairs down to the loading bay.

The bay doors groaned as Marcus hauled on the manual chain hoist. The freezing wind hit them like an iron door swinging shut, bringing with it the sharp smell of ozone and the fine, needle-like sleet of the approaching gale.

At the curb, a battered four-wheel-drive flatbed idled, its exhaust pipe puffing grey rings into the drift. In the flatbed’s open bed sat four drums of unrefined oil, strapped down with fraying hemp rope.

Elena climbed into the cab without a word, tucking the lead cylinders between her boots to keep the lithium cells from dying in the draft. Marcus climbed in behind the wheel, double-clutched the worn gearbox, and slammed the shift into low. The tires spun on the black ice, caught the grit Marcus had shoveled over the ramp an hour before, and bit into the street.

They turned north toward the airfield, their headlights cutting two yellow cones through the blinding swirl of the blizzard.

Above them, on the horizon of the storm, the invisible pencil of red light from the grain elevator held fast against the clouds—a single, fragile thread of coherence thrown across three thousand miles of cold ocean, waiting for an answer.

Behind them, down the dark alley of French Road, three pairs of high-intensity halogen searchlights flicked on through the falling snow, moving in a tight wedge toward the airfield gate.
