The high-frequency scream in Kira’s right ear hadn’t dropped an octave since midnight. Forty-eight hours without sleep had turned the edges of her vision into frayed gray rope, but her fingers kept moving, coaxing a microscopic retaining ring into the housing of an optical transceiver. 

The tool crib smelled of frozen gear grease, dry cardboard, and the bitter copper tang of ancient metal shavings. Through the clerestory window overhead, dawn didn't break so much as leak—a sickly, bruise-colored light that showed sleet driving hard against the cracked wired glass. Outside, past the rail sidings, six heavy diesel engines kept up a low, rhythmic thrum, their exhaust stacking dark against the winter sky.

A floorboard creaked in the corridor. Kira didn’t look up from her bench. She knew the cadence of those footsteps: light, precise, the habit of a woman who had spent fifteen years balancing on Italian leather pumps across marble lobbies, even if she now wore cracked work boots borrowed from the supply closet.

"The nursery is warm," Elena said softly from the doorway. "Marcus got the secondary heat exchanger patched into the boiler loop. Toby is asleep on three moving blankets."

Kira tightened a brass set-screw until it clicked. "Good for Toby."

Elena stepped inside, bringing with her the scent of scorched chicory and damp wool. She held two dented tin mugs. Steam rose from them in pale, erratic ribbons, instantly snatched away by the draft that cut under the crib’s corrugated siding. Her camel-hair overcoat, once tailored within an inch of perfection on Madison Avenue, was ruined—dark salt rings circled the hem, and the elbows were smeared with yellow sulfur grease from the well pump. 

She set one of the mugs on the corner of the zinc-topped bench, inches from Kira’s scattered prisms.

"Drink it," Elena said. Her voice lacked its usual boardroom polish; it was scraped thin, hoarse from shouting over the wellhead geyser hours earlier. "There’s sugar in it. Real sugar. Mrs. Gable found two bags in the pantry."

Kira laid her tweezers down with deliberate slowness. The metal tinged against the zinc. "Don't play house, Elena. It doesn't suit your billable rate."

Elena didn't flinch, but her left hand—the one not holding the mug—slipped into her coat pocket. Kira knew why. The fine tremor in Elena's ring finger always came back when the adrenaline ebbed, a nervous tic their mother used to slap down with the back of a wooden spoon at the breakfast table.

"I’m trying to bring you breakfast, Kira."

"You’re trying to buy absolution at wholesale." Kira picked up an optical lens, squinting through the frosted glass toward the yard. "You stood by the manifold for forty minutes with a pipe wrench in your hand, and now you think you’ve joined the proletariat. You think because your boots are dirty, the ledger is square."

"I never asked for an absolution from you," Elena whispered.

"No. You never asked for anything from Delray. You just extracted it." Kira spun around on the wooden swivel stool. The sudden movement sent a violent spike of vertigo through her skull, but she locked her spine against it, staring up at her older sister. "Did you tell Marcus how you made partner? Did you tell the mothers down in Bay Two that the reason you know municipal receivership law so intimately is because you wrote the eviction templates for seven thousand homes between here and Pontiac?"

Elena’s face went chalk-pale under the grime. The tin mug shook in her grip, a brown droplet slopping over the rim and sizzling against the cast-iron housing of a drill press. 

"Someone had to sit at that table," Elena said, her voice dropping into that terrifying, icy register she used when closing a hostile deposition. "Someone had to deal with the reality that the city was broken, the paper was worthless, and the state was sending marshals regardless of what poems you sang around the kitchen stove."

"And you made sure you were the one holding the pen."

"I took the money!" Elena cried out, the composure snapping like dry pine under a boot heel. The sound bounced harsh and ugly off the steel storage cabinets. "I took their retention bonus! Every filthy, corporate dime of it! Do you think I bought a loft in Tribeca with it? Do you think I bought diamonds?"

"I don't care what you bought."

"Look at me, Kira!" Elena lunged forward, slamming the second mug onto the zinc so hard chicory splashed across the schematics. Her eyes were bloodshot, rimmed with deep purple shadows that made her look ten years older than thirty-six. "Look at the damn dates! November. Three years ago. St. John’s Hospital, fifth floor palliative care. You were off in the Pacific Northwest at some communal solar collective that couldn't even keep its own batteries dry. Dad was drowning in his own lungs, coughing up black bile every twenty minutes, and the billing department was threatening to roll his bed out to the curb because the union health trust had been seized by the bankruptcy court!"

Kira felt the breath leave her ribs in a sudden, sickening vacuum. "The mortgage was paid," she said, her voice dropping to a harsh rasp. "The deed was cleared. He died in his own bedroom."

"Because I liquidated every share I owned!" Elena spat, leaning over the bench, her knuckles white against the metal edge. "I signed a three-year non-compete, I took the restructuring fee for the Wayne County portfolio, and I wired six figures directly to the predatory lien-holder who had a sheriff’s lock on his front door! I bought him three months of morphine and a clean bed to die in! And you didn't even show up until the funeral was over, smelling of river water and self-righteousness, accusing me of selling out his legacy!"

"You paid for his dying bed with the evictions of the men he worked the line with!" Kira’s voice cracked, rising to a fury that tore at her dry throat. She stood up, her chest knocking against the bench, bringing her face inches from Elena’s. "You laundered their misery to buy him off! He would have choked on that morphine if he’d known whose houses paid for the drip!"

"He knew," Elena breathed.

The silence that followed was heavier than the iron overhead. 

Outside, a sleet gust rattled the wired glass like buckshot. The distant diesels gave a collective, shuddering growl as their transmissions engaged, shifting thirty feet closer to the perimeter fence.

Kira felt her stomach drop away. "He didn't."

"He looked at the cashier’s check," Elena said, her tears finally breaking, cutting pale tracks through the soot on her cheeks. "He looked right at it. He couldn't speak through the oxygen mask, but he took my hand—this hand, Kira, the one that signed the foreclosure filings—and he squeezed it until his knuckles turned yellow. He knew what it cost. He knew what I traded away to keep the scavengers off his back. He didn't hate me for it. He hated that the world was built so that one of his daughters had to become a monster just to keep the other one pure."

Kira’s hands were trembling now, a violent, involuntary shudder that started in her wrists and climbed into her shoulders. She wanted to strike the table. She wanted to smash the transceiver, to shatter the delicate prisms she had spent two hours aligning. 

Instead, Elena reached into her coat, pulled out the matte-black aluminum cipher drive, and slammed it down between them. The heavy alloy bounced once on the grease-stained paper and settled against Kira’s pliers.

"If I’m the monster," Elena whispered, her chest heaving against her coat, "if I’m the blood-soaked corporate parasite who sold our father’s soul to Meridian Crest... then tell me, Kira. Why did you put the seed code in my lockbox? Why did you bring the network to *me*?"

The question hung between them, bare and jagged.

Kira looked down at the drive. She remembered the freezing rain in Zurich three months ago, the panic of the terminal breach, the knowledge that if she was intercepted at customs, everything they had built would vanish into a federal evidence vault. She had held the addresses of nine hundred trusted allies across the continent. 

And she had routed the master shard to a private safety deposit box in Detroit registered under an attorney’s bar number.

"Because," Kira choked out, her throat burning, her fingernails digging into her palms until they threatened to break the skin, "because when the world burns, you’re the only person I know who knows how to survive the fire."

Elena let out a short, ragged gasp that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite a sob. She reached out, her fingers hovering an inch above Kira’s sleeve, before pulling back, terrified of breaking whatever fragile, agonizing truce had just cracked the air.

"Then start acting like we’re in the fire," a deep voice cut from the darkness of the threshold.

Maya stood in the doorway, wrapped in an oversized shearling coat, her hair tied back with a strip of frayed electrical tape. In her left hand, she held a handheld spectrum receiver; its green display was scrolling frantic lines of zeroed signal bars.

"Both of you," Maya said, her voice completely devoid of patience, hard as structural rebar. "Pack your ghosts away. The three hundred people downstairs didn't sleep in the cold so they could burn on the altar of the Vance family funeral."

Kira wiped her face roughly with the back of her grease-blackened wrist. "What is it?"

"The cellular towers along the Conrail right-of-way just dropped off the air," Maya said, stepping into the crib and holding up the monitor. The screen showed flat, dead noise across every civilian band. "Total carrier jamming. No commercial signal from here to the river. Briggs isn't trying to starve the water anymore."

Through the frosted skylights, the low drone of the diesel carriers outside suddenly deepened, accompanied by the sharp, metallic screech of hydraulic outriggers biting into frozen ballast.

Elena looked toward the window, her corporate posture returning in an instant—the chin lifting, the tremor vanishing into a locked fist. "They’re isolating the perimeter before they serve the physical writ. Sheriff Miller couldn't stop them from dropping the cell repeaters."

Maya turned toward the main bay where the sound of three hundred voices hummed below the steady roar of the artesian well. "They're rolling the heavy transport skids across the tracks right now. We have about four minutes before they hit the south gate."
