The floor of Bay 3 did not just vibrate; it hummed inside Marcus’s shinbones. It was the distinct, low-frequency churn of heavy planetary drives biting into frozen ballast, a sound he had known thirty years ago when the scrap haulers tore through the south flats.

He knelt behind the second line of cribbing, an oil-blackened adjustable wrench hooked through his tool loop and a pair of wool-lined pigskin gloves pulled taut over knuckles swollen from damp. His right knee was a hot node of ache where he had taken a bad spill off a catwalk during the storm’s first push, but he ignored it. When the cold got down into the marrow, you stopped negotiating with pain. You simply worked around it, the way an old lathe worked around a hairline fracture in the casting.

Above him, the digital chronometer on the high beam blinked from red to pale amber.

06:00:00.

The cooling break was dead.

Across the bay, Kira let out a long, ragged exhalation that plumed white in the freezing draft. She didn’t look at the monitor banks or the radio desk. She stepped past the junction box and stopped three feet from Maya. For twenty-four hours they had worked in absolute silence, passing wrenches and conduit by hand without exchanging a syllable, their mutual hatred suspended only by the strictures of the covenant.

Kira reached out. Her hand was smeared with zinc paste from the grounding straps. She took the braided steel pull-line Maya was anchoring against the floor stanchion and took half the tension onto her own shoulder.

"The south shear won't hold if the blade hits at an angle," Kira said. Her voice was cracked, raw from cold air, but flat and steady.

Maya didn't flinch. She adjusted her grip, their knuckles brushing against the rough hemp jacket of the cable. "Then we don't let it hit at an angle. Anchor the deadman to the crane rail."

Two sentences. That was all it took. The silence broke, not with an apology or a handshake, but with the cold, unyielding calculus of two craftswomen refusing to let an arch collapse over their heads.

A savage jolt rattled the corrugated steel of the rail spur gate.

Through the narrow slit of the loading dock wicket, Marcus saw the first machine. It wasn’t a city truck or a police cruiser. It was a massive, high-track earthmover painted the sickly yellow of stripped quarry stone, fitted with a reinforced V-blade designed to rip tree stumps from frozen clay. Sixty yards behind it, idling in the gray pre-dawn mist, a second scraper waited with its floodlights blazing, blinding the plant’s perimeter cameras with raw white tungsten.

Behind the blades marched the staging line—two dozen contractors in dark, unmarked thermal parkas, their faces hidden behind ballistic shields and cold-weather respirators, moving with the disciplined, heavy-footed cadence of men paid by the hour to break things.

The lead dozer lunged forward. Its tracks threw slabs of frozen slag against the brick pilings. The steel blade struck the Monolith’s outer chain-link cordon, folding it like dry paper, and drove hard toward the inner gate.

"Now!" Kira shouted.

Maya threw the manual dog-clutch on the hoist drum.

There was no explosion. No gunfire. Only the terrifying, singing shriek of four-inch braided steel cable snapping out of the buried conduit troughs beneath the rail bed.

The snare was pure mechanical spite. Kira hadn’t tried to stop forty tons of steel with a blunt wall; she had laid a spider’s web of tensioned cable across the rail ties, buried under two inches of loose limestone gravel. As the dozer’s starboard track churned through the ballast, the cable caught the drive sprocket, pulled taut against the crane-rail anchor inside the shop, and wrapped itself three times around the planetary hub.

The machine shrieked. The massive diesel engine stalled in a violent cloud of black exhaust as the cable bound the drive teeth solid against the track frame. The machine listed ten degrees to the right, shuddered, and died.

The operator hit the reverse throttle in panic. The hydraulic relief valves screamed, dumping fluid in a steaming pink froth across the ice. The track was fouled beyond field repair. The dozer was an immovable ninety-thousand-pound wedge blocking the entire rail cut, sealing the gate against the second scraper behind it.

A ragged cheer started near the tool crib, but Marcus didn't join in.

He was watching the river.

From his vantage point near the intake well, the clerestory glass looked out over the Old Channel of the Rouge. Through the swirling sleet, a low-slung, flat-bottomed industrial tender was nosing in close to the Monolith’s western seawall. It had no running lights, no registry numbers visible through the grime. On its deck, two men in chemical-handling gear were wrestling with an open-ended discharge hose four inches thick.

A dark, curdled plume erupted from the tender’s stern.

It was not fuel oil. It hit the gray, slush-choked water and bloomed instantly into an oily, neon-purple slick that smelled of burnt plastic and concentrated solvent. The river’s sluggish current caught the mass, dragging it straight toward the plant’s raw-water intake cut.

Marcus felt the hair on his arms rise under his thermal sleeve.

"The intake!" he yelled, his voice cutting across the bay. "Shut the intake sluice!"

Sully scrambled to the utility board, his boots sliding on the wet slab. He hammered the pneumatic trip lever for the primary river gate.

The copper air line hissed, spat a cup of rusty condensate, and died. The gauge needle sat pinned at zero. The freeze had caught the supply line in the outer yard; the pneumatic piston was frozen solid in its bore.

"It's dead!" Sully yelled back, his face gray. "The actuator line ruptured under the frost!"

Marcus didn't wait for anyone else to calculate the arithmetic.

Eighty people were sleeping, eating, and breathing inside this building. Every drop of potable water they had ran through the low-pressure sand cisterns below the floor. If that chemical sludge bypassed the trash racks and reached the suction manifold, the filter beds would be saturated with toxic aromatic solvents within four minutes. You couldn’t wash that out. You couldn’t boil it out. It would poison the iron piping down to the copper fittings, turning the node into a dry desert surrounded by frozen swamp.

Marcus stripped off his heavy coat and boots in four violent motions, down to his woolen undershirt and wool socks.

"Marcus, what are you doing?" Maya shouted from the rail hoist.

"The manual shear valve," he barked, grabbing an eight-pound sledge and a four-foot length of steel pipe from the salvage rack. "Under Bay 3. In the intake sump."

"The water’s thirty-two degrees!" she yelled, running toward him. "The pit is flooded three feet over the deck!"

"Then I won't stay long," he said.

He kicked the iron diamond-plate hatch open with his heel. A blast of dead, freezing air hit him, carrying the reek of anaerobic river silt and rusted cast iron. Down below, ten feet down an iron rung ladder, black water lapped against the foundation walls with a hollow, rhythmic slosh.

Marcus didn't give his heart time to argue. He swung onto the rungs and dropped.

The cold hit him like an iron bar across the chest.

The water was up to his ribs, thick with floating shards of skim ice that sliced at his bare forearms. His breath locked behind his teeth in an involuntary, wheezing gasp. His vision tunneled instantly; the single work light hanging from the hatch rim shrank to a hazy yellow dot.

Every joint in his bad knee flared with agonizing, blinding white fire, then went instantly numb.

*Find the stem,* he told himself. *Don't look at the dark. Find the stem.*

He waded through the surge, dragging the steel cheater pipe through the black, freezing slurry. Debris bumped against his hips—rotted survey stakes, old drift-planks, grease rags. He knew the layout from forty years of industrial memory: third pillar from the wet wall, behind the secondary screen.

His hands plunged beneath the surface, raking over the slime-slick casting of the main penstock. His fingers had no feeling left; they were just wooden pegs he rammed against the cold metal until his knuckles struck the square bronze valve stem.

The wheel had broken off during the 1984 flood. To close it, you had to drop a socket wrench onto the naked squared stem and turn it forty-two full revolutions against seventy pounds of river head.

Marcus forced the open end of his cheater pipe over the square lug. He leaned his entire chest against the cold pipe and shoved.

Nothing. The bronze was frozen in its packing gland, welded by twenty years of lime and river silt.

From the intake duct six feet away, he heard the first gurgle of the arriving tide—the sickening, sweet chemical stench of the purple solvent pushing through the intake screens, riding the river’s winter crest.

"Come on, you bastard," Marcus growled, his teeth chattering so hard he tasted blood from his lip. "Move."

He lifted the eight-pound sledge with one arm, his shoulder screaming in protest, and smashed it against the base of the cheater pipe.

The ringing crack echoed through the subterranean chamber like a gunshot.

The valve grooved. A fraction of an inch.

Marcus threw his dead weight against the pipe again. This time it gave—a quarter turn. Then half. The threads groaned, shearing through decades of mineral scale.

One. Two. Three.

His chest was convulsing now, cold shock dragging his lungs into rapid, useless spasms. He couldn’t feel his feet; he couldn't feel whether the floor was concrete or silt. He only knew the circular sweep of his arms, pushing the pipe through the dark water, chest heaving against the resistance of the river.

Twenty. Twenty-one.

A hand grabbed his collar from above. Toby was hanging upside down from the access ladder, his face pale with horror, reaching down into the pit. "Marcus! Take my hand! Come up!"

"Get back!" Marcus croaked, his throat tight with frost. "Count the turns! Count!"

Thirty-five. Thirty-six.

The flow through the screen began to choke down from a roar to a thin, sucking hiss. On thirty-nine, the cheater pipe jammed hard against the packing stop.

Marcus gave it one final, violent heave with everything left in his spine. The bronze gate seated into its iron shoe with a muffled, heavy thud.

The hiss stopped. The water around his waist went dead still.

His fingers slipped from the pipe. The sledgehammer slipped from his grip, vanishing into the muck below with a dull splash.

"Pull!" Toby screamed.

Hands caught him—Toby’s, then Maya’s strong, grease-caked grip beneath his armpits. They hauled him out of the black hole like a drowned engine block, his skin mottled blue-gray, his wool clothes pouring freezing sludge across the clean floor of the bay.

They rolled him onto a dry canvas tarp. Kira was already there, dropping a coarse wool welding blanket over his shaking frame, while Toby shoved a metal mug of steaming tea against his lips.

Marcus coughed, choking on the heat of the chicory, his chest racking with violent, uncontrollable shudders. But his eyes were clear. He looked toward the utility board.

The intake sensor light flickered, then shifted from amber to a dead, dry gray. The cut was sealed. The cisterns were clean.

"Marcus," Elena’s voice was low, coming from the radio console ten yards away. She held the receiver against her shoulder, her knuckles white.

Marcus tried to sit up, shivering so hard his teeth clicked against the tin rim of the mug. "Did... did the water hold?"

"The water held," Elena said. She looked down at the desk. A small, secondary transceiver—the battery-powered commercial handset Hannah Campbell had used forty-eight hours ago—was blinking with a single uncurated carrier light. No encryption handshake. No public routing.

Elena picked up the handset, her eyes meeting Marcus’s across the cold floor.

"Vance," she said into the horn.

A woman's voice came through the small speaker, sharp and brittle as river ice, carrying the hollow acoustic resonance of a government sedan parked on an access ramp.

"This is Hannah Campbell," the voice said, quiet, unhurried, and deadly clear. "Tell your machinist to stay warm. The federal marshals just revoked IronClad’s transit authority on the rail spur. The state police are turning their haulers around at the bridge."

Hannah paused, the faint crackle of sleet ticking against her car windshield audible on the line.

"You have six hours before the appellate court convenes in Chicago," she said. "Don't waste them."
