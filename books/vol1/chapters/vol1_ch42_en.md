The roar of celebration from the bridge reached the stamping bay as a muffled, subterranean vibration, shaking dust from the iron trusses eighty feet above the concrete floor. Below the gantry where Kira Vance crouched, two hundred exhausted plant workers and their families in Bay Two were embracing, weeping, and hammering tin cups against the sheet-metal tables. Word had traveled through the mesh radios like fire through dry grass: the federal warrant was quashed, the marshals were standing down, and the bridge approach was clear.

Kira did not join the cheering.

Her fingertips, numbed by the sub-zero draft whistling through the cracked clerestory glass, moved across the battered field tablet resting on her knees. Every seven seconds, she tapped her right thumb against her index fingernail—four sharp clicks, an unconscious domestic rhythm picked up during twelve-hour graveyard shifts in her twenties, keeping her pulse tethered against the hypnotic drift of exhaustion. Her eyes burned from thirty-six hours without sleep, the skin around her cheekbones taut and chapped from the biting wind whipping off the Detroit River.

While the sanctuary celebrated a political victory, the plant’s perimeter telemetry told a starkly different story.

The municipal intertie feed from the eastern substation had flatlined three minutes ago. Meridian Crest had abandoned the legal battle in court and pulled the plug at the master transfer switches miles away. The Monolith was now running entirely on its isolated microgrid, drawing upon the reserve chemical banks stored in the basement vaults. Every watt was counted; every line was strained.

Then, a red perimeter marker flickered along the southwestern boundary.

Kira zoomed in on the thermal sweep. The sector covered the defunct rail siding where rusted boxcars had sat half-buried in sumac and frozen switchgrass since the nineteen-eighties. It was a blind spot for the federal monitors, screened from the public plaza by the mountain of scrap iron.

Five distinct infrared signatures were moving through the perimeter fence. They weren’t marshals, and they weren’t state troopers; there were no flashing light bars, no marked cruisers, and no loudhailers demanding entry.

They moved with low, hurried urgency, carrying heavy canvas equipment packs. One of the figures used an oxy-acetylene torch to sever the padlocked rail gate, the white-hot plume glowing like a matchstick on Kira's thermal display.

Kira tapped her radio. "Marcus. Southern spur. We have five intruders cutting through the rail gate."

A crackle of static broke through her earpiece, followed by Marcus’s labored breathing. He was down in the basement transformer vault, nursing the auxiliary lines. "Federal?"

"No," Kira whispered, her throat dry as sand. "Too fast. No badges. They’re carrying heavy ordnance packs."

She zoomed the gantry’s optical camera onto the lead intruder as he stepped under the sodium floodlights of the scrap yard.

It was Briggs.

His tactical jacket was unzipped against the bitter night, his ballistic vest stripped of its corporate security insignia. His jaw was set in a rigid, hollow snarl, his left shoulder dropping slightly with the old injury that flared whenever the mercury plummeted. The corporate commander who had sneered through a megaphone twelve hours earlier had vanished; in his place was a ruined mercenary whose bond had been forfeited, whose corporate masters had cut his communications channels, and who faced a federal indictment the moment he set foot outside the industrial corridor.

He had not come to arrest anyone. He had come to ensure there was nothing left to save.

Under his arm, Briggs carried an olive-drab steel canister with a flared magnesium igniter head—an industrial breaching thermite charge. A single canister placed atop the primary stamping press’s forged bedplate would burn at four thousand degrees, melting three hundred tons of specialized tooling into an unusable puddle of slag in less than ninety seconds.

"Marcus," Kira said, her voice dropping an octave, razor-sharp and cold. "It’s Briggs. He has thermite. He’s heading for the primary press."

"The sanctuary—"

"I’m dropping the blast curtains," she cut him off. "Get the families back into the boiler vault. Now."

Kira reached to the manual junction box bolted to the gantry railing. Her hand shook for a fraction of a second, not from fear, but from the brutal cold stiffening her joints. She slammed the yellow pneumatic lever downward.

Across the cavernous expanse of the stamping bay, high-pressure air screamed through the distribution lines. The massive, four-inch-thick steel acoustic fire doors—installed decades ago to isolate the deafening noise of the heavy mechanical drop hammers—groaned in their grease-packed tracks. With a deafening, thunderous boom that shook the concrete floor, the counterweighted steel slabs dropped into their floor channels, hermetically sealing Bay Two and its celebrating crowd behind two hundred tons of solid iron.

The cheering vanished instantly, severed behind the steel wall. In the stamping bay, there was only the hollow whistle of the winter gale and the rhythmic thrum of the autonomous transformer bank humming in the center of the room.

The sheet-metal personnel door beneath the gantry rattled violently.

A boot kicked it open.

Briggs lunged through the doorway, backed by four private security contractors in dark thermal gear. None of them carried rifles—the federal presence outside had stripped them of their long guns at the perimeter checkpoints—but they carried pry bars, sledgehammers, and the heavy canvas satchels of military incendiary compound.

"Spread out!" Briggs barked, his voice echoing off the corrugated roof like a cracked bell. "Two charges on the main ram! Two on the hydraulic fluid reservoir! Burn the whole damn cradle!"

The men moved with brutal, practiced speed. Two of them sprinted toward the hydraulic fluid manifold, where three thousand gallons of pressurized oil sat behind reinforced lines. If the thermite touched that reservoir, the resulting vapor explosion would blow the clerestory windows into the river and collapse the bay's primary roof girders.

Kira stood motionless on the narrow gantry walkway, forty feet directly above them. The air down below smelled of diesel exhaust and damp concrete. She couldn't shoot them—the Covenant was an unyielding wire running through every fiber of the network's design. Non-lethal containment was not a suggestion; it was hardwired into the machine's very logic.

She reached into her coverall pocket and pulled out the mechanical override key for the overhead crane system.

The crane was a relic: an eighty-ton bridge hoist used to lift die sets into the jaws of the mechanical press. It sat parked directly over the center aisle, its heavy cast-iron hook dangling fifty feet of solid steel chain above the floor.

Briggs reached the base of the primary press. He dumped his satchel on the lower anvil, his face bathed in sweat despite the freezing draft. His fingers worked the latch of the thermite canister, exposing the pull-ring igniter.

"You built a kingdom out of garbage," Briggs growled into the empty air, his voice ragged with exhaustion and venom. "Let's see you run it on ashes."

Kira stepped to the crane's local control pendant, suspended from an articulated steel arm beside the gantry rail. She bypassed the remote software lock with the brass physical key, feeling the mechanical contacts snap shut with a heavy clunk.

She didn't run a diagnostic. There was no time.

She hammered the high-pressure washdown override.

High above the bay floor, the automated fire suppression nozzles—fed by the plant’s auxiliary graywater cisterns—cracked open. A dense, blinding curtain of freezing river water and atomized mist exploded from the ceiling, saturating the air in a roaring downpour.

The four contractors near the reservoir shouted in shock, their flashlights instantly blinded by the dense, swirling fog. The concrete floor transformed into a slick, treacherous sheet of wet slurry. Two of the men lost their footing, crashing onto the steel deckplates with dull, echoing thuds, their pry bars clattering into the drainage sumps.

"Ignore it!" Briggs roared through the torrential spray, wiping water from his eyes with his sleeve. "It’s just water! Light the fuses!"

He yanked the pull-ring on the lead canister.

A blinding, violet-white flare erupted at the foot of the press. The magnesium core hissed with terrifying fury, sending showers of incandescent sparks twenty feet into the air. The violent thermal shock hit the wet concrete beneath the anvil, causing the surface layer to pop and fracture with sharp, gunshot-like snaps as water trapped in the porous stone instantly flashed to steam.

Kira’s eyes narrowed through the rising cloud of vapor.

Directly above Briggs hung the crane’s deadweight test block—a ten-ton slab of solid cast iron chained to the trolley hoist for maintenance calibration.

She gripped the mechanical brake release lever with both hands.

"Briggs!" she shouted through the roaring mist.

The contractor looked up, his face illuminated in the demonic, flickering white glare of the burning thermite. His eyes locked onto hers, forty feet above him in the gloom.

Kira shoved the release lever all the way forward.

The heavy steel cable unspooled with an ear-splitting shriek. The ten-ton iron block plunged out of the shadows, falling like an anvil through the blinding white spray.
