The magnetic crawler three fathoms down in the Detroit River had sharp carbide teeth, but it was chewing on a ghost.

Sully didn’t curse. He didn’t pound the console. His thumb, bound tight with black electrical tape where a split seam had festered from diesel wash, flipped three mechanical toggle switches on the intake panel. 

"High-pressure wash," Kira said from his shoulder, her breath smelling of bitter chicory and peppermint oil. Her hands didn't shake. She had spent the last twenty hours tracing circuit grounds through freezing mud, and her fingernails were rimmed with gray river clay. "Cycle the sluice."

"Cycling," Sully grunted.

Under the river ice, sixty pounds of trapped ballast water and coarse gravel back-flushed through the intake manifold with a subterranean thud that resonated through the concrete flooring of Bay One. The sensor reading on the auxiliary bus shuddered. The mechanical friction on the sub-fluvial line didn't cease, but the telemetry died—not because the link had failed, but because it no longer mattered.

"They're thirty seconds late," Kira whispered, pointing at the secondary monitor.

The green trace hadn't blinked out. The moment the river sheath took physical stress, the routing logic had already dumped the localized transit load, bypassing the border trench entirely. The signal had jumped south along the freight-rail fiber easement, vaulted into the commercial transatlantic trunks off the Nova Scotia shelf, and raced along nine thousand miles of abyssal glass into the European spine. 

The corporate tugboat sitting out in the gray chop between Detroit and Windsor had severed a length of dead galvanized steel. 

Marcus remained at the mezzanine rail, his heavy coat dusted with powdered gypsum from the ceiling vents. He didn't look at the screens. He was watching the ice floes in the channel slowly change color, shifting from slate to pale amber as the morning sun fought through the industrial haze over the Canadian shore. For forty years, the only thing that had ever crossed that border was cargo manifested for profit or men running from debt. Now, something else was moving.

On the master terminal, the amber line cleared. 

A single line of plain type materialized, simultaneously echoed on four screens across three continents:

`FOUR NODES SYNCHRONIZED.`
`DETROIT. GENEVA. SHENZHEN. LOS ANDES.`
`ENERGY LEDGER CLOSED. MATERIAL FLOW BALANCED.`
`THE COMMONWEALTH HOLDS.`

Elena Vance stepped out from behind the intake accumulator. She walked with the stiff, rolling gait of someone whose hip joints had seized after seven days of sleeping upright in an office chair. She stopped beside Marcus, resting her palms on the cold, pitted iron of the railing. Her fingers, perpetually yellowed around the knuckles from old nicotine stains, curled around the metal.

"It's done?" she asked, her voice dry as dead corn husks.

"No," Marcus said. His voice was quiet, a low rumble that felt older than the brick walls around them. "The seven days are done. The building starts."

Behind them, the terminal chimed. It wasn't an alarm. It was the dry, flat bell of an old telegraph relay tripped by a completed circuit. 

Maya Lin-Mercado stood before the glass screen, a woolen scarf wound thrice around her neck, her breath misting faintly in the drafty air of the mezzanine. She read the directive aloud, her voice carrying across the silent, cathedral expanse of Bay One where thirty night-shift machinists and neighborhood spotters stood motionless among the dormant lathes:

"‘The ledger cannot belong to an executive, a committee, or a market. No person shall purchase the power to decide who freezes and who eats. To preserve the commons against capture, sovereignty must dissolve into the body of the people.’"

Maya looked back over her shoulder, her dark eyes hollowed by exhaustion, reflecting the amber phosphor of the glass. "It’s calling the assembly. Three citizens per continental node. Selected by lot to establish the founding delegation in Geneva."

Sully wiped his face with a rag that left a smear of graphite across his cheek. "Three? Out of the whole basin?"

"Three out of three million," Kira said, stepping down from the control dais. Her boots clicked against the steel tread of the stairs. "Sortition doesn't care about your resume, Sully. That’s the entire point."

Down on the floor of Bay One, Toby and two line workers rolled an iron cart out from under the gantry hoist. Resting on the cart was a six-sided drum, four feet long, welded from scrapped sheet brass that had once served as liner plates for a turbine condenser. Marcus had spent two hours the previous night cleaning its axles with kerosene and emery cloth until the cylinder spun on its bearings with no more sound than a heavy sigh.

The drum was not an artifact of sentiment. It was an instrument of cold, mathematical probability. Inside it were thousands of stamped brass slugs, each punched with a unique alphanumeric hash tied to a verified household meter connected to the open grid over the past one hundred and sixty-eight hours. No names were stamped into the metal. No titles, no party affiliations, no credit scores.

The morning light broke fully through the clerestory windows, cutting shafts through the cold blue smoke of the shop stoves. 

Elena descended the stairs, her hand sliding along the greasy pipe rail. She hadn't seen her apartment on Trumbull since the first substation had been seized. Her radiator was probably cracked; her mail slot was undoubtedly stuffed with repossession orders and notices from the federal district court. But as she reached the bottom of the stairs, she saw forty neighbors standing in an uneven circle around the drum. 

There was Mrs. Hendershot from the bakery on Green Street, her apron dusted with rye flour, her knuckles swollen from arthritis. There was Arlo, who had lost three toes to frostbite twenty years ago while laying gas mains for the city and now lived on canned soup and community wood piles. There was a young woman with grease on her jaw whose name Elena didn't even know, holding a sleeping infant wrapped in a wool blanket against her chest.

They had not come to celebrate. Celebrations belonged to people who didn't have to worry about the price of propane by nightfall. They had come because the system had finally asked them to bear the weight of their own lives.

Marcus reached the bottom of the steps and stood beside the drum. He looked at the circle of faces. His father had taught him that an honest weld always showed its ripples—that anyone who ground a joint flat before the inspector arrived was trying to hide slag. The commons they had built over seven days was scarred, ugly, and held together by field splices and shared risk. But it was sound.

"Geneva has convened an emergency session," Maya called down from the mezzanine, a field telephone pressed against her ear. "The ambassadors are meeting behind closed doors. The shipping lines are holding their tankers in port. They think this is a strike. They think we’re holding out for terms."

"Let them think it," Marcus said, his voice carrying clearly across the quiet concrete. He laid his palm against the cold brass of the cylinder. "We aren't negotiating an hourly wage. We're showing them how the world stays warm."

He looked at Sully, who was leaning over the mezzanine rail, then down at Kira. 

"Spin it," Marcus said.

Kira stepped forward. She didn't hesitate. She took the cast-iron crank handle fitted to the axle of the brass drum and threw her weight into it.

The heavy cylinder turned.

Inside, thousands of brass slugs tumbled over the internal baffles with a sound like a riverbed shifting during spring thaw—a dull, rhythmic roar of metal against metal that echoed up into the corrugated roof trusses and shook the dust from the steel beams. 

Round and round it went, the inertia of four hundred pounds of balanced brass carrying it through forty revolutions, churning the identity of three million souls into an unyielding, unpredictable equality.

The drum slowed. The tumbling softened from a roar to a rattle, then to a trickle of sliding brass, until it settled with a single, clear metallic strike.

A small sliding gate at the bottom of the drum dropped open.

Three brass tokens slipped through the chute and clattered into the waiting pine box below.

The shop fell dead quiet. Outside, the Detroit River moved gray and inexorable under the cold morning sky, carrying chunks of lake ice toward the sea, completely indifferent to treaties, borders, or the men who claimed to own the water.

Marcus knelt. His knees popped in the silence. He didn't reach into the box immediately; he simply looked at the three stamped circles of yellow metal resting on the unfinished wood. 

The mandate was open. Across the Atlantic, in the marble halls beside Lake Geneva, the old world was drawing its cordons, preparing its police, its injunctions, and its embargoes. But the wire was already singing under the ocean. The ledger was alive, and the three people who would walk into that storm to speak for the cold cities of the north were already chosen.

Marcus reached his calloused hand into the dark of the box.
