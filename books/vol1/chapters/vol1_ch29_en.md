The iron throw-bar bucked under Maya’s grip, screeching as the sheared cotter pin sparked against the flange. Marcus hit her shoulder with the flat of his chest, not striking, but wedging his bulk between her fingers and the release lever before the latch could drop home.

"Get off it!" Maya yelled, her voice raw, white plumes of breath driving into his collar. "Let it go, Marcus!"

"The second that leaf swings, we lose the perimeter!" Marcus slammed his forearm across the locking dog, his cracked knuckles throbbing inside his torn leather glove. "You hear those diesels? They aren’t just herding neighbors, Mercado! They've got snatch teams on the flanks!"

"Let go of the bar!"

Before Marcus could twist the secondary dog into place, a gloved hand wedged itself directly into the pinch point between the lever and the stop-block.

"Sortition!" Elena’s scream was a sharp, splitting crack in the freezing air.

Marcus froze. His boots slipped two inches in the chemical slush, but his weight remained braced on the lever. If he dropped his mass, Elena’s fingers would be pulverized against the cast steel.

Elena didn’t flinch. Her face was gray with cold, dark crescents bruised under her eyes, but her gaze was dead level. "Article Eight, Section Three. Emergency impasse on perimeter defense. Neither of you touches that release. Sully! Sound the assembly horn!"

"Elena, there's no time—" Maya started, her chest heaving against Marcus’s arm.

"The Covenant holds or we are already an armed gang," Elena cut in, her voice dropping into a razor-edged cadence that brooked no debate. "Three arbiters. Drawn now. Step back from the dog, Marcus. Step back, Maya."

Marcus stared at the bruised skin of Elena’s knuckles resting against the shearing edge. The heavy carbon-arc searchlight from West Jefferson swept through the perimeter fence, turning their mingled breath into a blinded blur of magnesium-white. Outside, thirty feet away, a woman screamed as the low bumper of a tactical BearCat crushed an abandoned shopping cart against the curb.

Marcus eased his weight back. The counterweight rattled, but the dogs held.

"Three minutes," Marcus ground out, spitting a mouthful of road grit onto the slush. "Then those trucks push through the chain link."

***

They didn't go to the control room. There was no time to climb the gantry.

They backed into Muster Bay Four, where the air was five degrees warmer than the gale off the river, smelling of wet slag, scorched sulfur, and the sour wool of fifty shivering residents who had abandoned their bunks at the sound of the fence rattling. High overhead, the clerestory glass shuddered rhythmically to the low-frequency rumble of armored vehicles idling on the asphalt outside.

Sully slammed a round brass drum onto the top of an upturned grease barrel. It was an old grain-tester, its inspection hopper scratched raw from decades of seed sorting before Marcus had salvaged it from a feed mill in Riga.

Inside were ninety-two lead slugs, each stamped with an intake number corresponding to the physical ledger bolted to the node desk.

"No speeches," Elena said, her voice echoing off the corrugated iron walls. She spun the drum’s brass crank three times. The metallic tumbling sounded like river stones grinding in a sluice. "Three names. Simple majority binds the station. If they vote to open, Gate One opens. If they vote to hold, the perimeter stays sealed until dawn."

She reached into the trap door.

"Number forty-four," Elena read.

From the edge of the circle, Toby stepped forward. He wasn't the clumsy boy who had dropped a socket wrench into an open crankcase three days ago; he was pale, shivering in an oversized thermal coverall, his right thumb rhythmically tracing the deep grease-burn scar across his left wrist—a habit Marcus recognized from long graveyard shifts when the lad was trying to stay awake without coffee.

"Number twelve," Elena pulled the second slug.

Martha Gable pushed her way past two pipefitters. Her steel-rimmed spectacles were fogged at the edges, held together at the bridge by a strip of blue electrical tape. She was seventy-one, her knuckles swollen thick as hickory nuts from forty years of guiding heavy upholstery canvas through commercial sewing machines. She smelled faintly of menthol vapor-rub and dry cornmeal.

"Number seventy-eight," Elena called the third.

Lamar Washington lowered his chipped enamel mug. Lamar was a tall, stooped orderly from the regional trauma wing three miles north, a man who had spent fifteen years rolling gurneys through blood-slicked linoleum while municipal budgets collapsed around him. His face was entirely devoid of panic; it held only the dull, heavy endurance of someone who had seen every conceivable variety of human wreckage.

The three sat on an oak bench dragged from the locker corridor.

Marcus stepped into the center of the bay. He didn't raise his voice. He kept his hands open, though the cold made his split knuckles ache straight into the wrist bone.

"You look out that window, you see our people," Marcus said, gesturing toward the searchlight beams cutting across the ceiling. "I see them too. But I also see two unmarked Suburbans idling with their blackout lights on behind the second tactical rig. IronClad isn't stupid. They know we have non-lethal interlocks. The easiest way through an electrified blast gate is behind a mother holding an infant. You trip that release, and within ninety seconds, three men with thermite sticks are inside our secondary substation. If the bus bars blow, the blowers die. The clinic heaters die. The water pumps freeze. We don't save two hundred people by freezing eight hundred inside."

"The battery bank is already bleeding out," Sully barked from the wall, clutching his clipboard like a shield. "If we bring two hundred bodies into the heated bays, the radiant draw will pull us down to critical reserve in under an hour. The system can’t carry the thermal mass!"

Maya stepped up beside the grease barrel. Her hair was matted with frozen rain, sticking to her cheek. She didn't look at Marcus; she looked straight into Lamar’s bloodshot eyes.

"Four years ago, my brother Mateo sat on the curb outside Detroit Receiving," Maya said, her voice low, vibrating with a terrible, restrained clarity. "He had a secondary pneumothorax. The triage nurse told him the intake beds were reserved for catastrophic trauma under municipal insurance guidelines. They left him on the curb because admitting an uninsured case threatened the ward's operating margin. He drowned in his own chest while three administrators reviewed the protocol."

She pointed a rigid, trembling finger toward the rumbling iron of Gate One.

"Those people aren't operational variables. That baby isn't a thermal draw. If this node exists to protect the machines instead of the flesh, then let the federals burn it to the ground tonight, because it’s already dead."

Silence fell over Muster Bay Four, broken only by the thrum of the river wind and the muffled bullhorns from the street demanding immediate dispersal under maritime quarantine laws.

Elena looked at the bench. "Lamar Washington. Your finding."

Lamar looked down at his chipped mug, turning it slowly in his rough, graying hands. "In the ward, when the waiting room backs up into the ambulance bay, you don't unlock the double doors. You can't. If you let thirty screaming relatives into the surgical bays, somebody contaminates a tray, somebody knocks over an oxygen cart, and the patient on the table dies along with the ones in the hall." He looked up, his gaze meeting Maya’s without hostility. "We don't have the intake staff. We don't have the beds. We open that gate blind, we lose the clinic. I vote no. Keep the iron down."

One to zero.

Marcus felt a grim, sick weight settle in his gut. There was no victory in it.

Elena turned. "Martha Gable."

The old woman adjusted her glasses, the blue tape wrinkling against her brow. She looked around the cavernous, oil-stained shop, at the young apprentices holding fire axes, at the blacked-out windows vibrating against their frames.

"My sister’s grandson is out on West Jefferson," Martha said softly, her voice carrying the gravel of a lifetime spent shouting over industrial looms. "He’s nine years old. He has a winter coat, but the zipper broke last Tuesday, and his mother pinned it shut with diaper pins." She turned her rheumy eyes toward Marcus. "You talk about your substation, Hayes. You talk about bus bars. My daddy worked forty years at the foundry down on Zug Island, and whenever the slag crane failed, the foremen told the men to stay inside the pits because the machinery was worth more than their skin."

She shook her head, a single, decisive jerk. "I didn't leave my house to help build another company that counts iron before blood. I vote yes. Open the gate."

Tied. One to one.

The bay went so still Marcus could hear the faint, high-pitched hiss of the steam pressure release on the auxiliary boiler fifty yards away.

Every face turned toward Toby.

The boy sat with his boots flat on the concrete, staring at the floor between his knees. His shoulders were narrow inside the grease-stained coverall. Marcus remembered the first day Toby had walked into the shop—so terrified of the hydraulic shear he had dropped his calipers three times in twenty minutes. Marcus had spent two hours showing him how to set the gauge by eye, teaching him that steel didn't care about your fear, only your balance.

"Toby," Elena said gently.

Toby swallowed. The scar on his wrist showed pink against his knuckles as he gripped the edge of the oak bench. He looked at Marcus, his eyes wide and dark, searching his mentor's face for an answer, a permission, a way out.

Marcus kept his face rigid. He offered no sign. The Covenant didn't belong to the mechanic; it belonged to the lot.

Toby cleared his dry throat. "Marcus... Marcus told me on my first week that if an engine starts knocking because the oil line is clogged, you don't keep running it just so the dashboard lights look green. You pull the plug. Even if you're forty miles from home."

He looked toward the iron door of Gate One. Outside, the searchlight flickered as the BearCat’s engine roared, shifting into low gear.

"They're freezing to death," Toby whispered, then stood up, his voice cracking but firm. "Yes. Open the gate."

Two to one.

The tension snapped like a parted winch cable.

Marcus didn't hesitate. The doubt, the tactical calculation, the dread—he crushed them all down into the iron core of his chest. The assembly had ruled.

"Sully!" Marcus roared, moving toward the bay threshold at a dead sprint. "Shed bays six and seven! Dump all secondary storage into the radiant blowers in the staging annex! Move!"

He hit the iron throw-bar of Gate One with both boots planted, Maya right beside him, their shoulders slamming together as they threw their collective weight onto the release chain.

With a grinding groan that rattled the masonry of the outer wall, the counterweight dropped.

Gate One’s sixty-foot steel leaf shrieked upward into its tracks.

The three-million-candlepower carbon-arc light from West Jefferson flooded into the yawning mouth of the station, blinding Marcus with an explosion of white. The freezing wind hit him like a frozen wet sheet, carrying the roar of diesel engines, the smell of tear gas drifting from the south, and the desperate, stumbling rush of two hundred freezing neighbors surging toward the open threshold.

Across the street, the tactical BearCats ground to an abrupt halt, their searchlights illuminating a sea of women, children, and old men tumbling into the heated light of the plant, broadcasting the entire unedited intake through the node’s live optical mesh to twelve thousand screens across the city.

The federal assault team hesitated, their bullhorns dying mid-sentence.

But as Marcus caught Clara by the elbow, hauling her and her blue-lipped baby over the frozen threshold, his eyes caught the outer shadows beyond the searchlight beam.

Two figures in unmarked charcoal assault parkas, carrying low-slung cutting packs, slipped through the severed gap in the southern chain link, moving with silent, military precision straight toward the unguarded intake flume.
