The splintered birch toothpick rolled across Sully’s lower lip, tasting faintly of cinnamon oil and copper solder. Bay 2 smelled like a cross between a flooded root cellar and a battery yard after a lightning strike: damp limestone, the sharp vinegary bite of mineral salts, and the cold metal musk of three hundred people breathing in an enclosed hangar.

Outside, beyond the triple-wythe brick walls of the old turbine hall, the diesel engines of the rail carriers kept up an unbroken, low-frequency guttural growl. IronClad wasn't ramming the gates. They didn't need to. They had simply parked their armored flatbeds across the Conrail switches and laid heavy steel chocks against the rails, severing the access spur. They were turning Delray into an island of frozen mud.

"Keep the angle at thirty degrees," Sully said, his voice husky from three days of sleeplessness and dry throat. He didn't look up from the brass manifold in his lap. His thumbs, stained dark yellow from nutrient concentrate and raw zinc, worked a strip of PTFE tape around a threaded brass elbow. "If the drainage slope is too shallow, the runoff pools in the return trough. Standing water rots the root hair in four hours. If it's too steep, the mist slides off before the taproots can drink."

Beside him, two men from the neighborhood—retired boiler technicians from the Zug Island blast furnaces—nodded without speaking. Their knuckles were swollen with the chronic arthritis of Great Lakes winters, but their hands were steady. They pushed four-inch food-grade white plastic drainpipes into notched wooden A-frames, joining them with rubber slip-collars salvaged from an abandoned beverage bottling plant up on Fort Street. The frames rose six feet into the chill air, angled like the rafters of an unfinished cathedral, twenty rows deep under the frosted glass clerestory.

A sudden, sharp cry echoed from the elevated catwalk leading to the foundry nursery.

Sully paused, the tape snapping against his thumbnail. The sound cut straight through the dull rumble of the artesian well pump. It wasn't the startled shout of a sentinel or the scrape of steel against steel. It was the ragged, desperate wail of a mother who had just been handed an empty jar.

Footsteps clattered down the iron fire stair, frantic and uncoordinated. Toby’s cousin, an eighteen-year-old girl named Clara whose down jacket was held together across the shoulders with black gaffer tape, burst through the double plastic draft-curtains. Her face was gray beneath the warehouse halogen bulbs.

"The church van," Clara gasped, her hands clutching the collar of her jacket as if she were suffocating. "Reverend Hill’s Dodge. They had two pallets of whole milk and infant formula from the food bank in Dearborn. IronClad stopped them at West Jefferson Gate 1. They wouldn't even look at the parish manifest. They cited county sanitary quarantines. Turned the truck around at gunpoint."

A shudder went through the twenty volunteers working the PVC lines. A man dropped a brass wrench; it rang against the concrete floor with a sound like a tolling bell.

"Quarantines," one of the furnace workers spat, wiping his greasy brow with the cuff of his wool shirt. "They claim the artesian well has coliform. They're telling the city news we’re harboring a cholera outbreak in here to keep the soup kitchens from driving down the access road."

"We've got forty-six toddlers up in the gallery," Clara whispered, her voice cracking into pure terror. "The dry formula is down to three cans. Maya’s stretching it with boiled well water, but by dinner—"

"Clara," a steady voice cut through the rising murmur.

Maya stepped through the vinyl curtains. Her wool coat was buttoned to her throat, her hair pulled back into a severe, utilitarian knot secured with a length of copper wire. In her hands, she held a battered tin clipboard with the community galley rations penciled onto yellow legal paper. Her face showed the deep, parchment lines of seventy-two hours without rest, but her gaze was dead level, cold as well water.

"Nobody starves before dark," Maya said, her tone brook-no-argument flat. "And nobody runs through this bay spreading panic while there is work on the floor. I’ve already shifted the evening meal. We have sixty pounds of dried navy beans and salt pork from the church basement. The older children get bean broth with oatmeal paste. The formula is reserved strictly for the nursing infants under nine months. We have thirty-six hours of margin."

"And after thirty-six hours?" Clara asked, trembling.

Maya turned her eyes directly toward Sully. "After thirty-six hours, we eat what we grow, or we discover how long Delray can chew on its own pride."

Sully swallowed the bitter spit from his toothpick. He felt a sudden, familiar tightening behind his sternum—the exact sensation he used to get as a boy on his uncle’s broken-down commercial gill-netter on Lake Huron, when the bilge pump would choke on fish scales five miles off Presque Isle and the water began sloshing over the deckboards. In the old days, his uncle hadn't prayed or sworn; he had simply handed Sully an open-ended wrench and pointed at the manifold. *The lake doesn't care about your tears, boy. The lake only respects displacement.*

"Manifold’s sealed," Sully said, his voice dropping into the calm, mechanical rhythm that kept his hands from shaking. He stood up, his spine popping like dry kindling. "Marcus, hook the secondary feed line to the artesian discharge reservoir. Not the raw line—the settlement tank. We want zero silt entering the high-pressure loop."

Marcus, who had just entered the bay rubbing the stiffness from his bandaged shoulder, gave a tight nod. Beside him, Toby carried a crate of small, translucent plastic cylinders—each one no larger than a thimble, printed over thirty-six agonizing hours on the workshop's three open-source deposit printers using cornstarch filament. Inside each nozzle sat a tiny piezo-electric ceramic disc scavenged from disassembled ultrasonic humidifiers and household atomizers.

"Toby," Sully directed, gesturing toward the vertical racks. "Every eighteen inches along the distribution spine. Hand-tighten only. If you cross-thread the plastic collars, the backpressure will crack the casing the second the pump cycles."

The boy moved down the aisle with an intense, quiet competence, his fingers swift despite the biting chill of the unheated floor.

Sully climbed the short aluminum stepladder to the energy control board mounted on the brick wall. The heavy marine battery banks—salvaged from dry-docked tugs along the Rouge River—sat in a timber crib below, their lead plates humming with a barely perceptible vibration. On the crude analog gauges, the needles hovered precariously near the red zone. The overcast winter sky was offering only a meager trickle of diffused daylight through the roof panels. If he drew too much current for the misting cycles, the master inverter would drop into low-voltage fault, cutting power to the well pump and plunging the entire hall into freezing darkness.

He couldn't use standard grow lights. Metal halide bulbs would consume the battery bank in twenty minutes.

Instead, Sully reached up and threw a knife switch connected to four long rolls of commercial purple-gold light-emitting diode tape—narrow ribbons originally manufactured for commercial greenhouse seedling starts, stitched to aluminum angle-iron heat sinks with thermal mastic.

*Snap.*

A strange, alien violet dawn bloomed across Bay 2.

The light wasn't the warm, comfortable yellow of incandescent lamps; it was a harsh, hyper-tuned spectrum—deep ruby red at sixty-six nanometers intertwined with sharp royal blue. It bathed the bare concrete, the white plastic pipes, and the worn, exhausted faces of the Delray elders in an eerie, phosphorescent twilight.

"Pressure line opening," Sully called out.

He turned the brass gate valve. Cold, mineral-dense water from Detroit Artesian Well No. 3 surged through the sediment filters, mixing with a measured concentrate of crushed wood ash, compost leachate, and dissolved calcium from ground oyster shells scavenged from the fish-packing docks.

For three seconds, nothing happened. The pipes shuddered.

Then, from deep inside the hollow white columns, came a soft, sustained hiss—the sound of an approaching summer rain across dry pavement.

Inside each A-frame, the ultrasonic discs vibrated at a pitch beyond human hearing. The nutrient water wasn't merely sprayed; it was sheared into a dense, swirling cloud of five-micron fog. It drifted down through the darkness of the pipes, wrapping around hundreds of suspended root cups packed with washed pea gravel and toasted coconut husk.

Toby stepped to the first rack and carefully pulled back an inspection hatch.

Two days ago, while the sheriff was still serving papers at the gate, the women of Delray had laid thousands of germinated daikon radish, brassica, and winter pea seeds onto damp burlap trays in the boiler cellar. Under the sudden strike of the tuned violet photons and the rich, oxygen-saturated bath of sub-surface mist, the young shoots had exploded out of their seed-coats.

Before their eyes, cradled within the white plastic spines, lay a dense, vibrating carpet of vivid, saturated green.

Clara reached out a trembling, dirt-caked finger. She touched a curled, heart-shaped leaf of young brassica. It was crisp, bead-strung with ultrasonic dew, smelling overwhelmingly of crushed pepper, wet river loam, and unstoppable life.

"Sweet Jesus," an old boiler hand muttered, pulling his grease-stained knit cap from his head. "They're growing in thin air."

"Not thin air," Sully said quietly, wiping a smudge of copper flux from his nose with his sleeve. "Water, salt, and light. No banks. No corporate food trucks. No permission."

Toby took a pair of stainless utility shears from his belt. Carefully, his hands reverent, he clipped a handful of the dense radish microgreens and dropped them into Clara’s palm. The girl looked down at the bright emerald pile, then shoved a pinch into her mouth. Her eyes widened, her chest heaving as the sharp, peppery punch of pure living chlorophyll hit her tongue. A choked sob broke from her throat—not of grief, but of absolute, visceral relief.

"Take a basket upstairs to the kitchen," Maya ordered softly, though her dark eyes were shining under the violet lamps. "Tell them to mince it into the bean broth. Tell them the nursery eats first."

Clara turned and ran back toward the stairs, clutching the green bundle against her breast like gold.

Sully let out a long, shuddering breath, leaning his forehead against the cold iron of the ladder. His pulse was finally slowing. The battery needle remained steady, held aloft by the fine balance of the misting cycle. For the first time in forty-eight hours, the machine was breathing in rhythm with the people who had built it.

Then, the concrete slab beneath their boots shuddered.

It wasn't the diesel rumble of the trains outside. It was a deep, hollow, hydraulic percussion—echoing up through the cast-iron stormwater grate situated squarely in the center of the bay floor.

Sully froze. Toby’s shears slipped from his fingers, clattering on the floor.

Beneath the heavy slotted iron lid, thirty feet below the foundations in the ancient municipal sewer trunk that connected the foundry directly to the Detroit River, came the distinct, metallic scrape of cutting torches biting through rebar.
