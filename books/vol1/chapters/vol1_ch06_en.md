The phosphor on Kira Vance’s primary display was dying, casting a sickly greenish halo over the condensation dripping down the control room’s salt-etched glass. 

Outside, across the eastern rail spur, the diesel shunter screamed its metal-on-metal chorus, an ugly, grinding racket designed to pull every eye in Delray toward the front gates. But Kira didn’t look east. She pressed the worn foam of her headset tighter against her ear, listening to the static hiss of the short-range mesh antenna she had jury-rigged inside an empty grease tin on the roof. 

Her right hand rested on an unpainted trackball, its surface pitted from years of thumb-pressure. Long before she had walked away from the defense contractor’s proving grounds in the Florida scrub, she had learned that when an adversary makes a show of bringing iron to your front door, their real knife is already slipping under your ribs.

On the bottom monitor, fed by three repurposed perimeter thermals she had lagged to the rotting river pilings behind Bay 1, the river looked like black oil. Then the water broke.

It was faint: a four-lobed disturbance in the thermal gradient, glowing a dim thirty-five degrees against the river’s six-degree chill. Four human silhouettes, insulated in drysuits that bled only trace heat at the seams and necks, slipped out of a flat-bottomed inflatable tethered to the slimy green timber of the disused intake slip.

"Marcus," she whispered into the mic. Her voice was flat, dried out by stale instant coffee and the lingering chemical tang of the solder she’d burned rewiring the junction box an hour before dawn. "Bay 1 slip. Four guests. Low profile. They aren't carrying breach charges. They’ve got silent hydraulic shears."

Through the earpiece came the sound of Marcus Hayes breathing. It wasn't the ragged panting of a panicked man, but the slow, rhythmic intake of someone nursing an old childhood asthma rattle he’d never quite outgrown. 

"I'm at the lower catwalk," Marcus answered, his voice barely a rasp above the floor vibrations. "Can't see the water. The fog off the channel is thick as sheep's wool."

"Trust my glass, not your eyes," Kira said. She rolled the trackball. One of the camera lenses was blurring; moisture had penetrated the silicone gasket, turning the intruder on the left into a smeared orange thumbprint. "They're moving under the timber deck. They know the plant’s dead zones. Someone handed them the nineteen-eighty-four utility map."

"That map doesn't show the secondary bulkhead," Marcus muttered. "City sealed it during the high-water floods five years back."

"They aren't heading for the bulkhead," she said, watching the heat blooms crawl up the rusty intake ladder like beetles climbing a wet pipe. "They’re tracking the high-voltage trench. They’re coming for the core."

The IronClad operators moved with the sickening efficiency of men paid on delivery. No flashlights. No spoken words. Through the thermal feed, she watched the lead operator bring a compact, battery-driven hydraulic spreader to the intake grate. The ancient cast iron, brittle from forty Detroit winters of road-salt runoff, didn't shear; it popped with a dull thud that Kira felt through the soles of her boots three levels up.

Her chest tightened. A sharp memory—unbidden and cold—cut through the screen glare: an airfield hangar in Fort Walton Beach, watching telemetry from an autonomous target acquisition trial, realizing the software she’d written didn't know the difference between an armed sentry and an irrigation worker carrying a hoe. She had signed the non-disclosure agreements, packed her footlocker, and spent three years trying to wash the blue light of military-grade silicon out of her retinas. Now the same cold precision had followed her to the edge of the river.

"Marcus," she breathed. "They're inside the intake corridor. Speed is two meters a second. They’ll hit the main floor in four minutes."

"I don't have enough chain to lash the corridor gate," Marcus whispered. "And we don't shoot."

"We don't need to shoot," Kira said. Her fingers flew over the keyboard, calling up the plant’s pneumatic ventilation schematic. The display was a patchwork of open-source layout files and scanned architectural blueprints from the plant's mid-century expansion. "We let the building do the work. Remember the paint-curing bay?"

A beat of silence hung on the line. "The old downdraft bay? The solvent room?"

"The air seals on that bay were built to withstand an industrial vapor explosion," Kira said. Her pulse hammered in her throat, but her hands were steady. "If they cut across the machine shop, they have to pass through its service tunnel. Toby replaced the pneumatic cylinder on the east fire door yesterday, but the primary slab is tied to the plant’s magnetic hold-backs."

"The hold-backs are de-energized," Marcus said. "I pulled the fuses at midnight to save the bank."

"I’m routing six hundred watts from the rooftop solar bank directly into the secondary bus right now," Kira said, clicking through a custom command prompt. On her board, a row of amber indicators turned a deep, expectant red. "Marcus, you’re forty paces north of the paint bay entrance. Don't engage. Just trip the bypass lever on the air damper above their heads. Give them a draft to chase."

"On it."

Kira watched the camera lag. The plant's internal mesh was struggling; the thick reinforced concrete of the floor slabs chewed through the low-frequency radio packets, dropping every third frame. On her screen, the four heat signatures glided through the machinery shadows like ghosts, stepping over disused crane tracks without scraping a single boot heel against the iron. They held their suppressed short-barrels tucked tight against their tactical vests, safeties off, scanning the empty catwalks above. 

They weren't looking for a fight; they were looking for the central processing racks—the beating ledger that kept the power flowing to the streetlights and oxygen concentrators three blocks away.

A shadow broke the thermal noise near the intake plenum. Marcus. 

He didn't run. He moved with the heavy, deliberate gait of a man who had spent three decades navigating dark boiler houses by memory, feeling for the steam-pipe welds with his palms. Kira saw his thermal shape reach up into the darkness above a three-foot duct, find the weighted iron counter-lever of the ventilation damper, and pull.

A metallic shriek—groaning, rusted, agonizingly loud—ripped through the cavernous quiet of the floor.

The four intruders froze. Their muzzles swung upward instantly, tracking the sound. The lead operator made a sharp hand signal: two fingers forward, dispersing his team toward the only open access corridor that promised egress away from the sound—the concrete airlock of the paint-curing bay.

"They're taking the bait," Kira whispered, her teeth sinking into the soft flesh of her lower lip. "They're entering the outer threshold. Two inside... three..."

The fourth operator hesitated at the doorway, weapon lowered, sweeping his thermal optic across the dark floor plates. He saw something—perhaps the fresh scrape of Marcus's boot in the coal dust, or the faint click of a relay settling on the overhead tray. He stepped back, reaching for the radio clipped to his collarbone.

"Now, Kira!" Marcus roared, his voice bursting through the radio without tactical discipline.

Kira slammed her palm down on the broad execution key at the corner of the desk.

The magnetic coils, starved of juice for hours, took the solar bank’s stored current in a violent, hungry gulp. Forty feet below, four three-hundred-pound, lead-lined blast doors dropped from their overhead ceiling recesses along greased vertical tracks.

The sound was not an explosion. It was the monstrous, concussive *thunk* of solid industrial iron striking steel sills, followed immediately by the sharp *hiss* of expanding neoprene seals seating against vacuum flanges. 

The entire control room shuddered. A cloud of fine, fifty-year-old gypsum dust drifted down from the ceiling joists, coating Kira’s monitors in a pale gray skin.

On the camera feed, the four heat signatures were suddenly severed from the world. Behind three inches of solid structural plate and airtight ceramic baffles, their infrared glow vanished completely.

Inside the bay, they had sixty thousand cubic feet of breathable air, water from the dead rinse lines, and absolute, impenetrable silence. No cellular signal. No satellite uplink. No exit.

Silence fell over the plant, so absolute that Kira could hear the rhythmic drip of water from the intake slip forty yards away.

"Marcus?" she called, her voice trembling now that the adrenaline was draining into her marrow. "Marcus, talk to me."

"I'm here," came the rough reply. A long, shuddering exhale rattled in the speaker. "Doors are down. Latches are locked. They're banging on the inner plate with their boots, but it's like kicking an anvil."

Kira dropped her head onto her arms for three long seconds, breathing in the scent of fried wiring and her own salt sweat. Then she stood, her knees cracking in the chilly air of the control room. "The fourth one," she said, remembering. "He dropped something when the door came down."

She left the monitors humming and took the iron spiral stairs down two flights, her boots clattering on the perforated treads. 

Marcus was standing in front of the eastern curing bay door, his broad shoulders slumped, his flannel shirt soaked through with damp river mist. At his feet, half-crushed beneath the steel lip of the emergency blast door, lay a ruggedized tactical transit case and an encrypted comm-link, its display still glowing a cold, predatory blue against the oil-stained floor.

Kira knelt, her fingers cold as she slid the comm-pad out from under the door's lead skirt. 

The screen was not locked. It displayed an open operational manifest, mirrored across an encrypted private carrier node. 

Marcus leaned over her, his breath warm and sour in the freezing air. "What does it say, Vance? Is it Campbell's federal warrant?"

Kira scrolled through the digital parchment. Her breath hitched.

It wasn't an injunction. It wasn't a corporate seizure order or an asset-recovery warrant. 

At the bottom of the encrypted log, beneath a high-resolution satellite overlay of their exact substation coordinates, was an operational clearance code signed directly by the Vice President of Physical Infrastructure for Meridian Crest Capital. 

The authorization was brief, clinical, and stripped of legal pretension:

*Target: Node Core Class-Zero. Protocol: Kinetic Interdiction. Authorization: Total physical asset liquidation. Collateral containment: Non-recoverable.*

"Marcus," Kira whispered, the blue light reflecting in her wide, dark eyes. "They didn't come to turn the power off."

Above them, out on the river slip, the deep, hollow horn of an approaching Coast Guard cutter began to mourn through the fog.
