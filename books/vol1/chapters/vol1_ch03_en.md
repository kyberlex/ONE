The cold in Bay 3 didn’t just hang in the air; it sank into marrow. Every breath Marcus took tasted of sulfur, pulverized slag, and the wet, greasy river mist blowing through the broken clerestory windows eighty feet overhead. His right hand throbbed beneath the layers of black electrical tape, the stiffened knuckles aching from twelve straight hours of torquing rusted carriage bolts. 

He didn’t reach for coffee—there wasn’t any left, only a pot of boiled chicory that smelled like burned earth. Instead, out of an old freight-yard habit he hadn’t used in a decade, Marcus dug a thumbnail into the seam of his greasy Carhartt pocket, pressing until the sharp pinch centered his thoughts.

Down on the machine shop floor, the shouting had already reached the pitch of breaking iron.

"Step off it, Frank! I swear on my mother’s soul, you touch that console and I’ll put this through your ribs!"

"Your girl’s got the cramps because you gave her ditch water, Gus! But if Briggs brings those drop-rams through Gate 4, we won’t have ribs left to break!"

Marcus swung down the rusted steel ladder, his boots hitting the grease-slick slab with a dull *thud*.

Two men stood chest-to-chest in the greenish phosphor glow of the primary milling bay. The machine itself—a hulking, multi-axis automated cutter salvaged from the plant’s prototype line—sat idling with a deep, hydraulic hum that vibrated straight through the soles of Marcus’s boots. 

Gus stood on the left, trembling with a ragged, feverish rage. He was fifty-eight, a retired boilermaker whose ankles had swollen so badly from unmanaged diabetes that his work boots were slit down the tongue and laced with baling twine. In his raw, calloused hands, he clutched a five-gallon bucket of foul, tea-colored liquid pulled from the Delray shallow wells, its sulfur stench turning Marcus’s stomach. Behind Gus, stacked like cordwood on a wooden pallet, were six heavy cylinders of pressed river clay and diatomaceous earth, waiting to be bored into ceramic filtration candles.

Facing him was Frank, thirty-five, a commercial roofer with a neck baked into leather by decades of tar fumes, missing the outer half of his left index finger from an old hoist accident. Frank was clutching a three-foot pinch bar, his eyes wild with the trapped panic of an animal backed into an alley. At Frank’s feet lay three heavy plates of structural steel, dragged from the scrap heap behind the blast furnace.

"We got two hours on Kowalski’s court order," Frank spat, his voice shredding at the edges. "Less than forty minutes left on the clock now! You think that judge gives a damn about Delray? You think Briggs’s boys are gonna wait for your mud pipes to dry? We need these gussets cut and slotted for Gate 4! If we don’t tie the rail stanchions to the main footings, their front loader will fold that sheet metal like a cardboard box!"

"My Mia’s been vomiting blood since midnight!" Gus roared, the spit flying from his cracked lips, his chest heaving inside a coat stuffed with old insulation batting. "The city shut the pressure off to the hydrants yesterday, and the tanker Meridian promised never showed! She drinks this sludge raw, she doesn’t make it to tomorrow! The machine runs the filter cores *first*!"

Frank raised the pinch bar six inches, knuckles turning the color of skim milk. "One hour of steel work. That’s all I’m asking! Then you can play with your dirt!"

"Touch that chuck," Gus whispered, pulling a rusted drywall knife from his waistband, "and see what happens."

Marcus didn't yell. Screaming only made men double down on stupid. He stepped between them, his heavy twenty-four-inch pipe wrench held in his right hand. He didn’t raise it; he simply brought the cast-iron head down onto the concrete between Frank’s boots with a flat, deafening *crack* that sent a shockwave through their shins.

Both men jumped back, breathing hard, the mist smoking from their nostrils.

"Put the steel down, Frank," Marcus said, his voice a low, gravelly rasp that came from thirty years of talking over running diesels. "And Gus, slide that blade back in your pocket before you drop it and cut your own toes off."

"Marcus, you saw the flatbeds," Frank pleaded, his jaw working violently, gesturing with the pinch bar toward the rail spur outside. "You saw the drop-rams! They’re lining up right now! They’re gonna crush us!"

"I saw them," Marcus said. He didn’t look away from Frank’s eyes. "And I know exactly what six tons of hydraulic ram does to an unbraced post. But you look at me, Frank. You look at that screen." 

Marcus pointed the wrench at the flickering interface above the spindle. The battery reserve bar at the top of the monitor was creeping downward, shedding a fraction of a percent every ten minutes as the plant’s auxiliary inverters struggled against the morning damp. 

"The machine doesn't care about your fear, and it doesn't care about Briggs," Marcus continued, pacing his breath to keep his own pulse from hammering. "Under the Covenant we signed at the gate this morning, Article Two is clear. Usufruct goes to biological survival before structural defense. Life support first. If we turn this place into a fortress and let the children inside poison themselves on sewer drainage, then Briggs doesn't need to ram the gate. He just has to sit out there and wait for us to bury each other."

Gus let out a ragged, weeping breath, his shoulders slumping. But Frank took a step forward, his boot sole grinding metal shavings into the floor.

"That’s a fine sermon, Marcus," Frank hissed, his eyes bloodshot. "A real pretty speech. But when that gate buckles, your rules don’t mean spit. Briggs doesn't sign your damn paper."

"He doesn't have to," a voice broke in from the dark space behind the tool carousel.

Toby slipped out from the narrow passage between the coolant reservoir and the scrap bin. His knuckles were black with dried machine grease, his oversized canvas jacket pinned shut at the collar with an old safety pin. He was chewing the inside of his cheek, a habit Marcus had noticed whenever the boy was trying to solve a puzzle he hadn't yet put into words. 

In Toby’s left hand was a splintered wooden crate, stamped with faded blue lettering from an automotive supplier that had folded during the last recession.

"What you got there, kid?" Marcus asked, keeping his body positioned squarely between the two older men.

"Carbide twin-flutes," Toby said, setting the crate on the edge of the tool table with a sharp rattle. "Fourteen-millimeter shank. Roughing geometry with chip-breakers. Someone stashed them in the bottom of the sump locker before the plant closed down and coated them in cosmolene."

Frank sneered. "We don't need bits, boy. We need time! The spindle only holds one toolholder at a—"

"No, it doesn't," Toby interrupted, his voice dropping out of its adolescent register into something steady, sharp, and focused. He pointed a grease-stained thumb at the tool turret head above the machine bed. "The main carriage has an auxiliary dovetail on the secondary slide. It was rigged for dual-station automotive head boring back in the nineties. If we mount the ceramic core fixture on the left trunnion and clamp Frank’s angle iron on the right bed..."

Toby pulled an oil-spotted spiral notebook from his coat. On the grid paper, drawn in clean, precise ballpoint strokes, was an interleaved sequence. 

"The toolpath for the clay core only uses the z-axis for boring out the center channel," Toby explained, looking directly at Frank, then at Gus. "The spindle clears the workpiece every ninety seconds to flush the dust with the air jet. During that ninety-second retraction, the gantry traverses thirty inches to the right. We can punch the slot holes through your plate brackets on the return stroke without interrupting the drying cycle on the ceramic."

Marcus stared at the sketch, then up at the multi-axis gantry. His mind traced the mechanical clearances, calculating the spindle speed, the feed rate, and the risk of chatter. It was tight—dangerously tight. A single misaligned clamp would throw the spindle out of true, snapping the irreplaceable carbide bit and killing the bay entirely.

"The air pressure's marginal," Marcus murmured, thinking aloud. "The compressor's leaking at the manifold."

"I wrapped the manifold fitting with vulcanized rubber and hose clamps twenty minutes ago," Toby said quietly. "It's holding ninety pounds."

Marcus looked at Gus. The older man wiped his nose on his sleeve, nodding once, desperately. Marcus looked at Frank. The roofer’s jaw remained tight, but the pinch bar lowered another three inches, its tip resting against the greasy concrete.

"Can you write the g-code to interleave them?" Marcus asked Toby.

"I already loaded it from the terminal upstairs," Toby said. "Shenzhen’s open-source library had the ceramic profile; I merged the hole pattern Frank drew on the wall with chalk."

Marcus let out a long breath, the tension in his shoulders easing just enough to let the cold creep back in. "Set the clamps. Gus, get your clay sleeves centered on the rotary table. Frank, square that steel against the stops. Toby, you ride the emergency stop button. If that toolhead chatters by half a millimeter, kill the feed."

For ten minutes, Bay 3 transformed. The rage didn't disappear—it was converted into the frantic, sweating friction of human labor. Frank hoisted the heavy structural steel onto the T-slot table, his boots slipping on the oil slick until Gus reached out with a scarred hand, bracing Frank’s shoulder to keep him from going down under the weight of the plate. They didn't apologize. They didn't shake hands. But Frank handed Gus the centering square, and Gus tapped the steel true with the wooden handle of his trowel.

Toby keyed the cycle start.

The automated cutter roared to life. A high-pitched, screaming whine filled the cavernous bay as the carbide bit bit into the dense river clay, sending a pale plume of dry, mineral dust curling toward the exhaust hood. Ninety seconds later, the head rose with a pneumatic hiss, shot horizontally across the carriage, and plunged its secondary cutter through Frank’s mild steel plate, spitting an arc of dull orange sparks against the safety glass.

Gus leaned his forehead against the safety enclosure, watching the clay hollow out into a smooth, porous cylinder that would strip the lead and cadmium from his daughter's drinking water. Beside him, Frank counted the punched slots, his lips moving silently, measuring the reinforcement that would keep the drop-rams from tearing the hinges off Gate 4.

Marcus stood back near the tool rack, leaning against the cold iron of the gantry pillar. His right hand still ached, but the twitch in his thumb had stopped. For the first time in thirty-six hours, the plant felt like something other than a tomb.

Then, precisely at ten o'clock, the high windows vanished.

The dirty gray light of the Detroit morning was obliterated by an instantaneous, blinding wall of white brilliance. 

Half a million lumens of high-output stadium floodlights, mounted to the cabs of Briggs’s diesel transports out on the rail siding, struck the Monolith’s northern face simultaneously. The light tore through the broken glass, projecting the massive, skeletal shadows of the roof trusses across the floor of Bay 3, turning the air into an incandescent fog.

The scream of the mill was swallowed instantly by the crushing roar of a heavy commercial bullhorn echoing off the concrete silos outside.

*"OCCUPANTS OF PARCEL FOUR. THIS IS COMMANDER BRIGGS, CANTON TACTICAL LOGISTICS. THE WAYNE COUNTY STAY HAS BEEN DISSOLVED BY EMERGENCY ORDER OF THE CIRCUIT BENCH. YOU HAVE FIVE MINUTES TO CLEAR THE APERTURE AT GATE FOUR."*

The spindle continued to carve, sparks showering against the glass, but every man in the bay froze, blinded by the glare. Outside, through the vibrating floor plates, Marcus felt the rhythmic, hydraulic thud of the drop-rams beginning to cycle.
