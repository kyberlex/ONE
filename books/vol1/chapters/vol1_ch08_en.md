The wind off the Detroit River smelled of salt, wet soot, and rotting reeds. It hit David Sullivan squarely in the chest, cutting through his thin Gore-Tex shell and three layers of cotton like a wire blade.

High on the plant’s parapet, forty feet above the silent cordon of federal and county cruisers, Sully knelt beside a metal transit case strapped to a ventilation housing. His fingers, numb inside fingerless wool gloves, shook against the rotary dial of the optical transceiver. Stale drip coffee—sour and bitter from six reheats in the breakroom microwave—churned in his stomach, firing sharp bursts of acid up his throat.

Down on West Jefferson, the sheriff’s deputies were holding their line, pinned under the glare of their own cruiser spots, but the airwaves were dead. Every civilian cell tower from Clark Street to Zug Island had gone dark an hour ago. The local carriers had dropped Delray into a silent hole, isolating the enclave like an island cut loose in open water.

Except the water didn’t belong to them.

Twelve hundred meters across the gray, churning shipping channel, the concrete grain silos of historic Sandwich, Ontario, loomed against the falling dusk. Somewhere on that weathered Canadian rooftop, behind the rusted letters of an abandoned milling company, an identical infrared receiver was waiting for a pulse.

"Packet loss is sitting at forty percent," Kira’s voice crackled through Sully’s earpiece, thin and strained. She was huddled two floors below in the transformer vault, watching the packet buffer creep toward catastrophic overflow. "The river mist is eating the wavelength, Sully. If we can't lock the optical bridge in five minutes, the buffer corrupts and drops the handshake."

"I'm on it," Sully muttered through chattering teeth. He peered through the optical scope, blinking away tears whipped up by the gale. The red diode crosshairs danced erratically against the dark Ontario shoreline. "The mounting bracket is vibrating. The gale's catching the crane superstructure."

Ten feet above him, clinging to the icy ladder of the plant’s retired rail gantry, Marcus Hayes shifted his boots. Marcus didn’t have safety harnesses; he had a pair of stiff leather work gloves and forty years of muscle memory built on high-steel framing. Above him, tucked into the narrow iron basket beneath the pulley housing, Toby was wedged between two rusted angle irons, a brass alignment wrench clenched between his teeth.

A high-intensity searchlight suddenly flared from the perimeter fence below. The beam slashed upward through the rolling river fog, catching the crane’s lattice in a searing diamond glare.

Marcus didn't flinch. He just leaned his shoulder against the frosted steel strut, shielding the boy from the direct dazzle of the light. "Keep your eyes on the bezel, Toby," Marcus called down, his voice gravel-rough over the wind. "Don't look at the road. Look at the brass."

Toby spit the wrench into his palm. His knuckles were raw and split from a week of running cable through conduit, but when he set the jaws to the micro-adjustment screw, his hands didn't shake. During his summer working the tire balancer at the truck stop on Fort Street, he’d developed an instinctive feel for quarter-millimeter tolerances. He didn't need to see the graduation marks; he felt the threads seat against the cold grease.

"Azimuth is out by two clicks south," Toby yelled down through the buffeting air. "The mount twisted when that freight barge pushed through the channel wake!"

"Take it two clicks north," Sully called out, his thumb hovering over the transceiver’s diagnostic toggle. "Gently, Toby. Just a hair."

Through the lens, Sully watched the Canadian grain elevator. A faint, invisible beam—nine hundred and five nanometers of coherent infrared—was stabbing through the freezing chop. In clear summer air, the optical link would have closed in a microsecond. But the river was exhaling a dense, thermal river-breath, swirling damp plumes of vapor into the boundary layer between the two countries. The light was scattering like buckshot in a snowdrift.

*Sixty-two percent packet loss.*

"The buffer's at eighty-eight percent capacity," Kira warned. Her cadence had tightened, stripping away every nonessential syllable. "The Zurich peer node is timing out. If we drop the session key, the international route seals for six hours."

Sully’s heart hammered against his ribs. It was the same sick, hollow flutter he’d felt eight years ago when he sat before the disciplinary committee at the exchange, watching five senior partners quietly redact the anomaly reports he had spent two years compiling. They had called it an *unsupported operational divergence.* They had escorted him from the building with two security guards and a brown cardboard box, ensuring his name would never clear an institutional background check again.

They had buried the truth because it existed only in private ledgers behind corporate firewalls.

*Not this time.*

"Toby," Sully whispered, then caught himself and yelled against the gale: "One click back! You overshot the focal prism!"

A metallic *tink* rang out from the crane. Toby leaned out over empty air, his knee hooked into the rusted gusset plate, his weight suspended forty feet above the gravel railbed. Marcus’s right arm shot out like an iron clamp, his hand gripping the belt of Toby’s work coat, anchoring the boy against the whistling void with brute, unyielding leverage.

"Got it," Toby gasped. "Set the lock ring!"

On Sully’s monitor, the amber diagnostic light flickered once, twice, and then snapped into brilliant, unblinking green.

The scattering cleared. The crosshairs locked dead-center onto the receiver prism across the international boundary.

A soft, low-frequency hum vibrated through the casing as the high-speed data stream took hold. Denied access to the severed domestic fiber lines and jammed local towers, Delray’s isolated network punched its line-of-sight laser through the open sky, crossing the border at the speed of light, bridging into the Canadian fiber trunks in Windsor, and diving straight into the transatlantic cables anchored off Nova Scotia.

"Link confirmed," Kira breathed over the circuit, her relief practically fogging the mic. "Throughput is thirty gigabits. We’re in."

Below in the staging bay, Elena was already sitting before the terminal bank, her reading glasses perched on her nose, the glow of the screen illuminating the tight set of her jaw. The ledger arrived not as a file, but as a torrential downpour of cryptographically signed public records: corporate registry filings from the canton of Zug, Frankfurt clearinghouse receipts, and municipal bond clearing certificates from 2018.

Sully scrambled down the ladder, his boots hitting the roof gravel with a crunch, and ducked through the heavy steel hatch into the warm, grease-scented air of the mezzanine. Toby and Marcus were right behind him, stomping the sleet from their boots, their breath billowing white in the unheated stairwell.

"What are we looking at?" Marcus asked, peeling his gloves off to reveal skin reddened to the color of raw beef.

Elena didn't look up. Her finger scrolled through a dense cascade of land survey coordinates, environmental baseline audits, and debt-restructuring covenants.

"It wasn't a market failure," she said, her voice dropping into an icy, dangerous calm. "Nathan Sterling didn't buy the plant’s paper after the foundry shuttered. He manufactured the default."

Sully leaned over her shoulder, squinting at the foreign-currency clearing manifests. His training took over, his mind peeling back the nested shell companies until the underlying architecture lay bare.

"The 2018 soil tests," Sully whispered, pointing at an encrypted German registry stamp. "The state declared the plant an uninsurable toxic hazard because the runoff samples showed six hundred parts per million of industrial solvents. That’s what triggered the bond acceleration and forced the union to surrender the deed."

"Look at the processing lab," Elena said, tapping the glass. "The testing entity wasn't state certified. It was an off-ledger subsidiary of Meridian Crest, incorporated in Liechtenstein forty-eight hours before the soil core samples were drawn. The actual state baseline samples taken the same morning were suppressed—bought out through a private settlement agreement buried in an offshore holding escrow."

"The ground wasn't poisoned," Marcus said. His voice was very quiet, but it vibrated with a terrifying, subterranean pressure. "They poisoned the paper."

"They stole sixteen city blocks and four thousand jobs with an unfiled pdf and a bought-and-paid-for lab tech," Sully said. The full magnitude of it settled into the room, cold and heavy as iron. "And now we have the original, unredacted federal custody log. Cryptographically verified by three independent registries in Europe."

"Get it to Campbell," Marcus said, turning toward the door. "Show the prosecutor what he's protecting out there."

"Wait," Toby said.

The boy had not taken off his coat. He was standing by the high casement window that overlooked the river channel, his forehead pressed against the grime-filmed pane.

"Sully," Toby called, his voice suddenly sharp. "Look at the water."

Sully hurried to the glass.

Down on the dark, choppy surface of the Detroit River, a hundred yards off the Canadian boundary line, a low-slung, black-hulled patrol boat was idling without navigation lights. Its twin diesel engines threw a low, wet growl across the water, barely audible beneath the roar of the shore wind.

Mounted on the vessel’s foredeck was a squat, twin-barreled launcher.

Before Sully could shout, a sharp, concussive *thwump* split the night air.

Two canisters arched into the sky over the channel, tumbling end over end through the sleet. At the apex of their trajectory, fifty feet directly in the path of the rooftop laser bridge, they detonated—not with fire or shrapnel, but with a dense, blinding cloud of hot, particulate zinc smoke that bloomed instantly across the freezing air, rolling like a black curtain across the international boundary.

On the desk behind them, the green signal bar violently collapsed into red.
