The screaming began on the third floor, thin and jagged as torn tin.

It was not the panicked shouting of the machinists, nor the iron rattle of the blowers fighting the evening frost. It was a four-month-old infant in the nursery ward over Bay 2, starved down to colic and fury.

Toby Morales stood at the foot of the mezzanine stairs, his knuckles wedged into the pockets of his grease-stained canvas jacket. Through the open fire door above, he could see Maya kneeling on the bare floorboards beside a row of milk-crate cribs. She was scraping the bottom of an industrial tin of powdered formula with the edge of a plastic measuring scoop. The sound was dry and hollow—metal screeching on cardboard. Three mothers sat against the brick wall with their knees pulled to their chests, coats zipped to their chins against the descending chill, their faces gray under the low-draw sodium worklights. One of them was softly beating her knuckles against her own forehead in an exhausted, rhythmic rhythm of helplessness.

"Two ounces each," Maya was saying, her voice strained to a thin wire. "If we stretch it with warm boiled well water, it will coat their stomachs until morning."

"There is no morning without fat in it, Maya," a woman whispered back. Her baby’s cries had hitched into a dry, gasping wheeze. "She can't digest plain starch. She’ll convulse."

Toby turned away before Maya could look down and catch him watching. His chest felt constricted, as if someone had wrapped an exhaust clamp around his ribs and taken two turns with an impact wrench.

He knew every dead end in Delray. Long before the plant had raised its gates, back when the neighborhood was just four square miles of crumbling salt-spalled bungalows and illegal chop shops, Toby and the boys from Cavalry Street had lived beneath the street level. When the school district truant vans had patrolled West Jefferson, they hadn't hidden in the alleys; they had dropped into the pre-depression brick drainage trunks that vented stormwater straight into the Old Channel of the River Rouge. He knew which iron rings were rusted through to needle points and which conduits had collapsed under eighty years of freight-train ballast.

More importantly, he knew what was sitting three hundred yards east, cached under the rotting timbers of the Conrail rail trestle: two wax-sealed shipping totes of high-calorie infant formula and pediatrics, left there three hours ago by couriers from the Holy Redeemer parish soup kitchen who couldn't pierce the perimeter wire.

He walked swiftly back to the maintenance bench in Bay 3.

Marcus wasn't there; the big mechanic was up on the catwalks inspecting the secondary louvers, his sling tucked into his coat to keep the cold from setting into his broken collarbone. Toby reached for his heavy leather tool pouch, then paused. The thick cowhide would hang up on the twelve-inch clearance collars where the old overflow pipes necked down. He unbuckled the belt and laid the pouch squarely on the anvil. Next to it, he set his brass calipers—the ones Marcus had spent three nights helping him true against a granite surface plate.

"Leave the steel," Toby muttered under his breath, a habit he’d had since he was nine, talking himself through the tight crawlspaces beneath his grandmother’s porch. "Steel makes noise. Noise brings the light."

Two shadows detached themselves from the oil-drum heater near the scrap bin: Leo and Jax. Delray kids. Jax still wore a high school track jacket with the sleeves torn off, his forearms mottled red from the biting factory draft.

"You got the bolt snips?" Toby asked without looking up, slipping a bent tire iron and a roll of self-fusing silicone tape into his coat pockets.

"In the bucket," Leo whispered. His teeth were chattering, but his eyes were bright with that sharp, wild defiance that came to boys who had grown up knowing nobody was coming to rescue them. "Father Frank said the church van got chased off toward Fort Street by two of those black armored pickups. But the crates are under the bridge deck. Wrapped in construction plastic."

"The water in the trunk is three feet deep," Toby said, checking the lithium button-cells in his taped-up headlamp. "It’s river backflow. Near freezing. If you soak your core, you don't make it back up the ladder."

"My brother’s kid is up there screaming," Jax said flatly. "Let's go."

They lifted the round cast-iron manhole in the corner of the boiler trench. The smell hit them instantly—not fresh river water, but the ancient, sour reek of industrial sediment: coal tar, machine oil dumped into the dirt fifty years ago, and the sweet, suffocating funk of rotting river weed.

Toby dropped through first.

His boots hit the slime-slick invert with a heavy, dead splash. The cold struck through his leather soles within five seconds, an ache that bit straight into the marrow of his shins. Above him, Jax and Leo lowered the heavy lid back into its seating rim until only an eighth-inch gap remained for air, then dropped down beside him.

The conduit was five feet high, arched with hand-laid red brick that had black sulfur crystals sweating between the joints. The city didn't keep maps of this section anymore; the paperwork had burned in the municipal basement fire back in the nineties.

"Keep your heads low," Toby breathed, the vapor puffing white against his taped headlamp. "Don't touch the ceiling. The brick mortar has turned to paste. If you dislodge a crown key, the whole section drops."

They waded in single file, moving against a sluggish, greasy current that dragged at their knees. The water was forty-two degrees—warm enough to be liquid, cold enough to paralyze a cramp into a calf muscle within twenty paces. Toby kept his left hand sliding along the springline of the brick arch, feeling for the rhythmic vibration of the surface.

Fifty yards in, the deep, mechanical hum of the foundry died away, replaced by a hollow, cavernous silence broken only by the slosh of their thighs and the distant, heavy thrum of diesel engines on the grade above.

*Conrail siding,* Toby thought. *IronClad patrol boats on the river, idling at the mouth.*

Behind them, five hundred feet back at the plant, a sudden clatter echoed down the brick cylinder.

Marcus.

Toby didn't have to look back to know. He could hear the distinct, heavy drag of Marcus’s steel-toed work boots hitting the rungs of the access shaft, followed by a hoarse, ragged roar: *"Toby! Get your ass back up that shaft!"*

The voice broke on a dry cough. Marcus was moving fast, careless of his cracked bones, driven by that blind, furious terror that Toby had seen in him only once before—the night the warehouse roof had buckled under wet snow.

"Don't stop," Toby whispered harshly to Jax. "Move."

"Marcus is gonna break both our necks, man," Jax hissed.

"Not if the babies eat first. Move!"

They reached the junction where the municipal brick vault met the corrugated galvanized pipe that dumped into the River Rouge culvert. The metal was jagged, corroded away at the waterline by decades of acid runoff, leaving rusted iron teeth that snagged at Toby’s canvas coat. He dropped to his hands and knees in the freezing black sludge, the water rising to his collarbones, his breath catching in his throat as the icy shock hit his sternum.

He didn't pray. He remembered his mother boiling tap water on an electric skillet when the gas was shut off, humming an old church chorus to drown out the sound of the wind rattling the cracked storm windows. He bit the inside of his cheek until he tasted copper, dragging himself forward on his elbows.

The culvert opened into the open air beneath the railway bridge.

Freezing fog hung thick over the Old Channel, gray and heavy as wet fleece. Above them, the iron girders of the rail span loomed like the ribcage of a beached freighter. And there, lashed to the concrete footing with frayed nylon ratchet straps, sat the two plastic milk crates, covered in black poly sheeting.

"Got them," Toby wheezed. His fingers were completely numb, wooden clubs that refused to hook into the buckle. "Jax. Tire iron. Pry the strap."

Jax jammed the iron under the nylon and hauled back with his full weight. The strap snapped with the crack of a pistol shot.

The sound seemed to hang suspended in the frozen fog.

Across the water, three hundred yards out on the channel, a low-slung black patrol launch swung its bow. But it wasn't the boat that found them.

A hundred yards up the rail embankment, perched on the crushed basalt ballast of the switch-track, a pair of boots crunched on gravel.

A high-pitched electronic whine cut through the river mist—a capacitor charging.

"Down!" Toby lunged, shoving Jax backward into the mouth of the corrugated pipe.

A blinding, seven-million-candlepower halogen searchlight dropped out of the fog like a solid white pillar, striking Toby square in the chest.

The glare was total, absolute. It burned away the darkness, the culvert, the river, turning the world into a searing white plate. Behind the light, the rhythmic, metallic clatter of heavy tactical gear came vaulting down the steep stone embankment.

*"Subject identified at the waterline,"* an amplified voice barked, flat, professional, and deadened by the snow. *"Stay on your knees. Hands behind your neck. Do not enter the conduit or we deploy lethal interdiction."*

Toby sat back on his heels in the freezing mud, clutching one eighty-pound crate of formula against his ribs with an arm that had gone entirely dead. His vision was bleached white, but through the roar in his ears, he heard the frantic splash of water behind him in the pipe, and Marcus’s voice, raw and ruined, coming from the black mouth of the drain:

*"Toby!"*

A red laser dot dropped through the white glare, dancing erratically across the wet canvas over Toby’s heart.
