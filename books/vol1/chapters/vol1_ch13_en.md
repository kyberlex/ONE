The silence after the drowned generator didn’t bring relief; it brought the cold.

Within twenty minutes of the river water killing the contractor’s rail rig, the biting draft off Lake St. Clair found every missing pane and loose corrugated seam in the warehouse roof. The ambient heat from three hundred bodies huddled on shipping pallets rose toward the forty-foot iron rafters and vanished into the Detroit night.

In the curtained annex off Bay 4, Maya Lin-Mercado’s hands were raw. The lye soap had stripped the oils from her knuckles, leaving fine, stinging fissures that bled pink whenever she flexed her fingers. She dried them against the coarse hem of her canvas work jacket, watching the shallow rise and fall of the newborn twins nestled in a plastic produce crate lined with clean wool blankets. Four days old, born three weeks early to a twenty-year-old machinist’s daughter from Cavalry Street. Their mother was sleeping on an adjacent cot, her face gray with postpartum anemia.

Maya reached for the stainless-steel kettle on the single induction ring. The battery bank in the corner hummed with a thin, dying whine—a rhythmic, shallow flutter that she knew in her bones meant the plant had only hours of low-voltage life remaining.

She turned the brass spigot over the basin to draw four ounces for the dry powdered milk.

The tap gave a violent, metallic shudder.

Not a steady gush. A dry, rasping gasp—the bronchial wheeze of an iron lung fighting an empty tube. A single fat bubble of orange rust puffed out against the porcelain, followed by an angry suction hiss as the copper line pulled air backward. Then, silence.

Maya held her hand under the spout. Nothing.

She turned the cross-handle all the way to its stop. A hollow knock traveled through the wall, clattering down twenty feet of vertical riser into the foundation slab.

Across the room, the second twin stirred, let out a thin, mewling cry, and rootled against the wool.

Maya’s throat went dry. A pediatric nurse’s mind does not think in gallons or pipe diameters; it thinks in milliliters per hour, fontanelle tension, and the elasticity of pinched skin over a sunken ribcage. In freezing cold, an adult body burns moisture simply exhaling against the frost. A four-day-old infant in dry air dehydrates to irreversible renal shutdown in less than thirty-six hours.

She hurried down the dark corridor toward the tool crib.

Elena sat at a makeshift desk of two chemical drums and a slab of plywood, illuminated only by the cold blue wash of a laptop screen. Kira stood behind her, blowing into cupped hands, her nose red from the wind.

"The line is dry," Maya said without preamble. Her voice was flat, keeping the panic strictly behind her teeth.

Elena didn’t look surprised. She dragged two fingers across the trackpad, tilting the screen toward Maya. A municipal utility dashboard, patched through the local mesh before the commercial carrier had dropped, showed the southern transmission trunk along Dearborn Street. The telemetry trace was flatlined at zero.

"They closed the quarantine interlock at the rail junction at midnight," Elena said, her voice gravelly from two days without sleep. "The city board issued an emergency sanitary containment order. They're claiming the fire runoff from the switchyard contaminated the main."

"That’s a lie," Kira muttered, pulling her hood tighter around her ears. "The switchyard is three hundred yards down-gradient from the city main. Briggs pressured the water commissioners. They want to smoke us out without having to fire a single tear gas canister."

"It doesn’t matter why the wheel turned," Maya cut in, stepping between them and the screen. "We have three hundred people under this roof. Fourteen nursing infants. If the line is dry, what do we have in the basement?"

Elena closed the laptop halfway to conserve the display battery. "The gravity cistern beneath the boiler room. It holds eleven thousand liters when the floats are high. We haven't inspected the sediment trap since Tuesday."

"Eleven thousand liters," Maya whispered.

She did the mental triage instantly: three hundred people, strict survival ration of three liters per person per day for drinking and cooking alone—no sanitation, no washing, no heating runoff. Less than twelve days under ideal control. But panic doesn't calculate; panic hoards.

A sudden, sharp crash echoed from the central floor, followed by the scrape of dragging galvanized tubs and a man’s angry shout.

"It’s already started," Maya said.

She broke into a run, her work boots striking the concrete in quick, hard slaps.

In the dim sub-basement vestibule where the gravity feed dropped from the ceiling, sixty neighbors had already gathered in the dark, their faces pale in the sweep of three yellow work lights. Someone had dragged a five-gallon pickle barrel to the outflow manifold; two younger men from the casting line were wrestling over the brass wheel of the main valve with a rusted monkey wrench.

"Get your hands off it, Carlos!" a woman screamed, clutching two plastic milk jugs to her chest. "My kids haven't had clean tea since yesterday morning!"

"Back off, Sal! We’re filling the barrels first or nobody gets nothing when the bottom runs out!"

The steel wrench clanged against the iron housing. A child began to shriek in the dark. The crowd surged forward against the wet concrete curbing, shoulders slamming into ribs, desperation turning reasonable men and women into a panicked herd inside a concrete box.

Maya didn't scream. Shouting was just more wind in a burning room.

She vaulted onto the concrete pump pedestal beside the cistern wall, her boots locking onto the damp ledge four feet above the crowd. She caught Carlos by the padded collar of his car-coat with both hands, using his own momentum to pull him back from the manifold until his shoulders hit the brick pillar.

"Look at me, Carlos," she commanded. Her voice wasn't loud, but it carried the absolute, razor-sharp authority of a trauma room at 3:00 a.m.

The man froze, the heavy wrench slipping down in his grip. His pupils were blown wide with terror, his breath coming in white, ragged gasps.

"In through your nose," Maya said, her hand firm on his collar, her fingers digging through the coarse wool into his trapezius. "Three counts. Now."

Carlos stared at her, blinking against the halogen glare. His chest rose, shuddered, and fell.

"Look at the people behind you," Maya said, her gaze sweeping across the sixty upturned, frightened faces. "If you wrench that brass stem cold, you'll shear the packing gland off the seat. Then every drop we have drains into the sewer sump in ten minutes, and by tomorrow night, Carlos, I'm watching your niece choke on her own tongue from salt imbalance. Is that what you’re doing with that wrench?"

The basement fell dead silent, save for the wet drip of condensation from the overhead pipes.

Carlos lowered the tool. His hands were shaking.

Maya stepped down to the lower tier of the tank footing. "The city turned the curb stop at Dearborn Street. That was Briggs. He expects us to turn on each other down here in the dark over five-gallon buckets. That is how a siege works. It doesn't break iron gates; it breaks common decency."

She pointed to the copper manifold. "Article Two of the Covenant is in effect as of midnight. Biological primacy. No barrels. No hoarding. One liter per adult every twelve hours, issued at the clinic counter. Infant formula and the dialysis patients in Bay 2 take priority from the upper valve. Anyone who touches this gate without the sortition sign-off answers to the whole assembly."

She looked at Carlos. "Give me the wrench."

The machinist swallowed hard, looked down at his boots, and passed the heavy iron tool to her. Maya handed it immediately to Sal’s husband, an older retired trackman. "Stand watch on the valve. Two-hour rotations. If the stem so much as drips, you call Marcus."

The tension broke like a fever, the crowd exhaling in a low, collective murmur before slowly sorting themselves into a quiet, single-file line toward the ration spigot.

Footsteps splashed through the standing water behind the boiler room.

Marcus Hayes stepped into the yellow light, his left arm bound tight to his chest in a fresh canvas sling soaked through with black grease. Toby followed two paces back, carrying a double-ended pipe wrench and a heavy sealed lantern. Marcus’s face was drawn, his skin the color of wet river clay after fifty hours on his feet, but his eyes were hard.

"She’s right about the tank," Marcus said quietly, stopping beside Maya. "I checked the head pressure with Toby. We have maybe thirty-eight hours if nobody washes their hands. Forty-eight if we let the boiler jackets run bone-dry and freeze the north wall."

"We can't freeze the north wall," Maya said. "The infants will take pneumonia."

"Then we have forty hours," Toby said, his voice cracking slightly before he cleared his throat.

Marcus turned his head toward the dark archway at the far rear of the sub-basement—a low, forgotten tunnel that led toward the pre-war sump pits beneath the original 1920s casting floor. Water stood four inches deep back there, black and thick with sixty years of machine oil and pulverized slag.

"Come on," Marcus grunted, shifting the lantern to his good hand. "Bring the four-foot pry bar, Toby."

Maya followed them, her rubber boots sloshing through the freezing, greasy brine. "Marcus, what’s back here? The municipal main enters from the street."

"The city main came in 1954," Marcus muttered, his breath pluming thick in the damp air as he ducked beneath a low-hanging steam header. "Before the city ran the water lines down West Jefferson, this whole flat was swamp and salt marsh. The old brass foundry that stood here didn't pay the municipal board for a drop."

They reached the farthest corner of the sump beneath Bay 3. Here, the floor dropped another two feet into a brick-lined trench. Water swirled around their knees, black and frigid, smelling intensely of iron and deep sulfur.

Marcus held the halogen lamp high. The beam cut through the mist, illuminating a cracked concrete footing half-buried in river silt.

Toby knelt, plunging his bare arms into the freezing sludge without hesitation, dragging away rotted wooden timbers and chunks of spalled mortar. His fingers scraped against rusted metal.

"Here!" Toby breathed. "Marcus, it's here!"

Maya leaned over the curb, directing the lantern beam down into the trench.

Bolted flat to a five-foot square of solid, blue-gray limestone was a massive cast-iron blind flange, sixteen inches across, secured by eight heavy hexagonal studs encrusted in thick orange lime scale. Stamped into the curved perimeter of the iron cap, the crude foundry letters still showed through a century of corrosion:

*PENINSULAR FOUNDRY CO. — 1924 — ARTESIAN WELL NO. 4 — DEPTH 220 FT.*

Marcus reached down, his scarred fingers resting against the cold iron plate. A faint, low vibration hummed through the metal—not from the building, but from hundreds of feet below in the deep bedrock, where the subterranean pressure of the Great Lakes basin pushed silently against the ancient stone.

"There's an ocean of sweet water down there," Toby said, his eyes wide in the dark, looking up at Marcus with desperate hope. "We can drill the seal. We can plumb it right into the gravity line!"

Marcus didn't smile. He wiped a smear of iron rust from the flange, his face set like granite.

"It’s trapped beneath the salt cap under eighty pounds of artesian head," Marcus said, his voice dropping into the freezing damp. "If we tap this wrong with hand tools in the dark, we don't get drinking water, Maya. We blow an eight-inch geyser of pressurized sulfur brine that will drown this basement and cave the foundation of Bay 3 into the river in twenty minutes."

He raised his eyes to Maya, the yellow lantern reflecting in the dark hollows of his face.

"And Toby only has enough carbide bits to try it once."
