The cast-iron griddle on the floor of Bay Four smelled of scorched chicory. Maya Lin-Mercado kept her palm an inch above the metal, measuring its fading warmth against the draft cutting under the roller doors. Her mother had taught her that trick during the winter blackouts of ninety-nine, when the substation on Livernois blew and the neighborhood survived on kerosene wick-stoves and stubbornness: *you don’t touch the iron to know if the heat is dying; you listen to how the air shivers above it.*

The air was barely shivering now. The emergency circuit was dropping toward dead-cold, rationing its remaining watts to the thermal loop tucked around the central core.

Around her, seventy people lay huddled in clusters of three and four beneath quilted moving pads and layers of industrial felt stripped from the stamping line. Breath plumed white against the ceiling ribs. When someone turned over, the dry rustle of plastic drop cloths sounded like dry corn husks.

Beside the dead hydraulic press, Leo sat cross-legged, his knuckles jammed deep into the pockets of an oversized chore coat. His eyes were wide, tracking the blue status diode blinking on the mesh receiver every four seconds. He wasn't rocking anymore. The terror had calcified into a rigid, bird-like stillness.

"Marcus is still up there," Leo whispered. His voice was cracked, paper-thin.

"He's got the wool watch-coat," Maya said, though she knew the wool was useless against a sub-zero gale coming off thirty miles of open lake ice. "And he's got the spotlight. They won't come up the ladder while the light is burning."

Above them, through two floors of reinforced concrete, the low hum of the carbon arc lamp vibrated in the rebar. It was an erratic pulse. Marcus had been on the parapet for six hours, his boots frozen to the gravel roof deck, pinning Donald Briggs’s contractors in their own staging yard with three thousand watts of raw, blinding glare. But every few minutes, the light flickered as the wind sheared across the burner rods.

Outside, the idling diesels of the IronClad transports answered with a rhythmic, guttural purr.

It was 04:40. The deepest trough of the dark, before the false dawn turned the river into greasy slate.

Maya stood up, her knees popping with the sharp crack of cold cartilage. Her left hip ached from sleeping on corrugated cardboard—a dull, throbbing reminder of a warehouse pallet collapse six years ago that still forecasted every drop in atmospheric pressure. She pulled her scarf over her mouth and walked down the central aisle toward Gate Four.

Every window along the southern wall was coated in feathering frost, except where the heat of human bodies had melted peepholes through the grime. She pressed her forehead against the chilled glass.

Two hundred yards away, across the frozen mud of the Norfolk Southern rail spur, the flat-black breaching haulers sat lined up bumper to bumper. Their heavy steel push-bars were coated in rime. In the white spill of the rooftop searchlight, Maya could see the contractors moving behind the safety glass of the cabs—heavy men in charcoal parkas, checking tactical displays, sipping from insulated mugs, completely untouched by the freezing wind.

They weren't monsters. That was what made them terrifying. They were salaried specialists with second mortgages in Macomb County and children in parochial schools, sitting in heated cabs waiting for the dawn whistle so they could punch the clock and clear the floor.

To them, Bay Four was just an unpaid lien. A distressed asset full of squatters obstructing a municipal right-of-way.

Maya looked down at her hands. The skin between her knuckles was raw, bleeding in the dry chill. In her pocket, her fingers found the stub of a yellow lumber crayon she’d carried since the first night. She gripped it until the paraffin wax softened against her palm.

At 05:08, the diesel engines across the tracks changed pitch.

It wasn't a sudden revving. It was the deliberate, synchronized growl of five heavy transmissions dropping into low gear simultaneously. The headlights of the lead transport snapped on—twin blades of high-intensity halogen that cut through the river fog and struck the frosted windows of Gate Four.

Inside the bay, a child began to cry. A sharp, muffled wail that was immediately smothered by a mother’s coat.

"Down," someone hissed from the darkness behind the press line. "Get behind the bolster plates!"

Maya didn't move. She watched the lead transport crawl forward, its massive off-road tires crunching through the crusted ballast stone. Fifty feet behind it, two utility trucks followed, their beds carrying industrial diamond-wire saws and heavy hydraulic spreaders designed to peel the corrugated siding off the steel frame like tin foil.

Then, from the north, came the sound.

It was not an engine.

It was too low for a motor, too wide for a siren. It came rolling down the corridor of West Jefferson Avenue like the dull, percussive vibration of an approaching freight train, but without the metallic shriek of steel on rail.

Maya pressed her ear to the cold glass.

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

It was the wet, heavy slap of rubber-soled work boots striking salted asphalt in unison. Thousands of them.

Through the fog rolling off the Rouge River basin, a pair of headlights appeared at the intersection of Dearborn Street. Then another pair. Then a double row of amber clearance markers on the roof of a municipal utility truck. Behind them came the high, yellow beams of rusted pickups, battered domestic sedans, flatbed haulers from the independent scrap yards, and three yellow school buses with their hazard lights pulsing in rhythmic syncopation.

The vehicles were moving at five miles an hour, not driving, but pacing.

Surrounding them, filling the four-lane width of West Jefferson from curb to curb, was an ocean of high-visibility lime and orange.

Autoworkers in grease-stained Carhartt coats with local union numbers stenciled on the shoulders. Third-shift nurses from Detroit Receiving still wearing teal scrubs beneath open parkas. Women from the neighborhood bakeries on Vernor Highway pulling red plastic wagons loaded with silver soup urns and cardboard flats of coffee. High school teachers in knit caps, retired steelworkers leaning on aluminum canes, young men from the tire shops down by the slip with salt-caked work gloves tucked under their arms.

They did not have weapons. They did not have signs.

They walked with the heavy, unhurried gait of people who had spent their entire lives walking through slush to punch an iron timecard at six in the morning.

"Maya?"

She turned. Marcus was standing at the bottom of the fire stairs.

He looked hollowed out. His face had the waxy, grayish-blue tint of second-stage hypothermia, his lips nearly purple where the river wind had chapped them raw. His knuckles were locked into rigid claws, frozen in the shape of the searchlight’s brass handles. He swayed where he stood, catching himself against the flanged iron stringer of the staircase.

Leo was already there, throwing his small frame beneath Marcus's shoulder to keep him upright.

"Marcus," Maya breathed, hurrying to them. She stripped off her heavy wool outer scarf and wound it around his neck, smelling the sharp ozone and burnt carbon clinging to his coat. "You came down."

"The lamp... the carbons burned out," Marcus stammered, his teeth chattering so violently his jaw clicked like a loose relay. "Five minutes ago. I had no more rods. I thought..." He looked toward the rattling glass of the bay doors. "What is that?"

"Look," Maya said.

She caught him by the elbow, guiding his staggering weight to the nearest clear pane.

Across the ditch, Donald Briggs’s lead breaching vehicle had ground to a halt. Its hydraulic ram was lowered within twenty yards of Gate Four, but its tracks were completely blocked.

A battered Ford F-250, its bed piled high with rolls of pink fiberglass insulation, had rolled calmly across the spur and stalled directly across the contractor’s turning radius. A man with gray mutton-chops and a stained ballcap stepped out of the cab, set his emergency brake with a loud, ratcheting clatter, and deliberately popped the hood.

Before the contractor in the lead cab could hit his air horn, the crowd closed around them.

They didn't charge. They didn't shout slogans. They simply filled the physical space. Three hundred people, then eight hundred, then two thousand, until West Jefferson, the railway siding, and the open gravel staging yard were choked with bodies.

They linked arms, four deep, facing the black bumper of the breaching truck.

Maya saw Briggs jump down from the running board of his command vehicle. His charcoal coat flapped open in the freezing slipstream. He pulled his tactical radio to his face, his jaw working furiously as he barked orders, but the contractors around him weren't advancing. They were standing on their running boards, looking out over a sea of five thousand of their own neighbors, their own second cousins, the men who played in their Friday bowling leagues, the women who drew their blood at the clinic.

A woman in a neon-yellow vest walked straight up to the grill of Briggs's command truck, set a two-gallon stainless thermos on the front bumper, and turned her back to the driver to hand paper cups of steaming broth into the crowd.

Inside the bay, the silence was absolute.

Marcus leaned his forehead against the cold pane, his breath fogging the glass, a slow, ragged sob catching in his throat. His shivering didn't stop, but the violent trembling smoothed out as three dozen hands from the dark floor of the plant reached out to touch him—patting his back, wrapping him in blankets, steadying his knees.

Through the eastern windows, over the flat expanse of the Detroit River, the sky cracked.

It wasn't sunlight yet. It was a thin, cold blade of pale lemon cutting under the cloud deck, turning the industrial smog into an iridescent mist. Day Six had arrived. The Monolith was still standing, unbreached, surrounded not by an army, but by the city itself.

Then, from the far side of the rail line, behind the wall of high-visibility coats, the long horn of a Norfolk Southern switcher engine echoed across the marsh.

Briggs spun around.

Down the main freight line, moving under a green signal that should not have cleared for another four hours, a half-mile of dead-slow unit cars came rolling into view—hauling two thousand tons of structural concrete ties, and cutting the contractors' retreat line completely in half.
