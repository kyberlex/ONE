The concrete floor of Bay Two shivered through Marcus’s work boots before the sound registered. It was not the hollow shudder of the turbine intake or the sharp slap of freezing lake gales against the rolling shutter. It was the low-frequency churn of planetary gears—twin turbo-diesels grinding down West Jefferson under brutal throttle.

"Clear the lane!" Marcus bellowed, his voice raw from smoke and sub-zero air. He lunged forward, catching a woman by the padded sleeve of her coat as she tripped on the icy lip of the rail groove, swinging her bodily into the warm wash of the staging bay. Behind her, three dozen stragglers from the Rouge tenements stumbled through the open threshold of Gate One, coughing in the sulfurous updraft, frost clinging to their scarves.

Up on the gantry crane runway, forty feet above the gravel siding, Kira Vance didn't shout. She leaned out over the safety railing, her windbreaker snapping in the blizzard like a cracked whip. The red tally light of the live-feed camera array reflected in the curve of her safety goggles. Through the blinding curtain of horizontal snow, two massive silhouettes crawled out of the darkness beyond the rail cutoff: sixty-ton earthmovers, stripped of corporate decals, their operator cabs welded into crude steel pillboxes with narrow slit vision ports. Angle-iron cowcatchers and heavy ripper tines had been grafted to their noses, churning the packed ice of the avenue into gray sludge.

The federal marshals in their BearCats across the street had pulled back fifty yards, flashing warning beacons but keeping their distance. The contractor bulldozers had no flashing lights. They had no sirens. They were advancing in parallel directly toward the open rolling steel shutter of Gate One, moving at eight miles an hour to crush the entryway before the human corridor could fully clear.

"They aren't trying to gas us," Kira's voice cut through Marcus’s collar radio, brittle and flat. "They’re going to cave the lintel and drop the masonry on the threshold. Seal us in with the cold."

"Toby!" Marcus yelled, waving the boy back from the threshold. "Get those blast jerseys between the families and the curb! Don't let them cluster at the threshold!"

Toby didn't freeze. The hesitation that had haunted the boy when the sortition council began had vanished into the biting wind. He grabbed a five-foot hexagonal pry bar, levering an industrial steel hand truck loaded with sandbags across the pedestrian gap, funneling the last cluster of shivering mothers and elders through the side fire-door into the insulated shelter of the machine shop. "Go! Inside! Past the green line!" Toby’s voice broke on the cold air, but his feet stayed planted on the frost-heaved tarmac, guiding the tail end of the crowd past the concrete blast barrier.

Marcus turned his back to the blinding headlights of the lead dozer, sprinting toward the siding spur that ran parallel to the plant's south face. There, rusted to the flanged rails under two inches of glazed ice, sat an uncoupled Norfolk Southern hopper car—forty tons of thick cor-ten steel, long retired from hauling foundry coke.

"Kira!" Marcus shouted upward against the howling draft. "The brake shoes!"

"Already on the catwalk!" she yelled back.

She had dropped down the vertical access ladder from the gantry in five reckless bounds, boots ringing against the rusted rungs, landing in the snow beside the hopper’s rear bogie. During her years auditing pipeline manifolds in the Dakotas, Kira had developed a peculiar, obsessive habit under crisis: she stopped looking at the threat and focused entirely on the fasteners holding the physical world together. She pulled a thirty-inch pipe wrench from her tool harness, setting the hardened jaw against the manual brake wheel’s dog-latch.

"Pawl is rusted solid!" she grunted, her knuckles going dead-white through her goatskin gloves as she threw her weight against the rusted pawl. "Ice in the ratchet ring!"

Marcus hit the rail beside her, driving the wedge tip of his pry bar directly under the ratchet dog. "Together! On three! One—two—three—"

They slammed their weight downward. Steel shrieked against steel with the crack of a rifle shot. The ice shattered out of the gear teeth in a spray of gray crystals. The brake wheel whipped counterclockwise, spinning so violently the iron spokes blurred in the snow glare, releasing forty tons of dead weight onto the four-percent grade descending toward the plant gates.

"Pry bars! Under the wheel treads!" Marcus ordered.

Toby hit the south rail, Marcus the north. They jammed the hardened steel bars beneath the rear wheel flanges, using the track ties as fulcrums, heaving until the tendons in Marcus’s neck threatened to burst. The hopper groaned. A skin of orange rust flaked from the leaf springs. Then, with a sickening, grinding shudder, gravity seized the mass.

The iron hopper rolled.

It picked up speed down the spur track, cutting across the frontage apron just as the lead bulldozer surged over the curb forty yards out. The machine’s heavy tracks were throwing clods of frozen gravel and crushed asphalt into the night air, its diesel stacks belching black exhaust against the glare of the plant lights. The dozer driver, peering through his reinforced slit, saw the iron wall rolling across his vector and slammed his left steering brake to avoid a broadside collision.

The hopper car jumped the spur derailer with an ear-splitting bang, its front trucks burying themselves axle-deep into the frozen ballast, slewing forty tons of iron plate squarely across the main vehicle apron.

It formed an impassable steel redoubt. The path to the pedestrian threshold was blocked.

The lead bulldozer veered hard to the right, its steel tracks tearing up the asphalt as it angled into the narrow fifteen-foot corridor between the derailed hopper and the plant’s brick retaining wall. Its massive, forward-canted blade scraped along the corrugated siding of the hopper, throwing a rooster-tail of orange friction sparks that lit the blizzard like flares. It was squeezing through, determined to ram the exposed corner of Gate One’s structural pier.

"Marcus!" Kira cried out from the gantry landing overhead. She had scrambled back up to the intermediate hoist deck, her breath coming in ragged, white gasps. "The hoist rig! Catch the pigtail!"

Above the corridor, suspended from the overhead crane’s auxiliary trolley, hung a coiled spool of two-inch, six-strand braided steel wire rope—remnants of the heavy rigging used during the fall stator replacement, thick as a man’s forearm and coiled like sleeping vipers on a wooden pallet.

Marcus didn't ask questions. He understood mechanical friction the way an offshore sailor understood knots. He scrambled onto the sill of the hopper car, reached up, and grabbed the dangling hemp tag line Kira had tied to the spool's safety pin.

"Drop it!" Marcus yelled.

Kira kicked the release dog on the gantry winch.

The wooden pallet tipped. Three hundred feet of stiff, grease-caked, two-inch steel wire rope cascaded down through the falling snow, tumbling directly into the narrow trough of track bed between the hopper and the brick footing—straight into the path of the advancing bulldozer.

The contractor at the controls had no chance to brake. The heavy machine’s right track rode directly over the coiled belly of the braided steel cable.

What happened next was not an explosion, but the inescapable mathematics of raw mechanics.

As the bulldozer’s rear drive sprocket turned, its hardened manganese steel teeth hooked the loose bight of the cable. Instead of snapping, the two-inch wire rope was drawn violently upward into the track guides, whipping through the return rollers like an iron python. In three engine revolutions, the thick braided cable wrapped itself tight around the drive hub, binding the sprocket against the chassis armor.

The diesel engine screamed, torque converters straining against an immovable load. Thick gray smoke erupted from the machine’s belly vents.

Then came the mechanical catastrophe. The braided cable caught the front idler pin, tightening until there was zero clearance left in the track assembly. With an explosive *thwack* that rattled the teeth in Marcus’s jaw, the track pins sheared under twenty tons of lateral tension. The bulldozer’s left track slithered off its drive rollers like a dead iron centipede, piling up in an accordion heap of dead links ten yards short of the gate.

Deprived of one track, the machine spun violently on its belly, its armored blade biting deep into the concrete foundation of the siding bumper, the high-pressure hydraulic lines bursting with an aerosol hiss of hot mineral oil. The diesel engine choked, shuddered against the locked transmission, and died in a violent wheeze of stalling pistons.

Behind it, fifty yards back on the avenue, the second bulldozer locked its brakes, skidding sideways across the icy asphalt as its driver saw the lead machine tangled and dead in the mechanical snare.

Silence fell over the threshold—sudden, vast, and freezing, broken only by the ticking of the dying diesel manifold and the steady, rhythmic roar of the radiant blowers keeping the families warm inside the bay.

From the slit of the disabled bulldozer, a green signal flare hissed out onto the snow, sputtering uselessly in the slush. Beyond the rail cordon, the tactical BearCats shut down their high beams, turning their front wheels away from West Jefferson toward the federal staging post at the bridge.

Marcus stood on the hopper sill, his pry bar resting across his boots, his chest heaving steam into the sub-zero wind.

Above him on the gantry, Kira lowered her hands from the hoist lever, her goggles fogged, looking down through the snow at the unbroken steel threshold.

Inside Bay Two, the door clattered shut. The bolt dropped into place.

Then, from the darkness of the river channel to the south, beneath the frozen shadow of the turning basin, a low, wet horn echoed twice across the black water.
