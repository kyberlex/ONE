The rhythmic shudder in the concrete slab rose through the soles of Marcus’s work boots, vibrating straight into his ruined left knee. It wasn’t the wind tearing off Lake St. Clair, and it wasn’t the Conrail switchers grinding their teeth against the siding two miles north. It was deep, wet, and heavy—the dull, sickening churn of something dense boring through river mud and river silt, grinding against the limestone pilings far beneath the plant.

Marcus leaned his shoulder hard against an iron stanchion, letting the cold metal bite through his flannel shirt to numb the ache. His arm, wrapped tight in torn bedsheet and electrical tape, pulsed with every heartbeat. 

Across the bay, the light of Day Three had crept through the soot-stained fiberglass panels. It wasn’t real daylight; it was the color of lead solder, flat and merciless, carving long shadows across the grease-blackened floor.

Beneath that dead light, two women knelt over a steel workbench, locked in absolute, unbroken silence.

The council’s decree hung between them like an iron curtain. Maya and Kira had not exchanged a single syllable since Elena had snapped the grease pencil before dawn. Neither had cried out, neither had pleaded, and neither had broken. When Kira picked up the socket wrench, her jaw was clamped so tight the tendon stood out like wire under her bruised skin. When Maya stepped in to brace the heavy aluminum manifold, her breath puffed white in the draft, steady, shallow, and entirely controlled.

Between them sat the clinic’s pediatric oxygen concentrator. The machine was an old beast, salvaged from a shuttered parish infirmary in Delray—its outer casing scarred by years of damp basements, its internal diaphragm cracked and weeping air with a thin, wheezing sigh every time the motor kicked. In the corner by the cold forge, Toby lay wrapped in three layers of furniture pads, his breathing shallow but clear, guarded by Rosa. The infant Leo was asleep against Rosa’s collarbone, quiet for the first time in eighteen hours, his skin still hot to the touch. But the boy wouldn’t last the morning if the concentrator couldn't hold its seal.

Marcus watched from six paces away. As the shop’s custodian, every instinct in his body screamed at him to step in, take the tool from Kira’s stiff, bloodied fingers, and seat the valve himself. But he held his place. To touch the bench would be an intrusion; to speak would shatter the brittle, terrifying peace that held the assembly together.

Maya tapped twice on the bench with the flat of her thumb. 

Kira didn’t look up at her face. Her eyes remained locked on the copper tubing. At the second tap, Kira’s left hand shot out, catching the brass flange before it could tilt and strip the threads. Maya slid a freshly cut neoprene ring over the intake port with her other hand, her fingers blackened with graphite lubricant. Their knuckles grazed. For a fraction of a second, Kira’s hand flinched, a soldier’s somatic twitch to incoming contact, but she did not pull away. She bore down, holding the collar true while Maya took the hex key and began to turn.

Quarter turn. Pause. Kira checked the clearance with her thumbnail, feeling for the microscopic ridge where metal met synthetic rubber. A single nod from Kira. Maya gave it another eighth of a turn.

The choreography was agonizing in its precision. Deprived of words, they had stripped their communication down to breath, pressure, and metal. They were two women who four hours earlier had been inches from tearing each other’s throats out on the gravel, now bound together by the terrifying fragility of an eight-month-old child’s lungs.

Marcus rubbed his thumb over the scarred handle of his folding knife. The quiet reminded him of the winter of seventy-nine, during the great shutdown, when the furnace in his and Sarah’s first flat on Junction had cracked wide open in January. They hadn’t had sixty dollars between them for parts, let alone a plumber. Sarah hadn’t spoken for three days—not out of anger, but out of the sheer, marrow-deep terror of what poverty did to young couples when the frost started crawling up the bedroom wallpaper. They had worked together on their bellies in the crawlspace, passing the heavy pipe wrench back and forth by touch in the dark, their coats brushing, their breaths freezing against the joists, working until their fingers bled and the pilot light finally caught. 

You didn't need love to survive together in the cold. You only needed the shared dread of what happened if your hands slipped.

A sharp, metallic *ping* broke the silence of the shop floor.

Maya froze. Her shoulder hitched. 

The brass intake seat had seated too fast, biting into the new gasket at an angle. A hairline fissure, no wider than a horsehair, ran across the vulcanized edge. The hiss of escaping air resumed—soft, petulant, and fatal.

Kira didn’t swear. She closed her eyes for a count of three, her chest rising against her tactical vest. When she opened them, she didn’t glare at Maya. Instead, she reached into her breast pocket, pulled out a small flat tin of jeweler’s abrasive compound, and laid it between them. 

Maya looked at the tin, then at Kira’s hand. 

Kira turned the palm of her hand upward, showing the rough, callused pad of her index finger. She dipped it into the paste, reached directly into the valve seat, and began to lap the metal by hand, grinding out the burr with tiny, circular strokes. Maya watched the rhythm of Kira’s wrist, then reached out, took the tube of setting compound, and began buttering the opposite side of the fitting, matching Kira’s circular stroke in counter-time. 

Their forearms moved in tandem, inches apart, brushing against the rough gray wool of their sleeves. No apologies. No recriminations. Just the dull, steady rasp of carborundum grit grinding down an imperfection.

Beyond the brick walls, the sleet had turned to freezing rain, rattling like birdshot against the high tin roof. The drone from beneath the river channel had faded twenty minutes ago, sinking back into the deep limestone bedrock, leaving behind only the steady hum of the plant’s base batteries.

Then, from the north catwalk, a sharp double-click cut through the rain.

It was the low, dry rattle of an empty shell casing dropped through an iron grate—the lookout's quiet signal.

Marcus turned his head, wincing as his neck muscles caught. Up on the observation perch above the rail crane, one of the neighborhood youths was leaning flat against the corrugated siding, his arm extended downward, pointing through a gap in the blast doors toward the Conrail spur.

Marcus limped toward the rolling fire door, his left knee dragging slightly on the grit. He slid his face into the narrow observation slit where an old blast louvre had rusted open.

The gray expanse of the rail yard was desolate, crisscrossed by tracks glazed with black ice. But three hundred yards out, where the spur intersected the perimeter fence, an armored tactical hauler had rolled to a dead stop across the switch points. 

It wasn’t an assault team. No men in riot gear were dismounting into the freezing rain. No steel rams.

Instead, the rear bed of the heavy hauler was open to the sky. Mounted on an articulated steel boom was a flat, matte-black hexagonal panel, six feet across, its surface entirely featureless except for a tight matrix of ceramic cooling fins along its rim. Three technicians in high-visibility foul-weather gear were securing heavy ground anchors into the ballast with hydraulic impact drivers, working with the calm, methodical speed of men laying down a survey line.

Marcus frowned, his pulse picking up a jagged rhythm. He had spent forty years around heavy industry, from arc furnaces to induction welders, and he knew the shape of power when it wasn’t meant to throw lead.

A fat umbilical cable snaked from the trailer bed into a trailered turbine generator, which began to spit a thin, blue-gray haze of exhaust into the sleet.

The black panel rose on its hydraulic ram, tilting upward at a thirty-degree angle.

It wasn’t aimed at the blast doors. It wasn’t aimed at the windows.

It was tilted directly toward the roofline—pointing straight at the delicate laser transceiver perched on the brick parapet, the tiny glass eye that held the encrypted pulse across the river to Windsor.

A strange, dry heat suddenly crawled across the skin of Marcus’s face, like standing too close to a toaster before the coils turned red. Inside the bay, thirty feet behind him, the old mercury-vapor lamps along the ceiling began to buzz with a vicious, high-pitched whine, and the thin frost coating the iron door frame began, impossibly, to boil.
