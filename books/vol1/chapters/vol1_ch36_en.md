The frost came off the Detroit River not as mist, but as airborne needles that stuck to the corrugated steel of Gate Four and turned the razor wire into white coral.

At eight below zero, Marcus Hayes could feel the cold deep in the marrow of his left hip, right where an off-track freight dolly had pinned him against a loading dock two decades ago. Every sudden plunge in barometric pressure woke that old injury like a rusted hinge grating in his pelvis. He rubbed the stiff joint through his insulated canvas overalls, listening to the wind scour the riverfront. It groaned through the empty boxcars on the Zug Island spur, carrying the sulfurous stench of slag heaps and the oily tang of winter bilge.

Then the light hit the gravel at his feet.

It was tiny. A single crimson bead, no larger than an elderberry, quivering against the frozen grit of the rooftop parapet.

Marcus stopped mid-stride.

Two seconds later, a second red dot bloomed on the rusted chimney cowling five yards to his left. Then a third, dancing over the edge of the copper flashing above the foundry floor. They flickered through the freezing river haze—sharp, coherent lines of ruby light stretching across the black expanse of the shipping channel from the dark silhouettes of the Zug Island coke ovens.

Snipers.

"Marcus." Kira’s voice crackled through the short-range radio pinned to his collar, stripped of its usual razor-edged confidence. "Get off the roofline. Now."

Marcus didn't run. Running on a roof coated in river rime was how you went over the sixty-foot drop into the rail pit. He dropped into a low crouch behind the cinderblock elevator penthouse, the frozen brick biting through his heavy gloves.

"How many?" he muttered into the mic, his breath pluming thick and white in the shadows.

"Four rifles confirmed on the high coal conveyor across the channel," Kira answered, her breathing shallow over the channel. "Two more on the ballast dock. IronClad brought out heavy long-range chassis. High-caliber, suppressed. And Marcus—the federal marshal transports left twenty minutes ago. Campbell’s people are gone. There’s no legal buffer out there. It’s pure mercenary staging."

Marcus clamped his back teeth together until his jaw ached. He knew the type Briggs hired. They weren't municipal cops worried about internal affairs reviews or pension boards. They were corporate perimeter contractors paid by the day through offshore shell accounts, men who measured their liability entirely by whether their employers had enough capital to bury the reports.

He slid down the access ladder through the penthouse scuttle, his steel-toed boots clanging softly against the cold rungs. Inside the upper mezzanine, the air smelled of ozone, woodsmoke, and damp wool.

Toby was already at the foot of the ladder, clutching a three-foot length of galvanized pipe like a club. The skin around the boy’s knuckles was raw and split from hauling sandbags, his eyes wide and glassy with adrenaline. Beside him stood two third-shift tool-and-die hands from the neighborhood, their faces hollowed out by five straight days of sleep deprivation. One of them held an industrial flare gun; the other was wrapping a heavy chain around his forearm.

"They're painting the roof," Toby breathed, stepping forward, his voice cracking with panicked defiance. "I saw the beams through the skylight. Let me take the south fire escape. We’ve got four gallons of kerosene left in the shear shop. If they try to bridge the intake ditch—"

"You put that pipe down," Marcus said. His voice wasn’t loud, but it had the weight of dropped pig iron.

Toby flinched. "Marcus, they’re setting up to shoot us!"

"They’re setting up to clear a line of sight," Marcus corrected, his eyes fixing on the boy with unyielding hardness. "A man with a rifle on a coal tower wants you to do something stupid with a pipe or a bottle of fuel so his commander can call it armed resistance on the police log. You run out there with kerosene, you give Briggs the only thing he doesn't have: an excuse."

"We can't just stand here and wait for them to kick the doors in!"

"Nobody is waiting," Marcus said. He turned to the toolmaker with the chain. "Unwrap that. Go down to the boiler vault. Tell Elena to double-check the auxiliary capacitor banks. If the line drops when they hit the rail spur, I want the optical downlink running on local reserve before the contactors finish opening."

The toolmaker hesitated, then saw the steady, immovable cold in Marcus’s eyes. He nodded once, unspooling the links from his arm with a dull metallic rattle, and vanished down the iron stairs.

Marcus walked past Toby to the salvage bench along the mezzanine wall. Resting on two wooden blocks was an old commercial marine searchlight—a brass-jacketed quartz beast salvaged from a decommissioned lake freighter three summers back. Its thick convex lens was scratched, its reflector pitted with salt bloom, but Marcus had spent four hours two nights ago rewiring its transformer to take raw direct current from the node’s high-discharge cells.

He checked the twin copper leads. Solid. He flipped the heavy knife switch on the battery carriage beneath the bench; the filament gave a low, subsonic hum that vibrated in the soles of his boots.

"What are you doing with that?" Toby whispered.

"Briggs operates in the dark because shadows keep his contractors out of the press," Marcus said. He gripped the steel handles of the searchlight carriage. It weighed eighty pounds, dead ballast that pulled at his ruined hip, but he lifted it with a grunt, settling the weight against his chest. "Take the other end of the cable drum. Feed me slack. Don't let it snag on the scuttle hatch."

"Marcus—the snipers—"

"Feed the slack, Toby."

Marcus climbed back toward the cold. Every rung sent a spike of white heat through his pelvic joint, but he climbed with rhythmic, mechanical stubbornness. When his head broke the plane of the roof scuttle, the wind hit him like a frozen slab. The river was a sheet of churning black glass below, choked with grey slush drifting toward Lake Erie.

Across the channel, on the rusted superstructure of Zug Island, the dark was absolute. Briggs had cut the perimeter floodlights on the industrial spur. In the gloom, only the faint diesel thrum of idling armored personnel carriers betrayed their positions.

And the lasers.

The moment Marcus hauled the brass searchlight onto the parapet coping, three crimson needles snapped onto his body.

One burned dead center on the heavy canvas over his sternum. Another touched his throat, painting the coarse gray stubble of his neck with an electric red spark. The third hovered an inch below his left eye, casting a blood-colored glare across his cheekbone.

Marcus did not drop. He did not duck behind the parapet.

He felt the instinctive, animal terror of a man looking down the invisible barrels of scoped rifles across two hundred yards of open water. His throat went dry as lime. A tremor started in his knees—not from fear, but from the brutal minus-eight wind chewing through his wet layers. He bit the inside of his cheek until he tasted copper, grounding himself against the pain.

Through the short-range link, Kira’s voice was a choked rasp. "Marcus... they've taken position behind the concrete ballast wall. They have clean shot lines. Back down. Please."

"Hold the stream open," Marcus spoke into the collar mic. His voice was entirely level now, scrubbed of rage, scrubbed of hurry. "Is the optical link mirroring to the international nodes?"

"Shenzhen and the Andean relays have the roof feed live," Kira whispered. "Over eight hundred external monitors recording every frame. But Marcus, a live feed won't stop a five-hundred-grain bullet."

"It stops Briggs," Marcus said. "Because Donald Briggs knows how to count."

Marcus reached down with his right hand. He found the heavy toggle switch on the base of the freighter searchlight.

He didn't make a speech. He didn't preach about the covenant, or the union, or the cold concrete under his boots.

He threw the switch.

A blinding, violet-white spike of five thousand watts ripped through the black river fog.

The beam struck the high coal conveyor on Zug Island like a physical hammer. It tore through the industrial murk, vaporizing the shadows, illuminating the rusted rivets, the salt-crusted corrugated siding, and the two crouched figures in black tactical parkas behind a scoped sniper chassis on the gantry. The contractors recoiled instinctively, throwing their gloved forearms across their night-vision optics as the sudden, blistering glare burned out their tubes.

Marcus swung the brass housing smoothly on its swivel.

The beam swept down to the ballast dock. It caught an armored breaching vehicle—unmarked, flat-black, fitted with heavy steel ramming bars—and pinned the driver's cab in brilliant, undeniable clarity. Inside, Marcus could clearly see the corporate patches on the contractors' sleeves, the numbers stenciled on their helmets, and the tactical radio operator freezing in the sudden exposure of noon-day light.

The red dots on Marcus's chest wavered. They trembled against his coat, dancing erratically as the snipers across the water blinked against the white bloom of the arc-light, their scopes overwhelmed by the backscatter through the freezing mist.

On the roof of the Monolith, standing exposed against the black Detroit sky, Marcus Hayes gripped the hot brass handles of the spotlight. The wind screamed off the river, tearing at his hair, freezing the tears in the corners of his eyes, but his gaze remained locked across the water.

Far down on the Zug Island rail spur, the passenger door of a black command truck opened.

A man in a heavy charcoal field jacket stepped out onto the frozen ballast. Even across two hundred yards of ice-choked water, Marcus recognized the square, brutal carriage of Donald Briggs. The commander looked up into the piercing glare of the searchlight, his face illuminated in harsh, pale relief.

Briggs did not draw a weapon. He simply stood beside his vehicle, watching the lone man on the parapet. Then he lifted a radio to his mouth, spoke a single, short sentence into the wind, and looked down at his watch.

Beneath Marcus's boots, the deep structural steel of the stamping plant vibrated as the city bells across Delray began to toll.

Twelve slow, heavy chimes rolled over the frozen mud of the riverfront.

Midnight.

Day Five was dead. Across the water, the diesel engines of the breaching vehicles revved into a synchronized, guttural roar, preparing for the dawn. Marcus did not move. He kept the blinding white beam pinned straight to Briggs's chest, and waited.
