The gate held, but the river did not forgive.

Marcus stepped back from the chain-link fence as Raymond Miller’s cruisers formed a wedge of flashing amber and blue across the slush of West Jefferson. The wind off the Rouge was turning mean, sleet rattling against the corrugated siding of Bay 2 like handfuls of buckshot. Inside, the shop smelled of old kerosene, damp concrete, and the sharp, hot-garlic tang of sulfurized cutting oil.

Every bone in Marcus’s back throbbed with forty-nine hours of standing watch. His knuckles were raw, swollen stiff in the damp cold, and behind his eyes sat a dull, throbbing pressure—not just exhaustion, but the phantom burn of Elena’s tablet. That doctored soil report from Meridian Crest had sat in some executive’s climate-controlled safe for six years while the sulfur drifted south into the Delray porches, while Sarah’s lungs slowly hardened into chalk.

"Marcus!" Toby shouted over the mechanical shriek echoing from the far corner of the bay. "The feed rate's dragging! It’s dropping RPM!"

Marcus jogged down the oil-stained aisle between the workbenches. Bay 2 was running hot. On the primary bed, bolted down under sodium halogen floodlights, the massive five-axis milling housing was screaming through an emergency batch of articulating socket sleeves. Delray’s third ward had three diabetic elders whose prosthetics had snapped during the power drop, their plastic joints shattered by the sub-zero chill. Without replacement couplings, they couldn't move to the heated commons.

The spindle was turning at twelve thousand revolutions a minute, carbide teeth biting into high-density polymer and aircraft scrap. But the sound was wrong. Instead of the clean, rhythmic hiss of sharp flutes shearing alloy, the machine was producing a dry, chattering squeal that vibrated right through the soles of Marcus’s work boots.

"Look at the manifold," Toby panted. 

The younger man was trembling, soaked in sweat beneath a greasy canvas vest. A pencil was tucked behind his ear, bitten down to the eraser. He’d spent three years dodging truancy officers and running hot-wire patches for basement hydroponics in southwest Detroit, but right now his pupils were huge with terror. His eyes kept darting toward the frosted skylights, where the blue flashes of Miller’s squad cars washed across the webbed girders.

"Coolant pressure is bottoming out," Toby said, voice cracking. "The sump pump’s pulling air."

"Filter’s choked," Marcus said, laying a bare palm against the cast-iron headstock. The vibration was erratic, a sick, stumbling hitch in the bearings. Heat was radiating off the casing in waves, warping the smell of the shop with the sweet, sickening odor of frying mineral oil. "We’re running recycled coolant from the wash pits. The swarf from the aluminum batch wasn't fully separated. Metal fines are clogging the brass intake screen."

"If the spindle seizes, the run is dead," Toby said. He stepped toward the back of the enclosure, grabbing a pair of slip-joint pliers from the rolling cart. "I can pull the intake fitting from behind the secondary loop. I can blow it out with the shop air."

"Don't touch that valve," Marcus warned, his voice low and sharp. "The return line is sealed under thermal backpressure. You pop that brass fitting while the spindle’s turning, and the reservoir will flash-boil."

A violent *bang* rattled inside the shroud. The digital readout on the controller dropped into the amber warning band. A plume of white vapor hissed from the bronze bushing at the spindle's throat.

"It’s going to weld itself!" Toby yelled. The siren from outside—the deep, rhythmic horn of a federal transport clearing the rail crossing—echoed through the metal walls, amplifying the boy’s panic. "Marcus, we lose this spindle, we lose the whole bay! They'll shut the power down and say we were never code-compliant! They’ll take the shop!"

Before Marcus could grab his shoulder, Toby scrambled onto the scrap pallet behind the mill. He didn't have leather on. He had bare hands, dirt caked under his fingernails, his sleeves pushed up to the elbows. He jammed the pliers onto the hex nut of the pressurized brass return line, leaning his full body weight into the turn.

"Toby, no!"

The brass hex gave with a sickening *crack*.

A shrieking blast of pressurized, superheated steam and atomized cutting oil erupted from the split fitting. The vapor flashed instantly in the cold air, white-hot and smelling of scorched petroleum.

Toby froze, stunned by the deafening roar, his eyes wide in the blinding cloud as the jet hissed directly toward his face and bare throat.

Marcus lunged.

His boots slipped on the oil-slicked concrete, but he drove his shoulder into Toby’s ribs, throwing his full weight forward. The boy went flying sideways, crashing hard into a stack of empty plastic pallets. 

Marcus took the impact on his feet, his left hand reaching blindly into the blinding fog of steam to catch the emergency manual relief lever.

The jet caught him.

A column of scalding vapor slammed across his left forearm and wrist. Pain tore through his skin like raw fire, sharp and electric, stripping the breath from his throat. The heat penetrated straight through his flannel sleeve, searing flesh against wet cloth. Marcus clenched his teeth until his jaw joints popped, refusing to let his hand retreat. He felt the iron lever with his blistering fingers, found the cotter stop, and jerked it down with all his remaining strength.

The mechanical relief dumped.

With a gut-shaking *thud*, five gallons of superheated fluid bypassed directly into the gravel floor pit beneath the slab. The screaming pitch died. The spindle shuddered, its speed dying down to a slow, hollow whine before coming to a dead stop.

The bay went abruptly silent, save for the wet hiss of water droplets falling onto hot steel and the muffled drum of sleet on the roof.

Marcus leaned against the heavy cast-iron housing, his breath coming in ragged, shallow gasps. His left arm hung limp. The fabric of his sleeve was soaked, steam still curling off the sodden cotton. When he pulled the cuff back with his right thumb, the skin beneath was already mottled scarlet and blistering white along the forearm. 

He didn't make a sound. He just stood there, his forehead pressed against the cold iron of the mill frame, waiting for the gray spots in his vision to clear.

Across the gravel, Toby was crawling out from beneath the pallets. His hands were shaking so violently he couldn't push himself up. He looked at Marcus’s arm, his face draining of whatever color the heat had left in it.

"Marcus... Marcus, I..." Toby’s voice broke into a dry, gasping sob. He sat in the dirt, clutching his knees to his chest, his knuckles white. "I was just trying to keep the machine running. I thought if it stopped... they’re out there, Marcus. The marshals. The cruisers. They’re coming to take everything, and I broke the machine."

Toby buried his face in his grease-stained palms, his shoulders heaving. He wasn't crying like a rebel; he was crying like a kid whose mother had been evicted while he was still at school, sitting on the curb waiting for an uncle who was never going to show up.

Marcus straightened slowly. His arm burned with a deep, throbbing ache that made his fingers twitch, but he walked over to the wash sink. He kicked the foot pedal, letting the icy city water run, and held his burned forearm under the gentle stream. The cold was a knife, but it stopped the cooking.

"Get the zinc box from Maya’s desk," Marcus said. His voice was entirely level, steady as a plumb line.

Toby sniffled, wiped his nose on his sleeve, and scrambled to his feet. He ran to the forewoman’s desk and returned thirty seconds later with a white jar of silver sulfadiazine and a fresh roll of lint-free shop gauze.

"Sit," Marcus said, nodding toward a wooden crate.

Toby sat, his eyes red-rimmed, clutching the gauze like an offering.

"You didn't break it," Marcus said, holding his arm out while Toby, hands still trembling, applied the cold white paste with clumsy gentleness. "The spindle housing didn't crack. The run is sixty percent cut. We can finish the bore on the manual lathe with a boring bar once we cool the headstock down."

"I panicked," Toby whispered. "I thought... if we don't finish the sleeves, the people in the hall won't trust the network. They’ll take Miller’s deal. They’ll sign the relocation vouchers."

"Craft isn't speed, Toby," Marcus said quietly, watching the boy wrap the clean gauze around his blistered skin, tucking the end neatly into the wrap just like Marcus had shown him three weeks ago on a strip of scrap copper. "You hurry because you think the machine owes you an outcome. A machine doesn't care who’s at the gate. It only cares about tolerance. You respect the pressure, or it takes your skin. Every time."

Toby pinned the bandage. He looked up, his jaw trembling. "How do you do it? How do you not run?"

Marcus looked past him, through the wire-reinforced glass of the Bay 2 doors. Outside, through the frozen rain, the low, mechanical rumble of diesel engines rattled the panes. Two black tactical transport vans had pulled onto the gravel shoulder opposite Miller’s roadblock. Men in heavy olive jackets and tactical vests were stepping out, their faces obscured by balaclavas, carrying pry-bars and hydraulic cutters.

Federal marshals. The jurisdictional clock had run out.

Marcus flexed his scorched fingers. The skin pulled tight, burning fierce and bright, but the grip held.

"Because nobody else is going to turn the sleeves, Toby," Marcus said, his voice dropping into the quiet, unmovable center of a man who had already buried everything he had to lose. "Go get the three-quarter boring bar. We have four hours before dawn, and Delray is cold."
