The celebratory roar in the high bays had not died so much as it had seeped into the concrete, leaving behind the heavy, sulfurous quiet of three in the morning. Outside, over the soot-stained roofs of Delray, the Detroit River ran black between shelf ice, reflecting the sodium haze of the rail yards and the high, unblinking stars. 

David Sullivan sat on an overturned cable drum on the mezzanine, his elbows on his knees, staring down at the amber glow of the terminal. His eyes felt packed with road dust. For seven days, sleep had come only in twenty-minute seizures snatched against cold iron, leaving his jaw aching and his temples throbbing behind his glasses. On his wrist, an old matte-black digital watch with a cracked resin crystal pulsed in silence, tracking the UTC offset against the master clock on the screen.

Down below, the vast floor of Great Lakes Stamping was nearly still. Elena and Kira had collapsed hours ago onto an arrangement of wool army blankets and folded canvas behind the sheet-metal shear, sleeping with the dead, motionless weight of survivors who had finally run out of adrenaline. In Bay Four, the soft hum of Maya singing a Spanish hymn to the neighborhood children had long since trailed off into the gentle, cavernous respiration of the radiant floor heating. Only the low, sub-audible shudder of the river intake pumps remained, drawing water through the heat exchangers to keep the lines from freezing.

Boots scraped against the steel rungs of the ladder. 

Marcus Hayes reached the landing, carrying two enamel mugs that smelled faintly of scorched chicory. He did not speak. He handed one mug to Sully and lowered himself onto an empty wooden crate, his knees popping like dry spruce twigs. His work jacket was stiff with grease and dried hydraulic fluid; a fresh burn mark on his right sleeve showed where a soldering iron had kissed the nylon.

Sully took a sip. It was scalding, bitter, and thick as engine sludge. "Toby asleep?"

"Out cold on the crane operator's bench," Marcus said. His voice was cracked, reduced to a dry, low gravel. "Left his boots on. Couldn't pry them off if I tried."

Marcus set his mug between his steel toes and leaned forward, his massive hands clasped loosely. The factory had belonged to men like him for three generations, but sitting here now, in the quiet between an ending and a beginning, he seemed diminished in scale, reduced to the raw, fragile outline of a father who had outlived his world.

"Sarah used to keep a bowl of water on the bedroom radiator every November," Marcus murmured, his gaze fixed on the dark trusses across the bay. "Said the dry heat ruined the skin. Every morning, she’d drop two dried cloves into it. House smelled like an orchard in the middle of a blizzard." He paused, rubbing a thumb across the scarred flesh of his knuckle where an old drill bit had skipped twenty years ago. "Funny the things that don't come back until the noise stops."

Sully didn't offer a platitude. He had known Marcus long enough to know that grief, in this plant, was simply another form of structural fatigue—something you monitored, accounted for in the load calculations, and bore without drama.

Instead, Sully turned his head toward the telemetry array.

On the split-screen monitor, four distinct traces scrolled in continuous, unbroken green. Halfway across the world, on the high crags above the Rhône valley outside Geneva, the optical transceivers of the second node were battling a localized sleet storm; the telemetry showed dropped packets climbing to four percent before the buffered relays along the railway line caught the slack and stabilized. In the Atacama, under the bone-white blaze of high noon, a solar field was feeding steady current into a ring of salt-bath capacitors, the thermal curves flatlining into perfect equilibrium. And in the industrial maze of Shenzhen, where the morning shift was already filling the open-source electronics markets along the Huaqiangbei alleys, a cluster of basement servers hummed in lockstep with Detroit.

"They're holding," Sully said quietly. "All four."

"How long?" Marcus asked.

Sully checked the terminal’s hardware clock. 

`05:38:12 UTC.`

"Three minutes and forty-eight seconds," Sully said. "At 05:42, it hits one hundred and sixty-eight hours. Seven full days since the first handshake in the tunnel. Exactly seven days since the city cut the feed and told us we’d freeze by Thursday."

Marcus didn't smile. He looked out over the floor, where the massive primary press stood motionless, its freshly stamped disc still resting on the inspection bench like an offering. "The court injunctions?"

"Dissolved," Sully said. "The federal trustees in Manhattan couldn't find an operating entity left to serve. The holding company collapsed its paper before midnight. As far as the commercial registry is concerned, this plant is an abandoned lot with zero balance-sheet value."

"And the river line?"

Sully reached out, tapping a command into the terminal’s scuffed keyboard. A diagnostic window snapped open, showing the bidirectional power and water telemetry bridging the Detroit River to the grain silos in Sandwich, Ontario. For six days, the swap had been erratic—a precarious balance of hand-adjusted valve stems, jury-rigged inverters, and desperate radio calls across the international border. Now, the twin lines mirrored each other with mechanical elegance. Every gallon of treated wash-water sent north was balanced to the ounce by agricultural ethanol pumped south.

"Zero deficit," Sully said. "Thermodynamically closed. A steady state."

Marcus picked up his mug, holding it in both palms to draw the heat into his stiff joints. "Then Gate One clears."

"It clears," Sully breathed. 

He leaned closer to the amber phosphor. The architecture was unyielding. The Founders had built the sovereign sortition protocol to be immune to charisma, immune to speeches, and immune to the sudden panic of crowds. It didn't care about the ten thousand men and women who had stood in the sleet outside the gates, nor did it care about the private equity directors currently boarding flights out of Metro Airport. It cared only for the brutal, objective physics of survival: seven continuous days of energy and material balance across four continental hubs. If a single node had flickered—if a single transformer in Chile had tripped, or if the water swap with Windsor had stalled for more than sixty consecutive seconds—the counter would have reset to zero.

`05:41:15 UTC.`

The mezzanine grew so quiet Sully could hear the mechanical click of the master terminal’s internal cooling fan. Behind them, in the dark rafters, a pigeon shifted on an iron girder, its feathers rustling in the updraft of warm air from the heat exchangers.

`05:41:45 UTC.`

Marcus stood up slowly. He didn't say a word. He stepped to the railing, looking down at the dark machines, his silhouette framed against the distant, ice-choked sweep of the channel.

`05:41:58 UTC.`

`05:41:59 UTC.`

`05:42:00 UTC.`

There was no trumpet. No alarm sounded through the bays. 

On the terminal, the scrolling green ledger simply paused for the fraction of a second. A single line of text, rendered in clean, unadorned system monospace, swept across the screen:

`INTERVAL GENESIS: 168.00.00 CONCLUDED.`
`GATE 1 VERIFIED. EQUILIBRIUM ACHIEVED ACROSS 4/4 SECTORS.`
`ENGAGING SOVEREIGN SORTITION PHASE: GATE 2 TRANSITION OPEN.`

Sully let out a breath he felt like he had been holding since the day the eviction notices were nailed to the Delray firehouse. His chest burned. The mathematical certainty of it washed over him, clean and cold as well water. The network was no longer an insurrection. It was no longer a squatter’s siege. It had transitioned into an autonomous commons, recognized by the immutable laws of its own physics.

He reached for the mug to take a drink, but stopped.

On the auxiliary monitoring bus—the low-frequency sensory channel connected to the sub-fluvial fiber casing beneath the riverbed—a numeric value flickered. 

It was not a software glitch. The numbers did not flash red; they simply began to drift downward in a slow, unnatural decay.

Marcus turned from the rail, his instincts honed by forty years of listening to machine stress before a bearing threw its race. "Sully?"

"Pressure variance on the sub-river conduit," Sully said, his fingers already darting across the keys, bypassing the sortition interface to isolate the raw acoustic feed from the intake trench. "The intake grating we heard an hour ago. It wasn't drift debris."

On the monitor, the riverbed sensor array showed a tiny, rhythmic pulse of mechanical friction three fathoms beneath the surface ice. Something heavy, cold, and magnetically tethered had just clamped directly onto the primary armored casing of the trans-river optic link.

And it was cutting through the sheath.
