The shockwave from the river ice shuddered upward through eighty feet of glacial clay, rattling the galvanized roof trusses of Bay Two like teeth in a skull. 

Two hundred people flinched as one. Under the dim, yellowed halo of the emergency work lights, the shelter was a dense landscape of damp wool, shivering shoulders, and the sour musk of collective fear. Children whimpered into coats; older men sat on overturned plastic buckets, staring at the sweat dripping from the weeping concrete walls. Outside, the subzero gale off the Detroit River whistled through the vents, carrying with it the persistent, rhythmic groan of heavy steel grinding through the shore ice.

Elena Vance did not look at the ceiling. She stood beside a rusted column, her thumb pressed hard against the power switch of a handheld spectrum wand she had assembled that afternoon from spare radio parts. Her eyes burned with the gritty, sand-rubbed ache of four sleepless nights, but her fingers were steady. 

On the wand’s narrow amber readout, a jagged needle spiked. 

It was not the broad, sweeping pulse of Ironclad’s perimeter jammers, nor the clean, synchronized heartbeat of the node’s internal mesh. It was a short, sharp burst. A dirty, localized radio ping, repeating every twelve seconds, punching upward through the corrugated ceiling toward the commercial tower standing half a mile north on West End Avenue.

A private cell phone. Transmitting internal coordinates from the middle of the crowded bay.

"Someone is drawing a line right to our heads," Elena said softly.

Beside her, Marcus Hayes froze. His shoulders, knotted with tension beneath a frayed canvas chore coat, drew tight. He didn't ask how. He didn't make a sound. Instead, he simply adjusted the weight on his boots, his breath venting in faint white plumes into the freezing air, his gaze tracking Elena’s hand as she tilted the copper wire antenna.

"Where?" he murmured, barely moving his lips.

"South quadrant. Row three. Near the warm-air discharge."

Elena moved through the labyrinth of cots and blankets with deliberate slowness. She stepped over piles of winter boots, avoiding eye contact, keeping the wand tucked down along the seam of her trousers. The cold here was different from the sharp bite outside; it was a creeping, clammy chill that settled into the bone, born of finite battery power that forced them to run the blowers at quarter-throttle. Every human body in this room was radiating heat, and every human body was terrified.

The needle spiked harder. Six bars. Seven. 

She stopped behind a rusted oil separator where the shadows hung thickest. 

Sitting on an upturned milk crate was Carl Davis. He was fifty-six, with the sloped, stooped posture of a man who had spent thirty-four years leaning over a hydraulic stamping press until the cartilage in his lumbar spine simply turned to dust. His fingernails were permanently rimmed with black bearing grease, and his heavy jaw was covered in four days of graying, uneven stubble. His hands were tucked deep into the front pouch of a greasy Carhartt hoodie, his shoulders hunched inward as if trying to fold himself into nothingness.

Elena stepped around the crate. "Carl."

The man flinched violently. His hands twitched inside the pocket, and in that brief fractional second, the screen of an old, rubber-cased prepaid flip-phone peeked through the fabric gap—its tiny green indicator lamp flashing in sync with the wand's hum.

"Take your hands out of your pockets, Carl," Marcus said, his voice flat, low, and cold as river silt. He didn't raise his fists; he simply stood between Davis and the fire exit, cutting off the light.

Davis looked up. His eyes were wide, wet, and bloodshot, swimming with a sickening mixture of guilt and sheer, animal panic. "I... I was just checking the time, Marcus. My girl, she’s in Toledo, and the power—"

"Take it out," Elena repeated. Her voice wasn't loud, but in the hush that had begun to spread through the immediate circle of cots, it carried like a dry branch snapping.

Davis swallowed hard, his throat clicking. Slowly, his thick, trembling fingers emerged from the pouch, holding the cheap plastic handset. Its screen was illuminated, displaying a sequence of raw GPS coordinates and a draft message addressed to an unsaved ten-digit number: *Intake bay clear. Generator cluster behind south blast wall.*

A woman sitting two cots away gasped. "What is that?"

"Is that a phone?" an older man rasped, stumbling to his feet. "We turned ours in! We all put them in the lead bin at the door! Who’s he talking to?"

The ripple was instantaneous. Within seconds, the weary stupor of the shelter cracked and shattered into a jagged, volatile roar. Fifty people were on their feet. Then a hundred. The crowd pressed inward, their faces twisted with the ferocious, paranoid rage that only comes when cornered animals realize one of their own has opened the cage from the inside.

"He’s giving them our spots!" someone screamed from the back.

"He’s working for Briggs!"

"Throw the bastard out!" a dockworker roared, shoving his way to the front, an eighteen-inch length of iron pipe clenched white-knuckled in his fist. "Put him in the river! Let him freeze with his friends!"

The press of bodies surged forward, smelling of fear, wet wool, and cheap tobacco. Davis recoiled against the rusted tank, curling his limbs inward, sobbing openly as hands reached for his collar. 

Marcus took a half-step forward, his jaw tight, his instincts screaming to brace for impact. But before the pipe could swing, Elena stepped directly into the breach, placing her back to Davis and her palms flat against the chest of the foremost man.

"Stop!" she shouted.

"Get out of the way, Vance!" the dockworker snarled, his spit speckling her cheek. "He sold us out! My kids are sleeping on that floor! If Ironclad drops a shell on this roof, we burn because of him!"

"And if you beat him to a pulp on this floor, what do you become?" Elena’s voice was like ice cracked across a pond—sharp, clear, and uncompromising. She didn't flinch from the iron pipe. "Look at yourselves! The second we use their violence, the second we cast someone into a subzero gale to die without an accounting, the Covenant is dead. And if the Covenant is dead, there is no node. There’s just two hundred people waiting to freeze in a warehouse!"

The crowd hesitated, the raw, violent momentum stalling against her stillness. The dockworker lowered the pipe an inch, his chest heaving.

"The law doesn't vanish because it’s dark outside," Elena said, her voice dropping into a register of absolute, quiet authority. "Founders' Covenant, Article Seven. Non-punitiveness. Bodily autonomy is absolute. We do not exile our own into a storm. We do not draw blood."

"So he just sits here?" someone shouted from the darkness. "Warm and fed while he points guns at our heads?"

"No," Elena said. She turned back to Davis, reaching down and taking the phone from his limp, shaking fingers. She dropped it to the concrete and brought her boot heel down on the center hinge with a sharp, final crunch. "Carl Davis is relieved of all external communication apparatus. He is placed under immediate Restorative Spatial Quarantine."

She looked at Marcus. "Move him to the scullery in Bay Three. He doesn't sit idle. He tends the coal stoker for the radiant blowers through the night. Two observers on watch at all times. He eats the same broth we eat, he breathes the same air, and he works until the frost is off the iron."

The crowd grumbled, the blood-lust cooling into a sullen, exhausted compliance. They didn't like it—retribution was easier, cleaner, more satisfying to an empty stomach—but the sheer structural weight of the protocol held them. The dockworker spat on the floor, turned his back, and shoved his way through the cots.

Marcus stepped forward, gripping Davis by the upper arm—firmly, without malice, but with unyielding leverage—and hauled the sobbing machinist to his feet.

"Carl," Elena said, stepping close to him as he wept into his sleeves. "Look at me."

The man lifted his ruined, tear-streaked face. "They... they bought my girl’s medical lien," he choked out, his voice a pathetic, ragged wheeze. "From the clinic in Toledo. Twelve thousand dollars. A man named Miller called me three days ago. Said if I gave them the generator layout, they’d wipe the note. Said nobody would get hurt... they just wanted to turn the power off..."

"Who is coming, Carl?" Elena asked, keeping her tone stripped of anger, leaning in so only Marcus and she could hear. "Where are they?"

Davis shivered violently, his knees buckling until Marcus hoisted him straight.

"The barge," Davis whispered, his teeth chattering uncontrollably. "The horn out on the river... it wasn't a warning. It’s a sweep. Briggs isn't hitting Gate One. He knows you welded the turnstiles."

Elena felt the hair on the back of her neck stand up. "Then what is that boat doing?"

"It’s not one boat," Carl gasped, staring at the floor. "The tug is just breaking the shelf. Behind it... coming out of the slip at Zug Island... they’ve got three flat-bottom aluminum skiffs. Six men each. Tactical gear. They’re running blacked-out along the Old Channel ice right now. They're coming up through the abandoned cooling intake under Bay Two."

Elena’s heart slammed into her ribs. She looked down at the concrete floor beneath her boots. 

Beneath the wet gravel, beneath the lime mortar of the old foundations, thirty yards behind where two hundred people lay sleeping, the dark, forgotten water of the river was rushing through the intake tunnels.

And the grate was rusted through.
