The ten-ton test block struck the concrete with a sound like a freight train jumping the rails.

The impact did not touch Briggs. Kira had not aimed for flesh; she had aimed for the machine bed. The solid cast-iron mass sheared through the charging sled’s guide rail, pulverizing the top three inches of factory floor into a gray geyser of crushed aggregate and dust. The concussive shockwave ripped through the bay, rattling Kira’s teeth in her skull forty feet above and blowing the atomized mist into an instant whiteout. 

Below, the blast of displaced air knocked Briggs flat onto his back. His hard hat spun away across the wet deckplates. The lead thermite canister, still spitting a furious fountain of white-hot magnesium, broke free from his grip. It skittered across the flooded floor, hissing like a nest of vipers as it spun toward the unprotected copper busbars feeding the central converter.

If that heat touched the copper, the busbars would vaporize in three seconds. The core would starve, the circuit would shatter, and the entire east side grid would drop into cold darkness.

Kira didn't scream. Her mouth was bone-dry, tasting of river silt and ozone. She shoved the crane pendant’s primary bridge controller to the right with the heel of her hand, then dropped her palm onto the secondary auxiliary drum toggle. 

High in the roof trusses, the auxiliary winch screamed. A three-pronged industrial scrap claw, suspended on heavy double-braided steel cable, dropped out of the shadows like a diving hawk. Kira didn’t have a digital targeting reticle; she had only the burning magnesium flare reflecting off the wet floor and twenty years of watching her father guide timber skids through Michigan mud without snapping a line.

She caught the hoist on the drum brake just as the claw grazed the flooded deck. The heavy steel tines plowed through the pooling graywater, catching the burning canister in a spray of boiling steam. With a vicious jerk of the hoist lever, she dragged the fire away from the busbars, sweeping it across the bay floor until the claw tipped the canister over the lip of the emergency sand casting pit.

The thermite plunged into four feet of dry silica.

A muffled *whump* shook the pit walls. The blinding glare died instantly, smothered beneath tons of packed sand, venting only a thick, sulfurous coil of yellow-white smoke through the floor grating.

Above the roar of the graywater spray, Briggs rolled onto his stomach, coughing water and pulverized stone. His uniform was soaked, plastered to his shoulders, shivering violently in the sub-zero draft sweeping in through the fractured dock doors. "Get up!" he snarled toward the shadows behind him, his voice shredded. "Get the auxiliary lines! Crack the transformer housing!"

Two of his contractors scrambled up from the slick concrete near the reservoir. They had lost their pry bars in the blast, but one carried a heavy pneumatic wedge, its high-pressure hose trailing back toward their service truck. They lunged for the narrow access corridor running between the stamping beds—the only unshielded approach left to the Monolith’s secondary distribution bank.

Kira looked down from the pulpit. Her hands were shaking so hard she had to clamp both fists around the gantry release dog. 

On the far wall of the bay, mounted twenty feet up the steel column, hung the manual mechanical clutch for the 50-ton gantry’s upper die-storage rack. It had no digital lock. It had no electric solenoid. It was a brutal counterweighted bronze pawl designed in 1948 to prevent overhead tool dies from shifting during earthquakes.

Kira kicked the crane cab’s emergency hatch open. Cold air slammed into her chest, stealing her breath. She stepped out onto the narrow service walkway, three feet of diamond-tread steel suspended over empty air. The mist from the graywater washdown had already coated the handrails in a thin, glass-slick skin of rime.

She scrambled four paces along the beam, her work boots fighting for purchase on the frosted steel. She reached the safety lanyard tied to the mechanical clutch pawl, wrapped the greasy nylon braid three times around her forearm, planted her heels against a structural gusset, and threw her entire body weight backward into space.

The bronze pawl snapped back with a metallic crack.

Across the bay ceiling, the overhead storage rack shuddered. Four tons of solid cast-iron stamping die—a brutal, monolithic block of dark tool steel milled to shape truck fenders—slid free from its retaining shoes.

It fell through the mist like a meteor.

The die struck the concrete trackway twenty feet ahead of the advancing contractors. 

The floor did not just shake; it groaned. The sheer kinetic mass buried the iron block four inches deep into the structural slab, obliterating the rail guides and throwing up a wall of shattered concrete chips that peppered the contractors’ tactical vests. The sheer physical bulk of the die completely choked the access corridor, sealing the trackway from wall to column with an impenetrable wall of cold iron.

The two men scrambled backward on hands and knees, terror finally breaking through their discipline. They turned toward the roll-up door, desperate to reach their truck.

"No!" Briggs screamed from the center floor. He was alone now between the deadweight block and the converter casing, his face black with soot and streaked with graywater. He looked like an apparition dragged from a coal mine. "We finish it! We are not leaving this yard in their hands!"

He lunged toward the converter’s secondary control pedestal, a four-foot hexagonal steel crowbar raised high in his hands. He was aiming for the high-voltage ceramic insulators. One clean strike would shatter the porcelain, grounding fifty thousand volts through the chassis and blowing the substation fuses all the way to Jefferson Avenue.

A shape moved out from behind the steam baffle.

Marcus had not run for cover. He had spent the last three minutes working in the absolute dark beneath the die-press table, moving with the slow, deliberate rhythm of a man who knew the precise weight of every loose bolt in the facility. 

He didn't have a rifle. He didn't have a knife. 

In his calloused hands, Marcus held a three-foot die-alignment jig—a hollow rectangle of tubular tool steel fitted with two knurled rubber grips, used to manually seat four-hundred-pound blanking plates without pinching fingers.

Briggs brought the crowbar down with both arms, putting his entire desperate weight behind the blow.

Marcus didn't step back. He stepped in. 

He had worked forty years on stamping lines where a flinching man lost his hands to the downstroke. He read the arc of the steel before Briggs’s shoulders even completed the swing. Marcus raised the jig horizontally, thrusting the open steel frame directly into the crowbar’s path.

The hexagonal bar hit the center slot of the jig with a dull, ringing *thwack*. 

Before Briggs could recover, Marcus pivoted his hips, dropping his center of gravity and snapping his wrists forty-five degrees to the left. The jig acted as a perfect mechanical fulcrum. The leverage tore the crowbar cleanly out of Briggs’s oil-slicked gloves with a sickening wrench that strained the contractor’s forearms. 

The crowbar spun across the floor, splashing into a puddle of graywater ten feet away.

Briggs stumbled forward off-balance, his chest heaving, his boots sliding in the wet slurry. He caught himself against the frame of the deadweight block, his bare, bleeding fingers clawing at the cold iron. He looked up at Marcus, his breath coming in ragged, white plumes.

Marcus did not raise the jig to strike. He simply held it across his chest, his breathing slow, steady, and terrifyingly calm. 

"Step back, Briggs," Marcus said. His voice was not loud, but it cut through the hiss of the cooling steam with the density of lead. "It’s over."

High above them on the catwalk, Kira reached the wall-mounted trip cable for the industrial fire-curtain bulkheads. Her knuckles were bruised, bleeding where she had scraped them against the crane's steel cage, but her grip was iron.

She pulled the brass shear-pin.

With a deafening, chain-driven roar, the three-inch-thick corrugated steel fire curtains dropped from their overhead housings. They slid down vertical guide channels on either side of the bay, hitting the concrete sill with the absolute finality of a tomb closing. 

The heavy stamping bay was sealed. The exits to the yard, the access to the control room, and the path to the converter were locked behind eighty tons of gravity-dropped structural steel. Briggs and his four operators were trapped in a dead zone of cold iron and crushed stone.

Silence fell over the bay, broken only by the steady drip of condensation falling from the roof trusses into the pools below.

Briggs slowly sank to his knees on the wet concrete. His head hung low, his trembling hands resting flat in the cold water. The contractors near the blocked door sat down against the wall, their heads tilted back against the corrugated steel, spent, frozen, and hollowed out by the sheer, unyielding weight of the plant itself.

Then, through the high, frosted clerestory windows near the roofline, the first flashes cut through the swirling snow.

Crimson and brilliant blue strobes swept across the rusted iron beams, painting the rising steam in violent, flickering color. The low, synchronized wail of federal marshal sirens vibrated through the foundation, followed by the heavy, hydraulic crunch of armored tactical vehicles smashing through the outer perimeter barricades along the river approach.

Kira leaned her forehead against the freezing steel handrail of the crane pulpit. Her heart was hammering against her ribs like an unseated piston, but the Monolith beneath her boots was humming—steady, unbroken, and alive.
