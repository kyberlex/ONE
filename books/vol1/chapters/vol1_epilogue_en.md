The pine box smelled of green sap and damp sawdust, rough-planed pine hastily knocked together to catch whatever fate spat out of the chute. 

Marcus’s calloused fingers closed over the three slugs. They were still warm, heated by the violent friction of forty revolutions against the drum’s steel baffles. When he lifted them into the gray noon light of Bay One, the silence of four thousand men and women gathered on the cracked floor became absolute. The collective breath of an entire industrial basin hung in the rafters, visible as faint white frost against the corrugated iron.

Marcus flattened his palm. He read the first alphanumeric string stamped into the yellow metal, checking it against the grease-penciled ledger sheet clipped to the podium.

"Clara Ramos," Marcus said. His voice, stripped of theater, rolled across the concrete and out through the open bay doors toward the slush-choked rail yard.

A murmur rippled through the eastern apron. Near the battery racks, a woman in an oversized navy parka blinked against the sting of the cold. A faded Detroit Receiving Hospital badge swung from her zipper pull; her knuckles were raw from six days of treating frostbite and lacerations on twelve-hour triage rotations. She didn't shout. She didn't pump her fist. She merely drew her shoulders back, her jaw hardening into the stubborn, weary line of someone whose name had just been called for an unpaid, unrefusable night shift.

Marcus set the first slug on the table and picked up the second.

"Jean-Marc Tremblay."

Across the international boundary line traced down the channel of the river, an automotive line worker from the Windsor engine plant stepped forward to the rail of the mezzanine. Jean-Marc was broad-shouldered, with the stooped neck of a man who had spent twenty years guiding transmission blocks onto overhead carriers. He wiped his palms down the thighs of his oil-darkened Carhartts and nodded once, solemn and tight-lipped, accepting the charge without ceremony.

Then Marcus turned over the third brass token.

His thumb traced the edge of the disk. A hairline seam marked where the lathe had parted the slug from the bar stock. When he read the stamped sequence, his chest tightened, the air suddenly thin in his lungs. For half a heartbeat, the veteran organizer in him—the man who had spent three decades measuring risk down to the millimeter—wanted to close his hand, turn the cylinder again, and spare what was fragile. 

He looked toward the secondary patch bay.

Toby was leaning against a steel pillar, his forearms smudged with copper grease, his thumbs hooked under the straps of an electrician’s tool pouch. He was watching Marcus with the quiet, vigilant attention of a kid who had grown up expecting bad news from men holding clipboards.

"Toby Morales," Marcus said.

The sound of his own name seemed to strike Toby like a physical blow. The color drained from his windburned cheeks. His mouth parted, but no breath escaped. Around him, the assembly did not erupt into cheers; sortition was neither an election nor a lottery prize. It was the drafting of a soul into public custody.

The men and women standing near Toby stepped back, not out of distance, but to clear a path. Sully, his face drawn gray with fatigue, reached out and gripped Toby by the crook of his elbow, steadying him as the younger man’s boots scraped numbly against the salt-pitted cement.

"Move, kid," Sully murmured, his voice gentle for the first time in eight days. "The river doesn't wait."

Toby walked toward the platform. His knees felt hollow. Back when his mother had worked the late shift at the packing plant, he had spent evenings sitting on the concrete abutment below the bridge, counting the bridge’s steel rivets from one to four hundred to keep his mind from wandering to the bill collectors knocking on their screen door. Now, every face he passed seemed carved from the same hard, enduring metal. These were the machinists who had kept the chillers from seizing, the lineworkers who had spliced high-voltage lines in seventy-mile-an-hour squalls, the neighborhood grandmothers who had stirred navy bean soup in fifty-gallon drums over open propane rings to keep four precincts fed. 

They looked at him with an unbearable, unblinking trust. They weren't looking for a savior; they were looking at a mirror.

Marcus met Toby at the steps of the timber platform. Up close, Marcus could smell the bitter tang of battery acid and stale coffee on the wool of the young man’s jacket. 

Marcus didn't offer a speech. He reached into his own coat pocket and drew out Sarah’s watch. The silver casing was scratched, the crystal scuffed where it had struck a thousand doorframes and workbench corners, its leather band worn dark and supple with years of sweat.

"She always wound it six turns backward every midnight," Marcus whispered, taking Toby’s trembling wrist and pressing the cold silver into his palm. "Just to feel the ratchet engage before she let it sleep. In Geneva, everyone will have a clock that tells them what they're worth. You keep this one. It only measures how long the heat lasts."

Toby looked down at the scratched watch face. The steady, mechanical click of the escapement beat against his skin, rhythmic and stubbornly alive. "Marcus... I can't talk to diplomats. I don't know the language of those rooms."

"You know how to solder a cold trace without burning the board," Marcus said softly, his hand resting heavy and immovable on Toby's shoulder. "That's the only language they don't have. Clara knows how many units of saline a human body needs before it surrenders. Jean-Marc knows what steel does when the heat is cut. You three aren't going to Lake Geneva to beg for an audience. You're going to tell them the power has already shifted."

By late afternoon, the winter sun hung low and bronze over the southwestern reach of the river.

In the old shipping office overlooking the slipways, Clara Ramos sat at the laminate desk, her fingers flying across a scarred tablet as she left terse, clinical instructions for the floor nurses at Receiving: how to rotate the insulated saline wraps, which emergency generators needed their fuel filters blown clear every four hours, who to call when the municipal pressure dropped below forty pounds. Beside her, Jean-Marc stood before the window, methodically packing a canvas duffel with clean wool socks, three tubes of industrial hand salve, and an expired Canadian passport with a dog-eared corner.

Between them sat the covenant drive. 

It was a cylindrical block of milled billet steel, no larger than a thermos, sealed with three wax impressions stamped directly into the recessed bolts. Inside lay the immutable state vectors of forty-eight hours of survival: the distributed log of every kilowatt generated by the microgrid, every medical ampoule rationed, every bushel of grain released from the grain elevators without a central bank clearing the transaction. It was the physical proof that three million souls had lived through the siege without kneeling to the market.

Kira entered the office, her parka unzipped, snow melting in her short, dark hair. She set three paper boarding dockets on the table beside the drive.

"Commercial airspace over the East Coast is completely locked down," she said, her voice gravelly from exhaustion. "The clearinghouse banks placed a maritime interdiction on all domestic freight leaving the lakes. But an ore carrier—a grain freighter out of Thunder Bay—is clearing the Welland Canal under a private Panamanian registry. Her skipper has family in Ecorse. He’ll take you out past the Saint Lawrence cordon to Halifax before the port authority freezes his bunkering cards. From there, a French cable-repair boat has agreed to run you across the North Atlantic."

Toby looked at the dockets. The ink was faint, run off an old thermal receipt printer. "And Geneva? If the United Nations committee refuses to seat us?"

"They will try," Kira said simply. "The European banking cartels have already filed three emergency injunctions with the cantonal police. They're claiming this drive is stolen property. They're going to treat you like contraband."

Jean-Marc zipped his canvas duffel with a sharp, definitive rip of teeth. "Then they can explain to six hundred thousand French machinists why the Saint Lawrence current isn't clearing their grain contracts. Let them call the police."

Toby took the heavy steel cylinder from the desk. The cold metal sank into the meat of his palms. He slipped the strap over his shoulder, feeling the cylinder settle against his ribs, heavy as an anchor, steady as a heartbeat.

He walked out onto the elevated gantry of the loading dock. 

The wind had shifted out of the northwest, biting and dry, scouring the grease from the slipway. Below, the dark water of the Detroit River rolled toward Lake Erie, parting around massive slabs of blue lake ice that ground against the concrete pilings with a low, primeval shudder. Across the water, the lights of Windsor were coming on—not the flickering, desperate emergency grids of four days ago, but a steady, deliberate constellation of small, stubborn human fires, matched lantern for lantern, window for window, by the shore he stood upon.

Marcus was standing at the end of the pier, his coat collars turned up against the river spray, watching the gray channel where the tugboat’s running lights were already cutting through the gathering dusk.

Toby closed his hand around the watch in his pocket, felt the cold wind fill his lungs, and stepped down into the dark.
