The child’s scream cut through the blackout like cold tin.

Matteo didn’t let go of Chiara’s sleeve until he felt her steady herself against the iron handrail of the inspection pit. His right knee sent a jagged bolt of heat straight up his hip—the damp cold of the concrete floor always bit into the old derailment fracture before his eyes could adjust to the dark. He coughed, catching the phlegm against the greasy canvas tape wrapped around his split thumb, tasting the familiar grit of rock dust and machine lime.

"Battery bank is holding the low-voltage loop," Alexandre’s voice drifted from three yards away, stripped of its usual clipped cadence. A tiny match scraped. The yellow flame flared against Alexandre’s hollow cheekbones, illuminating the blue veins branching across his temple before he touched the sulfur to a stub of paraffin tallow. "The relay mast didn’t short into the bus. The lines were cleanly severed on the peak."

"Cleanly severed means shaped charges," Matteo said. His voice was a dry rattle. "Which means AlpSec didn’t climb up there in snowshoes. They dropped a team by skid on the Swiss rim before the blizzard closed the pass."

Around them, the vast darkness of the roundhouse rustled with fifteen hundred bodies stirring in terror. The radiant floor was already bleeding its stored thermal mass into the rising draft. Outside, beyond the three-foot stone masonry of the south perimeter wall, came the dull, rhythmic hammering of iron bars against sheet metal.

Not mercenaries. People.

"The lower township," Chiara whispered, her breath frosting in the candle’s aura. "The municipal power died when the mountain line snapped. They’re running up the switchback from the station."

"There are four hundred families jammed against the bay three roll-doors," Ferrero’s heavy boots echoed as he hurried out of the gloom from the machine shop, holding a battery lantern pointed strictly at his boots to avoid blinding the room. His coveralls smelled of sulfurized cutting oil and damp wool. "The frost outside is twenty below and dropping. If we keep the outer vestibule sealed to protect the turbine reserves, those people will freeze on the gravel apron before sunrise."

"And if we open the locomotive bays," Davide said, stepping into the lantern’s dim amber pool, "we blow our thermal seal. We exhaust the hot-water floor loops in two hours, and the moisture from fifteen hundred wet coats will condense straight onto the battery terminals. It’ll short the low-voltage banks before the midnight shift can clear the flume."

A murmur rippled through the front row of refugees huddled over the radiant slabs. An old woman clutched a cloth bundle to her chest; a younger man with grease under his fingernails stood up, his jaw set hard against Davide.

"We don't hold backrooms while neighbors turn blue on the ballast," the young man snapped.

"It’s not a backroom, it’s our life support!" Davide shot back, his voice rising toward the rafters. "You flood the maintenance track with four hundred wet bodies, and we lose the pressure head! Then *everyone* freezes!"

"Quiet!" Matteo roared. The effort tore at his throat, triggering a deep, rasping cough that bent him double against a rail stanchion until his ribs ached. He spat into the dust, straightened his back, and slammed his calloused palm down on the rim of the heavy brass sortition drum mounted on the depot's old weigh-station desk. "Article Eight, Section Three. We don't scream each other down like stockyard hogs. Not tonight."

The silence returned, brittle as mountain rime.

Matteo reached into his vest pocket, pulled out the lead seal, and cracked the wire locking the sortition drum's polished lid. The three hundred bone tokens inside—each stamped with the registered parish census roll of the valley floor—clattered together with a dry, hollow rattle.

"Stage Three Local Mediation," Matteo murmured, his eyes sweeping the shadowy circle of faces. "Three arbiters. Random lot. No appeals, no technocratic vetoes. What the lot decides, the floor executes."

He spun the drum twice. The sound of tumbling bone seemed impossibly loud beneath the cavernous ceiling.

He reached his scarred hand through the felt collar of the hatch, drew three discs, and laid them flat beneath Ferrero’s lantern.

"Davide Vana," Matteo read. The mechanic stiffened, his jaw tightening as he realized he was bound by the very drum he had helped bolt to the floor.

"Signora Elena Rossi," Matteo continued.

From the second row of blankets, an elderly woman rose slowly, using a polished walnut cane to take the weight off her swollen left ankle. Her dark woolen cardigan was buttoned to the chin with mismatched horn buttons, her grey hair pulled back in a severe, no-nonsense knot. For forty years she had taught third-form arithmetic to half the mechanics in this shed; her gaze, magnified behind thick, round tortoiseshell spectacles, still carried the terrifying, quiet gravity of a woman who had broken up classroom knife-fights during the factory strikes of 1977.

"And Pietro Clataud," Matteo finished.

A tall, sinewy man standing by the airlock curtain stepped forward into the yellow ring. Pietro’s face was the color of smoked cedar, his lower lip split by alpine wind and patched with a square of amber beeswax tape. A coiled nylon climbing sling hung over his shoulder, smelling of cold mountain granite and wet hemp. He didn't speak; he merely nodded once, his grey eyes assessing the room with the emotionless calculation of an ice guide reading a hanging serac.

"The question," Matteo said, his voice dropping into the formal, level cadence required by the charter, "is whether to drop the mechanical interlocks on locomotive bays two and four, opening three thousand square meters of dry internal track to the four hundred residents trapped outside on the apron, knowing the thermal and electrical load will push the Pelton storage reserves into deep depletion."

Matteo laid an old brass three-minute hourglass on the desk. The fine white sand began to trickle.

"Davide," Matteo said.

"No," Davide said instantly, though his hands shook as he gripped the desk edge. "I’m not a monster. My sister is outside that door with her two boys. But if we pull the steam pipes into the outer bays, the penstock water velocity drops below scouring speed. The flume will slush up. If the flume freezes solid, the Pelton runner stops dead, and by four in the morning we won't have heat, lights, or water for anyone. It's suicide."

The sand hissed down the glass neck.

Matteo looked at Elena Rossi. The old schoolteacher didn't look at Davide, nor at Matteo. She looked back over her shoulder, out toward the blackened clerestory windows where the snow was packing into the corners of the iron frames.

"Davide," she said, her voice dry, precise, and completely devoid of sentimentality. "When your father had his leg crushed in the lower freight siding thirty years ago, the depot master wanted to leave him on the timber car until the night express cleared the main line. He said stopping the express would throw the entire regional schedule into chaos. Do you remember who threw the manual switch to bring him in?"

Davide swallowed hard, his Adam's apple bobbing. "You did."

"The rules of an engine house exist to preserve human life, not to turn an iron shed into an iron tomb," Signora Rossi said, tapping her walnut cane firmly against the concrete. "If the flume begins to freeze, we put forty young men on the high trail with wooden rammers to break the slush by hand, as my uncles did in the winter of fifty-six. We open the bays."

A sharp intake of breath ran through the circle. One for, one against.

The last grains of sand fell into the bottom bulb of the glass.

All eyes turned to Pietro Clataud.

The guide adjusted the sling on his shoulder. He reached out with two fingers—thick, scarred, missing the nails from frostbite—and tapped the thermometer nailed to the timber post beside the desk. The red alcohol column stood at four degrees above freezing, inside the warmest corner of the floor.

"The wind shifted ten minutes ago," Pietro said. His voice was low, rasping like granite shifting under boots. "It came off the north face. That means a black freeze in the valley. Anyone left on the ballast gravel past midnight will have fluid in their lungs before dawn. You can’t ram slush down a flume if your hands are frozen stumps."

He looked directly at Davide.

"We open the bays. But we don't open the radiant floor valves to them. We run the hot turbine runoff water through the ballast ditches beneath the track boards. It won't be warm. It will be five degrees, enough to keep wool dry and blood moving, but it won't drop the turbine head."

He looked at Matteo. "Vote is two to one. Open the doors."

"Motion carried," Matteo said. His heart hammered violently against his ribs, but the relief in the room was a palpable, humid weight that seemed to lift the freezing gloom. "Ferrero, take six men to the hand-cranks on bay two. Slow lift. Let them in ten at a time—no stampede, no baggage carts."

Ferrero turned instantly, barking names into the shadows. The iron dog-clutches of the bay two roll-doors engaged with a hollow, brassy *clonk*.

Matteo limped forward as the massive corrugated steel curtain began to creak upward on its rusted pulleys. A rush of sub-zero air flooded into the roundhouse, smelling of wild alpine snow, wet asphalt, and the sharp, chemical reek of burning diesel exhaust drifting from the highway checkpoints three miles down the valley.

Outside, huddled under the blinding halogen glare of the battery work-lamps mounted to the eaves, a sea of upturned faces blinked through ice-encrusted scarves.

Women holding infants wrapped in wool blankets pushed through the gap; old men in railway greatcoats supported each other, their breath rising in thick, ghostly plumes as they crossed the iron threshold onto the safety of the dry rails.

Matteo stood by the pillar, his hand resting on the manual brake lever, counting the heads as they filed past.

Then his eyes snagged on something wrong.

Ten paces out on the snowy apron, standing motionless while the desperate crowd surged past him, was a man in an unbuttoned, salt-stained blue road-crew parka. His hands were tucked deep into his pockets. He wasn't shivering, and he wasn't looking for shelter.

His eyes were locked directly on the heavy steel hatch that led down into the Pelton turbine gallery—and from beneath the hem of his dirty canvas parka, a thin spool of military-grade detonating cord trailed backward through the churned grey snow.
