The district heating manifold died with a wet, hollow shudder that traveled through two hundred meters of cast-iron pipe. 

Chiara Leone did not look up from the gurney, but she felt the drop through the soles of her clogs. The low, steady hiss of the methane burners along the roundhouse perimeter simply evaporated, replaced by the tick of cooling metal and the shriek of the blizzard scouring the corrugated zinc roof. On the wall beside the inspection pit, the brass needle of the gas supply gauge slumped against its lower peg. Zero bar. 

Vanguard had shut the main gate valves down in the Susa basin.

"Elena, seal the intake louvers on the southern bay," Chiara said. Her voice remained at the level, rhythmic cadence she had spent four years perfecting under the harsh strip lights of the Molinette trauma ward in Turin. Panic in a ward was infectious; tone was an antiseptic. "Bring the wool staging blankets to bay two. We lose ambient air temp in twenty minutes."

The massive sliding timber doors rattled in their tracks as another gust struck the depot. Through the pedestrian hatch, a dozen figures stumbled out of the whiteout, caked in wind-driven powder. Families from the rail cottages down by the marshaling yard, wrapped in mismatched duvets and nylon work coats, their breath coming in ragged plumes of steam. 

A young father carried a toddler whose lips were already the color of skimmed milk.

"Over here," Chiara commanded, pointing to the duckboards laid across the dry service trench. "Take off the wet outer layers. Don't rub his hands. I said *do not rub them*."

A volunteer had already reached toward the boy’s boots with a rough canvas rag. Chiara intercepted his wrist with a grip honed on steel retractors. 

"Frictional rewarming shears necrotic capillaries and floods the core with lactic acid," she clipped out, her Molinette ER training stripping every trace of patience from her tongue. "Swiss stage two hypothermia. If you dump cold, acidotic venous blood back into his right atrium right now, his myocardium will fibrillate before you finish untying his laces. Core only. Insulate the thorax, hot water bladders to the axillae and groin. Move."

The volunteer swallowed hard, nodding, and scurried to fetch the insulated rubber bladders from the electric sterilizer vat. 

The air in the roundhouse was thinning with cold. Without the gas heaters, the immense cavern of brick, iron, and grease was a thermal sink. The only heat left came from the Pelton turbine’s secondary loop—piping waste coolant from the generator bearings into radiant floor tubes around the center turntable—and the salvage battery bank hummed with a taut, low-frequency strain. 

The hatch groaned open again. Matteo Rossi backed through the opening, dragging Lukas Keller by the chest harness of his parka. 

Matteo’s face was an open wound of cold. Ice hung from his gray eyebrows, and his right knee buckled twice before he found the support of an iron pillar. Lukas was worse; the boy’s head lolled against his shoulder, his eyes glassed over in the stupor of severe systemic cold, hands curled into stiff, useless claws inside wet leather mitts.

Chiara crossed the grease-stained floor in four strides. She didn't waste breath on questions. She caught Lukas under the armpits, taking his dead weight as Matteo sank onto an inverted oil drum, his chest heaving with dry, barking coughs.

"How long was he exposed on the flume?" Chiara asked, tearing Lukas’s iced zipper open with a pair of trauma shears.

"Forty minutes," Matteo rasped, spitting a fleck of gray phlegm onto the concrete. His thumb, bound in blackened tape, trembled against his thigh. "The wind... sixty knots on the scarp. He dropped his outer shell when we secured the bypass. The optical laser to Switzerland... Alex says it’s down. Relay mast on the ridge is severed."

"Let Alexandre worry about the glass," Chiara said, her fingers finding Lukas’s carotid. The pulse was thready, forty-two beats a minute, dragging through cold-thickened blood. "Radial pulse is absent. Core temperature is dropping past thirty-one. Elena! Thermal wrap and two bags of warmed saline from the incubator. We need an intravenous line before the peripheral veins collapse entirely."

Across the roundhouse, thirty people were now huddled over the radiant floor plates. Children were crying with that shrill, monotonous whine that signaled genuine somatic distress. The concrete, soaked in seventy years of diesel fuel and machine oil, was radiating the black frost of the mountain through the blankets. The smell of wet wool, fear, and scorched electrical insulation hung thick in the dead air.

From the radio desk elevated above the locomotive pits, Alexandre Favre’s voice crackled through the depot’s industrial horn speakers. 

"Chiara. Look at the lower approach monitor."

Chiara finished taping the catheter into Lukas’s cephalic vein, securing the saline line beneath his collar so the liquid wouldn't freeze in the drip chamber. She stepped past the turntable to the gantry ladder and climbed to the glassed-in control booth.

Alexandre was pressing a palm against his left temple, his skin ashen under the green glow of the terminal monitors. He didn't look at her; he tapped a cracked screen with the tip of an insulated screwdriver.

The thermal feed from the yard perimeter camera was tearing in horizontal bands, degraded by the dense curtain of alpine snow, but the shape was unmistakable.

It wasn't a standard patrol truck. AlpSec hadn't brought light armor against the blizzard.

Creeping up the valley siding at three kilometers an hour was an industrial rail-mounted rotary snowplow—a monstrous, diesel-hydraulic beast requisitioned from the northern rail yards. Its twin front-mounted steel cutting drums, two and a half meters in diameter, turned slowly in the dark, throwing a low spray of ice chunks as it chewed through the drifts. Mounted behind the cutting cowl, two high-pressure glycol lances hissed continuously, spraying boiling antifreeze onto the rails to clear the switchgear.

"They’re clearing the spur," Alexandre muttered, his voice tight with migraine exhaustion. "They know we’re drawing tailrace water from the flume into the secondary loop. If that rotary plow reaches the lower turnout, the glycol will contaminate the discharge marsh, and the plow’s blade will shear the surface drainage pipe clean off the foundation. The flume will back up. The Pelton casing will flood and freeze solid in an hour."

"They aren't trying to shoot their way in," Chiara said, her eyes tracing the machine's slow, methodical advance. The cold had crept into the booth; her fingers inside her nitrile gloves were losing their spring. "They're just engineering the frost."

"It's clean," Alexandre said with bitter admiration. "No European warrant violations. No live fire. Just an emergency track-clearing operation authorized by the regional transport authority to 'restore critical infrastructure.' When the flume freezes, the cold does the eviction for them. We either evacuate the roundhouse into the blizzard or freeze where we sit."

Below them, the roundhouse shuddered. A dull, rhythmic clanking echoed through the floor plates. 

Matteo was back on his feet down on the turntable. He was holding a heavy steel pinch bar, his breath pluming like white smoke under the hanging sodium lights. He looked up at the booth, catching Chiara's gaze through the glass. 

"The gravity hopper," Matteo yelled up, his voice competing with the rising whine of the diesel engine outside. "Track three! We have eighty tons of basalt ballast sitting on the upper incline above the switch! If we drop the manual trip, it rolls down the spur and fouls the turnout before that rotary reaches the points!"

Alexandre leaned over the console mic. "The switch linkage is frozen solid, Matteo! The solenoid didn't fire—the frost jammed the points half an inch out of alignment. You can’t release the hopper from inside. Someone has to crawl under the switch apron with a torch and clear the ice by hand!"

Outside, the roar of the diesel engine deepened. The rotary cutter struck a packed drift fifty meters from the lower gate, sending an explosion of ice and rock against the corrugated iron siding of the roundhouse. The impact sounded like artillery. In the triage bay, three children screamed, their mothers pulling them tight against the cold concrete.

Chiara looked down at Lukas. The saline was running, but his shivering had not restarted—his central nervous system was hovering right on the edge of the metabolic cliff. Behind him, forty people sat shivering in the dying warmth of a hydro circuit that had minutes left to live.

She turned to Matteo, then to the heavy canvas parka hanging by the booth hatch. 

"Alexandre, take over Lukas's drip," Chiara ordered, stripping her rubber gloves and snatching her fleece liner. "Keep the infusion rate at sixty drops a minute. If his pulse drops below thirty, do not administer chest compressions—you'll crack the frozen sternum. Just keep the core warm."

"Chiara, wait," Alexandre said, reaching out. "You're a surgeon, you can't—"

"There are no surgeries if the turbine cracks," she said, her voice dropping into that flat, unyielding pitch of the triage floor. 

She took the heavy brass propane blowtorch from the maintenance rack, racked the igniter until the blue cone hissed to life in the freezing draft, and dropped through the hatch into the dark of the yard.
