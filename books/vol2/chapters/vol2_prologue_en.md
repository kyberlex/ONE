The frost inside the turbine pit did not melt; it sublimated under the blue flame of the weed-burner, turning straight from crust to a bitter, choking mist that tasted of dead iron and sulfur.

"Keep the torch moving, Davide," Matteo Rossi rasped, his voice scraping against early silicosis. "Linger more than three seconds on the bronze casting and the sleeve cracks. If the sleeve cracks, sixty atmospheres of Cenisio melt-water shears this roundhouse off its footings and drowns the valley floor."

Davide, nineteen years old, nodded frantically. His wool balaclava was stiff with frozen snot, his breath bursting in ragged white plumes. His oversized leather mitts trembled around the brass wand of the propane torch. Beneath them, the bypass valve of the decommissioned Pelton runner was a petrified fist of black grease and mountain ice.

Matteo wedged his good hip against the retaining girder, biting back a grunt as the arthritic cartilage in his right knee ground like gravel. His right thumb, split to the quick and bound in grime-stiffened canvas tape, throbbed with every pulse of his heart. Every time the draft whistled down the penstock flume from the high glacial reservoir, it brought the ghost of his brother Paolo: the same wheezing rattle Paolo had made in the company infirmary thirty years ago, drowning in rail-grinding dust while the Milanese directors argued about the cost of respirators.

*Not this boy,* Matteo swore silently, his jaw aching from the cold. *Not on my shift.*

"The indicator needle," Davide shouted over the torch’s hiss. "It’s twitching!"

"Hold the flame steady on the seat ring. Don't look at the dial. Feel the brass through the wand."

A boot heel clattered against the iron rungs of the pit ladder. Giulia Bellini dropped the final four feet, landing hard in the slush. Her tailored wool coat—cut for Milanese boardrooms, now smeared with hydraulic oil and soot—flapped against her shins. Her left hand was shoved deep into her pocket, but Matteo could see the fabric jerking; the thirty-six hours without sleep had reduced her nerves to raw copper wire.

"They just crossed the Dora Riparia bridge," she said, her voice pitched tight, barely riding above the torch. "AlpSec escorting three unmarked tribunal vans. The commercial receiver has the formal eviction writ."

Matteo didn't turn around. "Let them show their paper to the gates. The ballast hoppers are chained across the rails."

"Matteo, listen to me," she grabbed his canvas sleeve, her fingers digging in with desperate strength. "This isn't a civil lockout anymore. I took the restructuring ledgers from the safe in Frankfurt. If AlpSec takes this perimeter before the municipal network synchronizes, it isn't an eviction. It's an anti-terror extraction. They will take me straight through the Fréjus bypass to Le Vallette in Turin, and nobody will ever see those asset-stripping files again."

Matteo ripped his arm free, turning his bloodshot eyes on her. The class hatred burned hot in his throat, competing with the cold. "You sat in those glass towers for six years, Bellini. You calculated the liquidation value of our freight yards down to the copper in the switchgear. And now you come running up our valley because the wolves you fed smelled your scent."

Giulia flinched as if struck with an iron bar, her pale face draining of what little color remained. But she didn't step back. "I was wrong," she whispered, the tremor breaking into her throat before she locked her jaw. "I helped them hollow out this line. But that machinery behind you is the only thing keeping the valley clinic alive tonight. If you don't spin that turbine, forty people freeze before dawn. Hate me when the lights are on."

Matteo stared at her. The tremor in her hand was shaking her whole shoulder now, but her eyes didn't drop.

"Get up to the inspection bay," he growled, turning back to the valve. "Check the load bank. Now."

Giulia scrambled back up the ladder. Matteo took the heavy cast-iron spanner from the frozen duckboards, setting its jaws across the square nut of the bypass. "Shut the propane, Davide. Put your weight on the cheater pipe with me. On three."

Above them, in the cavernous warmth of the locomotive inspection shed, forty elders from the lower hamlets sat huddled along the sunken repair pits. Chiara Leone moved between them like a shadow in an oilskin smock, tucking thermal blankets around brittle shoulders. A paraffin heater guttered in the center of the bay, throwing long, anxious silhouettes against the flaking yellow brick walls.

In the corner, an eight-year-old boy named Marco lay on a canvas stretcher, his chest heaving with high, bird-like whistles. His mother knelt beside him, repeatedly pressing the manual bulb of an empty inhaler, her knuckles white.

"Chiara," the mother sobbed, her voice hushed so the elders wouldn't hear. "The battery on the nebulizer is dead. It won't take a charge from the truck inverter. He can't get the vapor down."

Chiara pressed two fingers to the boy's neck, feeling the frantic, shallow flutter of his carotid artery. The skin beneath his eyes was turning the bruised plum color of anoxia.

"Five minutes, Rosa," Chiara murmured, her fingers warm and steady despite the exhaustion clawing behind her temples. She reached into her medical bag, pulled out a small glass ampoule of saline, and held it close to her chest to keep it from frosting over. "Matteo is opening the head-gates. The wall sockets will have current in five minutes. Keep his head elevated. Look at me, Rosa. Breathe with him."

A sharp metallic thud echoed from the catenary catwalks fifty feet above. Lukas Keller hung in a high-angle harness, his face masked in grease and frost. Two fingers on his left hand were taped together, deadened by mountain chilblains, but his right hand deftly aligned the heavy gimbal of the Free Space Optics transceiver. Through the shattered panes of the roundhouse clerestory, the snowstorm was a churning wall of white fury, obliterating the valley floor.

Alexandre Favre leaned over the railing of the elevated crane cab, his laptop balanced on an overturned oil drum. A blue glow reflected off his wire-rimmed glasses, illuminating the deep gray hollows of a three-day migraine. In his palm, he rolled a heavy brass USB hardware key, its surface deeply scored with an intricate geometric cipher—interlocking hexagons etched with micrometric precision.

"Did you find the origin?" Lukas called down through the blizzard draft, his teeth chattering.

"It matches the seed-hash that surfaced in Detroit three hours ago," Alexandre yelled back, his fingers flying across the dirt-smudged keys. "Whoever wrote this protocol didn't leave a name. Just this key. If Matteo’s runner doesn't generate forty-eight cycles within the next two minutes, the local mesh won't have enough bus-voltage to fire the laser diodes. The Swiss relay on Rocciamelone will timeout and drop the route."

"What's the clock?" Lukas shouted.

Alexandre checked the lower corner of his terminal. The UTC timestamp blinked in steady green phosphorescence: *05:40:12*.

"One minute forty-eight seconds to Day Zero!"

Outside the double-leaf timber doors of the marshaling yard, high-intensity halogen beams sliced through the driving snow. The heavy rumble of diesel engines vibrated through the roundhouse floor—three armored utility carriers belonging to AlpSec, their tires wrapped in hardened diamond-pattern ice chains that crunched brutally over the ballast.

Over the external yard speaker, a voice amplified by a vehicle bullhorn cut through the storm, flat and bureaucratic:

"This is the court-appointed receiver for the Transalpine Freight Facility. Under order of the Turin Commercial Tribunal, this yard is closed. All occupants are in violation of regional infrastructure protection statutes. Clear the track apron immediately or private security contractors will deploy mechanical breaching apparatus."

Inside the turbine pit, Matteo didn't even blink.

"Davide," he breathed, his boots digging into the sludge. "Heave!"

The boy screamed through his teeth, throwing his full ninety pounds onto the end of the four-foot galvanized pipe. Matteo shoved with his shoulder, the iron spanner biting into the frozen nut. For a terrifying fraction of a second, the steel groaned and refused to yield. Matteo’s arthritic knee gave a sickening pop, pain blinding him with white sparks. Paolo's face flashed behind his eyelids—the dust, the blood on the handkerchief, the closed gates of the company store.

*Break, you bastard.*

*CRACK.*

The ice shattered like a rifle shot. The bypass spindle spun half a turn.

High above, deep within the mountain flank, six hundred vertical meters of pressurized glacial water hit the deflector plates. A sound rose from the bedrock—not a mechanical noise, but an oceanic, subterranean roar that shook the soot from the roof trusses fifty feet overhead.

"Clear the pit!" Matteo bellowed, throwing his arm around Davide’s chest and hauling the boy bodily up the iron rungs.

The main needle valve slid open under hydraulic servo pressure. Glacial water hit the dual-runner Pelton wheel at sixty atmospheres. The floor beneath them jumped. The massive bronze runner screamed as it caught the jet, spinning from zero to a blur of shrieking, hydro-driven torque. In the machinery bay, the salvaged locomotive battery banks took the initial surge, their heavy contactors slamming shut with a series of sharp, rhythmic cracks like artillery fire.

In the inspection shed, the dead overhead industrial lamps flickered, browned, and then ignited into brilliant, humming daylight.

Marco's mother gasped as the small electric nebulizer on the bench beside her hummed to life, pumping a fine, cool mist of medication into the boy’s plastic mask. Chiara collapsed against the brick wall, closing her eyes as the boy took his first deep, shuddering lungful of air.

"Load locked!" Giulia yelled from the master electrical switchboard, her face bathed in the green glow of the distribution meters. "Bus voltage stable!"

"Now, Lukas!" Alexandre roared into his headset. "05:42!"

On the snowy catwalk, Lukas slammed home the high-voltage breaker for the optical rig.

Through the blizzard-choked roof, a blinding, cyan-blue laser beam pierced the storm, stabbing straight up into the howling dark toward the 3,500-meter peak of Mount Rocciamelone, burning through the snow to lock the cross-border mesh with the Swiss passes.

At that exact second, the heavy timber gates of the marshaling yard splintered inward under the hydraulic ram of an AlpSec carrier, their tactical sirens tearing the mountain air to shreds.
