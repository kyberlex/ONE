The chemical light of the mortar flare hung over the crags of Mount Rocciamelone like an unblinking sulfur eye, staining the snowdrifts an oily, bruised yellow. 

At the transfer board, Lukas Keller didn’t look up at the parachute drifting across the rock face. Looking up cost seconds, and seconds cost heat. His left hand—the ring and small fingers deadened to wood from an old frostbite slip on the high-tension towers above Chamonix—ached with a dull, hollow throb that started in his marrow. Between his teeth, he ground a hard knot of dried larch resin, a habit picked up during three winters of alpine rigging to keep the dry mountain air from cracking his gums.

"Bell!" Elio shouted, his voice cracking against the scream of the tailrace below.

Lukas braced his work boots against the frost-heaved concrete. He caught the crossbar of the four-pole gang switch with both hands, throwing his weight backward. The heavy copper blades ripped free from their spring-loaded jaws with a blue-white snap that singed the grease on his canvas sleeves and turned the gloom of the switch room brilliant, blinding noon. 

Phase B went dead. Down in the valley throat, the orange rail-heaters died to dull charcoal. Phase C slammed home with an iron clatter that rattled Lukas’s molars.

"Three minutes to clear the third switch!" Franco bellowed through cupped mittens from the doorway, snow swirling around his shoulders like ground bone. "Tracks six and nine are icing over again! Shovel gang is dragging!"

Then, before Lukas could reset his stance for the next interval, the world outside simply vanished.

It didn't flicker. It didn't stutter with the brown-out sag of a strained line. Across the valley floor, the scattered sodium-yellow constellations of Susa and Bussoleno ceased to exist. Five miles of human settlement dropped into an absolute, suffocating void, extinguished in the time it took a heart to contract. The deep, familiar 50-hertz drone that had resonated through the alpine valley for eighty years—the foundational vibration of five thousand refrigerators, street lamps, and electric baseboards—evaporated.

The only lights left in the province were the blinding halogen work-torches of the rail terminal and the single, stubborn amber window of the Bussoleno district clinic across the river, fed by the terminal’s private umbilical.

"Grid isolation," Matteo Rossi said. His voice was completely flat, drained of surprise, though his fingers clenched hard into the railing of the turbine mezzanine until his knuckles showed gray through grease-stained tape. "Regional dispatch pulled the line breakers at the Avigliana sub. They’ve cut the valley adrift."

"They cut the mains?" Elio dropped his clearing crowbar; the steel rang dead on the frost-dusted floor. "It’s fourteen below out there! Half the apartment blocks on Via Roma converted to electric thermal storage three years ago! Those homes don't have flues. They don't have coal stoves!"

"Two thousand homes," Lukas murmured, spitting a splinter of bitter resin onto the concrete. His deadened fingers throbbed in rhythm with his pulse. He knew the housing blocks at the edge of Bussoleno. He’d lived in one through his first apprentice winter—concrete pre-fab slabs thrown up in the seventies with paper-thin insulation that let the alpine frost creep through the drywall until the baseboards were furred in white rime by midnight. Without the coils humming inside the plaster, an apartment turned into an icebox in ninety minutes.

Behind them, the twin Pelton runners snarled inside their iron casings. Freed from the tiny load of the rail heaters, the high-head jet from the Cenisio penstock slammed against the spoon-shaped buckets with terrifying, unspent kinetic fury. The tachometer needle shivered violently against its brass stop. The whole powerhouse floor began to oscillate, a high-frequency shudder that climbed through the soles of Lukas’s boots into his hip bones.

"Governor’s hunting!" Matteo barked, his knee hitching visibly as he lunged toward the hydraulic spear-valve actuator. "The hydro head is backing up! If we don't dump the load or give this water somewhere to go, the back-pressure will split the runner housing!"

"Open the bypass deflector!" Franco screamed over the mechanical shriek.

"If I open the deflector, we dump three hundred liters a second into the gorge and lose our head!" Matteo’s face was slick with a sheen of greasy sweat that was already beginning to freeze at his sideburns. "Once the penstock cools without flow, the ice skin thickens inside the conduit! If the pipe locks, the Cenisio head is dead until April!"

Lukas leaped over the conduit trench, his boots skidding on the slush tracked in from the track gang. He caught the rim of the municipal interconnect panel—the sealed iron locker painted the dull battleship gray of the old provincial rail authority, untouched since the privatization split in nineteen ninety-four.

"The local medium-voltage tie," Lukas yelled, his voice strained against the rising turbine howl. "The old municipal feeder still runs from this switchyard straight into the Bussoleno distribution ring. The breakers are manual oil-switches!"

Franco stared at him as if he’d pulled a grenade pin. "That’s non-isolated municipal grid! If we close that feeder, we take the entire residential load of the lower valley onto an untracked four-megawatt wheel! We’ll cook the stator windings in twenty seconds!"

"Not if we drop the rail switches entirely," Lukas shouted. "Forget the rail points! Let the switches freeze! Divert the entire Pelton output into the town ring! It won’t heat their water, it won’t run their ovens, but it’ll keep two thousand circulating pumps turning and fire their oil furnaces before the radiator pipes burst in the walls!"

"We don't have an order from the assembly!" Elio’s voice was high, pitched on the edge of hysteria. "The sortition panel certified the clinic and the tracks! We don't have three votes for the municipal grid! It violates the charter!"

"Then convene the panel right now," Lukas snapped, grabbing Elio’s canvas jacket and shoving him toward the brass clock on the wall. "Matteo! Franco! Pietro’s on the track! That’s three! Vote, damn you!"

Franco looked from the shaking turbine housing toward the absolute, pitch-black maw of the valley where seventy-five hundred people were waking up to the sudden silence of their radiators dying. A vein in the rail-boss’s temple pulsed against the grime of his forehead.

"Aye," Franco rasped, his voice dropping into his chest. "God forgive us when Enel’s lawyers get through with the paper. Aye."

"Aye," Matteo breathed, his hand already hauling back the mechanical trip lever on the municipal busbar.

"That's two," Lukas said. He didn't wait for Pietro to stumble in from the blizzard. "That's majority emergency certification under Section Four. Throw the interlock!"

Lukas slammed his shoulder into the six-foot lever of the manual oil-break switch. 

The resistance was colossal. The mechanical linkage had sat in its bath of thickened transformer oil for thirty years, sealed against the alpine damp. Lukas felt the cartilage in his good shoulder compress, his dead fingers slipping uselessly against the cold steel handle. He locked his jaw, his teeth grinding so hard the larch resin cracked like glass, and threw his full weight down with gravity behind him.

*CLANG-CHUNCK.*

The explosion of noise was not electrical, but hydraulic. 

Two miles of six-hundred-millimeter penstock shuddered inside the mountain rock. The sound traveled down the bedrock—a vast, subterranean groan like an iron bridge buckling under a freight train. Inside the runner casing, the water jets bit into the steel buckets under an instantaneous, violent demand for torque. The needle valves snapped wide. 

The scream of the Pelton dropped two full octaves into a guttural, earth-shaking roar. On the secondary panel, the line voltage dropped to a sickening dip—the needle fluttering near the red margin—before the heavy copper coils of the generator caught the alpine river’s weight and held.

Out in the dark expanse of Bussoleno, three miles down the gorge, a miracle of pale amber light flickered to life. 

It wasn't the brilliant blaze of the commercial grid. It was a faint, struggling glow—the low-voltage respiration of thousands of pilot lights, electronic furnace igniters, and low-draw emergency lamps catching current across two thousand freezing living rooms. The valley had not died. It was breathing through a single iron straw thrust into the Cenisio reservoir.

"We’re holding," Elio whispered, his hands trembling violently as he watched the governor needle hover three hairs above stall. "Sweet Christ, the wheel is holding it."

Then the optical laser transceiver mounted on the roof parapet began to shriek its loss-of-signal alarm.

A piercing, warbling electronic tone echoed through the high stone rafters of the powerhouse. On the wall-mounted communications terminal, the green synchronization carrier to the Swiss relay on Mount Rocciamelone flipped to a flat, dead crimson.

Lukas wiped freezing grease from his eyes and bolted up the iron stairs toward the observation deck, Matteo hobbling heavily behind him.

Outside, the snow was falling in dense, blinding curtains, each flake illuminated by the dying yellow drift of the flare above. Through a tear in the low alpine cloud deck, Lukas lifted his field glasses toward the sheer knife-edge ridge where the Cenisio penstock breached the rock face at two thousand meters.

Hovering five yards above the ice of the upper reservoir intake, the dark, unlit silhouette of a heavy-lift transport helicopter hung suspended in the rotor wash. 

Rappelling lines dropped from its open cargo bay, whipping against the iron catenary of the flume. Below them, on the narrow service catwalk that guarded the master head-gate—the single valve keeping the water falling into Lukas’s spinning turbine—three figures in white alpine oversuits dropped onto the steel grating. 

In the thermal bloom of their equipment packs, the blue, superheated lance of an oxy-kerosene cutting torch sparked to life, biting straight into the hydraulic control linkage of the main penstock gate.
