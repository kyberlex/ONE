The iron plate over the locomotive pit rattled, a fine shower of dried ballast and pine dust raining through the six-inch gap onto Giulia’s shoulders. Above them, the freight-train roar of the avalanche subsided into a vast, smothering stillness.

In the pitch black of the trench, forty people held their collective breath. The only sound was the wet, ragged wheezing of an elder three feet away and the rhythmic, uncontrollable drumming of Giulia’s own knuckles against her ribs. Her left hand was twitching again, the tendon jumping beneath the skin like a plucked wire. Whenever the sirens had echoed down the Corso Regina Margherita during the student sweeps in Turin, her father had pressed a heavy brass drafting compass into that same palm, forcing her fingers shut until the sharp brass points bit into her flesh. *Count the points, Giulia. Measure what’s real.* She had no compass now, only the icy lip of a stamped-steel first-aid box.

"Up," Matteo’s voice grated from the dark, stripped of breath. "The intake flume will choke if the drift packs the draft doors. Crowbars into the dog-latches. Slowly."

Giulia shoved upward alongside two valley track-workers. The deck-plate moved with an agonizing shriek of rusted iron, spilling an avalanche of powdery snow over their caps. Cold air slammed into the trench—clean, savage, carrying the sharp stink of pulverized slate and shredded larch bark.

They climbed out into a transformed world.

The roundhouse roof had held, but the snow had drifted five meters high against the southern masonry wall, blocking the bay doors completely. Through the shattered clerestory, the alpine sky had cleared to a brittle, terrifying indigo. The stars were diamond chips pinned to velvet. Outside the eastern portal, where the narrow-gauge spur curved toward the Cenisio pass, the snow lay unbroken, gleaming like poured salt under the moon.

"Comms are dead," Alexandre called down from the gantry, his breath pluming thick in the lantern light. He was shaking his head at the blackened infrared lens mounted on the window frame. "The blizzard scattered the beam. We’re getting eighty percent dropped signals from the peak relay. Valais can't see us."

"Forget the laser," Chiara said, her boots crunching over drifted snow as she inspected the floor drains. "Clear the snow from the turbine overflow bypass first. If the tailrace freezes, the head-pressure will blow the packings out of the pelton casing."

Giulia didn't join the shovel line. Her eyes were drawn to the blue shadows stretching across the rail cutting outside.

Something had moved along the upper embankment.

It wasn't the fluid, disciplined advance of AlpSec mountain mercenaries, nor the heavy silhouette of a tracked snowcat. It was an erratic, drunken hitch, like an animal dragging a broken leg through the drifts. A dark shape stumbled out of the scrub pines forty yards up the grade, rolled down the snowbank, and lay motionless against the derailed chassis of an empty ore car.

"Matteo," Giulia whispered, the word freezing instantly in the sub-zero draft.

The old machinist turned, spanner in hand. He squinted into the white glare, his jaw tightening. "Stay back from the portal. Could be a spotter drawing us into an open line of fire."

"Spotters don't crawl on their bellies in wool coats," Giulia said.

She grabbed an unlit battery lantern from the workbench and stepped through the gap in the broken wooden door before anyone could catch her arm. The cold hit her face like a wet sheet of lead. Her boots broke through the crust, sinking knee-deep into powder that offered zero purchase. The wind off the pass was dropping, but the ambient temperature was plummeting toward fifteen below; within ten strides, the moisture in her nostrils had frozen into sharp needles.

The figure didn't move as she approached.

He was face-down in the drift, one arm hooked over an iron rail tie as if trying to anchor himself to the mountain. He wore no gaiters, no technical alpine shell, no crampons. He was dressed in a tailored charcoal overcoat of fine city wool, soaked through and frozen stiff as sheet metal, the hem torn to shreds by brambles. His feet were wedged into low-cut leather dress brogues, the split leather coated in a thick carapace of gray ice.

Giulia dropped to her knees beside him, her gloves finding his shoulder. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

The man groaned, a thin, bubbling rattle. When she rolled him onto his side, the lantern light spilled across a face that looked barely twenty-five. His skin had the waxy, bloodless pallor of skimmed milk, his lips swollen and purple, crusted with dried blood where he had bitten through his lower lip to stay awake. One ear was solid white—frostbite, deep and severe.

"Nicht... nicht schießen," he mumbled in German, his eyelids fluttering open to reveal bloodshot, terrified gray irises. He switched immediately to broken, frantic Italian, his jaw chattering with a rhythmic clatter that sounded like dry sticks breaking. "Bitte... no police. No... no AlpSec."

"Nobody is shooting," Giulia said, keeping her voice level, stripped of panic, though her pulse hammered against her temples. "You're at the rail sheds. You're inside the commune perimeter. Who are you?"

Instead of answering, the young man clutched violently at his chest. His fingers, blackened at the tips from exposure, were locked into the heavy nylon straps of a commercial courier satchel cinched beneath his frozen coat. The webbing was reinforced with industrial zip-ties, cutting deep into his bruised collarbones.

"The registry," he gasped, spraying flecks of bloody spittle onto her coat. "The Frankfurt receiver... they signed the execution order on Tuesday. They didn't... they didn't file for restructuring. It’s an asset strip. Total liquidation."

Matteo and Lukas Keller came running up behind her, their heavy snowshoes kicking up clouds of crystals. Lukas instantly dropped his pack, taking one look at the young man’s feet and swearing under his breath.

"Get him inside," Lukas ordered, his voice clipped and urgent. "His core is cratering. If we leave him on this snow for three more minutes, his heart will slip into ventricular fibrillation. Matteo, take his legs. Don't bend the ankles—the boots are frozen to the socks."

They lifted him between them. The young man shrieked—a raw, animal sound of pure agony as the frozen fabric pulled against his flesh—but his blackened fingers never released their death-grip on the courier satchel.

Inside the warmth of the lower machine shop, away from the drafts of the main floor, Chiara had already cleared a workbench near the wood-burning drum stove. They stripped his ruined overcoat with shears, the stiffened wool cracking like cardboard under the blades. Beneath it, he wore a rumpled corporate suit, a thin silk tie still knotted tightly at his throat, soaked in sweat and melting snow.

From his inside breast pocket, an identification badge fell onto the grease-stained concrete.

Giulia picked it up. A laminate card from the *Vanguard Continental Debt Fund*, Mainzer Landstraße, Frankfurt. The photo showed a clean-shaven, pale young man with neatly parted blond hair and steel-rimmed spectacles, looking profoundly uncomfortable in his high-collared shirt.

*Florian Weber. Junior Risk Analyst – Distressed Alpine Municipalities.*

Florian lay on the bench, shivering so violently that the wooden legs rattled against the stone. Chiara had wrapped his torso in dry wool blankets, carefully elevating his blackened hands while Lukas worked with cold water compresses on his ears and feet, avoiding direct heat to keep the dead tissue from blistering.

"He walked from Bardonecchia," Lukas said grimly, examining the boy's split shoes on the floor. "Over the old military mule track. In town shoes. It’s eight miles through waist-deep drifts. He shouldn't be alive."

"Why?" Giulia asked, leaning over the bench. She held up the laminate badge. "Florian. Why did you walk through an avalanche warning for an abandoned locomotive shed?"

Florian opened his eyes. The delirium was receding slightly as the warmth reached his blood, replaced by a cold, lucid terror that Giulia recognized instantly. It was the look of a person who had seen the machine from the inside and realized there was no escape hatch.

"The debt covenants," Florian whispered, his teeth still clicking, but his voice gaining a desperate clarity. "The hydro-electric concession for this valley... it wasn't collateralized against the municipal grid. It was bundled into an off-balance-sheet special purpose vehicle in Luxembourg. They forged the local council’s counter-signatures two weeks before the winter freeze."

He nodded weakly toward the courier satchel.

"Open it," he breathed. "The yellow folder. And the brushed steel drives. The master keys are written in the margins of my tax filings. In Frankfurt... they told the tribunal that the valley defaulted on its sovereign infrastructure bond on the first of the month. But the payment clearinghouse never sent the notice. They blocked the routing codes intentionally."

Giulia used Matteo’s shears to snip the zip-ties. She hauled out a stack of dense, water-damaged legal briefs and two heavy, carbon-wrapped solid-state storage drives. She flipped the cover of the top docket. The stamp on the first page bore the blue seal of the Commercial Bankruptcy Tribunal of Frankfurt, alongside an emergency writ of seizure for every penstock, switch-yard, and sub-station between Modane and Turin.

"They're going to shut the flumes," Florian whispered, his eyes wide, staring at the soot-blackened ceiling. "They don't want the power. They need the failure. If the Cenisio penstock stays dry for seventy-two hours in this frost, the pipes will split from ice expansion. The repair costs will trigger the catastrophic clause. The entire watershed reverts to the creditor syndicate for one symbolic euro."

A silence fell over the workshop, broken only by the crackle of pine logs in the drum stove.

Matteo wiped grease from his forehead with the back of his canvas-taped hand, his weathered face darkening. "They aren't trying to evict us. They're trying to destroy the line so no one can ever run it again."

"And they know I took the audit mirrors," Florian said. He tried to sit up, but the pain in his feet made him gasp, his head falling back against the sheepskin roll. "They realized I was gone at six yesterday morning. They didn't call the police. You don't call the police when you run an illegal ledger."

Before Giulia could answer, the battery-operated field radio on the tool bench crackled to life, static hissing violently through the small speaker.

Alexandre’s voice broke through, tight, breathless, and stripped of all composure:

"Giulia. Matteo. Look down the gorge. Now."

Giulia crossed the shop floor in three strides, mounting the iron steps to the southern observation slot. She lifted Matteo’s heavy brass spotting scope to her eye, adjusting the frozen focus ring until the white expanse of the lower access road resolved into view.

Through the stand of snow-laden larches two kilometers below, twin high-intensity searchlights cut through the darkness. A heavy, tracked snow-interceptor was grinding up the switchbacks, its diesel engine a low, rhythmic vibration that Giulia felt through the soles of her boots.

Behind it came another. And another.

Mounted on the lead crawler’s roof was a directional sweep antenna, rotating slowly, homing in on the ridge.

Giulia pulled back from the sight, her breath catching in her throat. She looked down at Florian, whose eyes were fixed on the heavy winter boots Lukas had just cut from his feet.

Tucked into the hollowed heel of his left shoe, exposed by the shear-cut, a small rectangular casing was pulsing with a faint, intermittent red diode.

"Florian," Giulia said, the cold settling hard into her stomach. "Did your office issue those shoes?"

The young accountant looked at the blinking diode, the blood draining from what little color remained in his cheeks. "The corporate wellness package," he choked out, his voice cracking into absolute silence. "The alpine soles. They said... they said it was for insurance tracking."

Outside, the first crawler cleared the tree line, its halogen beams washing over the snow-covered walls of the roundhouse like cold morning sun.
