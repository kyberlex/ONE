The wind on the northern couloir did not blow; it hammered, dense with needle-sharp spindrift that found the seam between Matteo’s goggles and neoprene mask. Every step up the forty-degree scree slope drove a spike of cold through his right boot, straight into the arthritic knee he had carried since the derailment in ninety-eight. The joint clicked like a dry ratchet, grinding bone on worn cartilage. 

Behind him, tied into the same forty-meter braided nylon rope, Davide was breathing in ragged, shallow gasps. 

Matteo paused on a narrow ledge of frozen schist, leaning his forearm against the sheer rock face to steady his trembling quadriceps. He did not look down. Five hundred meters below, the floor of the Cenisio gorge was entirely smothered by the whiteout, though he could still picture the locomotive roundhouse crouched there like a stranded iron beetle. The sound of the rotary snowplow’s diesel engine had died away, swallowed by the roar of the gale funneling through the gorge. In its place remained only the vast, indifferent groan of the mountain.

"Matteo!" Davide shouted, his voice shredded by the storm. He dragged himself onto the shelf, his chest heaving against his heavy oiled-canvas anorak. "The radio... Alexandre says the head pressure is falling! Three bars every ten minutes!"

Matteo didn't waste oxygen on a sentence. He tapped the pressure gauge taped to his own forearm—a simple brass dial tapped into the high-altitude bypass line, its needle hovering dangerously near the red sector where cavitation would begin tearing the turbine runners apart down in the dark. 

If the water column inside the main penstock dropped below critical velocity, the sub-zero frost would seize the steel pipe from the outside in. Hundreds of tons of water would turn to solid ice in a matter of hours, bursting the riveted skin of the conduit and leaving the valley floor without a single watt of heat.

"Two hundred meters," Matteo yelled into the rim of Davide's hood. "The old stone intake. Move!"

They pushed upward into the throat of the couloir. 

Davide was nineteen, a former maintenance hand from the freight yards down in Bussoleno who had joined the roundhouse crew when the valley line was abandoned. He had an engineer’s eye for grade lines but had never climbed higher than the catenary gantries. Whenever fear took him, he had a habit of biting the inside of his lower lip until blood darkened the corners of his mouth. Matteo could see the dark smear through the frost on Davide's chin.

The path here was not a trail; it was a maintenance ledge blasted into the diorite during the late nineteen-twenties to service the bypass canal—an open masonry flume built to skim meltwater from the upper glacier before the lower reservoir had even been surveyed. The stone had been carved deep into the mountain’s hip, roofed over with rough granite lintels to protect it from spring avalanches.

When Matteo reached the lip of the flume terrace at twenty-two hundred meters, his right boot slipped on black ice. He dropped hard onto his bad knee. A dry, nauseating flare of pain shot up his spine, and he choked back a cough that tasted of iron and coal dust, his lungs burning with the old silicosis scar tissue. 

"Hold!" Davide barked, throwing his weight back into the slope, jamming his steel-tipped pick into a fissure to arrest the rope before Matteo could slide over the apron.

Matteo lay against the frozen scree for three heartbeats, his forehead pressed against the granite. His mittens were caked in snow, his breath coming in whistling wheezes. He forced himself up onto his elbows. 

"Good catch," Matteo grated, his voice raw.

"Don't do it again," Davide replied, teeth chattering violently.

The flume lay five meters ahead, half-buried under a fresh drift. It was a dark, square mouth in the cliffside, four feet wide, framed by moss-blackened blocks of stone. But no water rushed from its apron. Instead, a wall of translucent, greenish-blue anchor ice choked the portal entirely, sealing the intake like a plug of solid glass. 

Matteo dragged himself to the opening. Through the three-foot-thick sheet of ice, he could see the dark void of the channel. Somewhere deep within the mountain, fed by the subterranean warmth of the Rochemelon icefield, liquid water was pooling against the blockage, dammed up and dead.

"Propane," Matteo said, unbuckling the canvas harness that carried the ten-pound red tank on his back.

Davide unclipped the brass blowtorch wand from his pack, his fingers clumsy in their fleece-lined leather mittens. "Valve is frozen," the boy said, his voice rising in panic as he wrenched at the brass wheel. "It won't turn, Matteo! The regulator is caked in frost!"

Matteo didn't curse. Mountain air at fifteen below zero dropped the vapor pressure inside the cylinder to almost nothing; even if they cleared the regulator with a strike of a wrench, the cold would starve the flame within seconds. The laws of thermodynamics didn't care about their desperation.

"Put it away," Matteo said, his voice flat, stripped of everything but the next mechanical action. "Steel. Clear the drift."

They dropped to their knees, working with short trenching shovels to dig out the bottom lip of the masonry frame. The physical exertion brought heat back into Matteo’s arms, but his hands were going numb inside his mittens, the wet sweat on his palms beginning to pull heat directly from his skin. 

Beneath the snow, the condition of the weir was worse than he had feared. 

A heavy granite lintel, displaced by decades of frost heave and the weight of the latest drift, had sagged diagonally across the intake gate, wedging a three-ton slab of rock directly into the timber sluice frame. Behind it, the ice had formed a massive mechanical wedge, locking the stone and the oak shutter into a single immovable mass.

Davide unslung the eight-pound forged sledgehammer from his rucksack, while Matteo hauled out the two-foot hexagonal steel drift pins. 

Matteo positioned the tip of the first steel pin against the natural stress fracture where the green ice met the granite sill. "Strike here," Matteo said, holding the pin steady with his leather-clad hands, looking away to shield his eyes from flying chips. "Hard. Don't look at my fingers. Hit the steel."

Davide stood braced on the icy shelf, swung the sledge back over his shoulder, and brought it down.

The impact rang like a church bell, sending a violent shockwave through the pin that rattled Matteo’s teeth. A white circle of pulverized ice bloomed around the point, but the wedge barely bit half an inch. 

"Again!" Matteo yelled.

The sledge fell again. And again. The rhythmic *clack-clang* of steel on steel was the only sound against the shrieking gale. Sweat ran down Matteo’s neck, freezing instantly against the wool of his collar. His split thumb, bound in greasy canvas electrical tape, throbbed with a dull, sickening pulse under the pressure of his grip.

On the fifth strike, the steel pin sank three inches into the seam. A high-pitched, singing crack echoed from deep inside the conduit.

"Wait," Matteo commanded, raising a mittened hand.

He leaned his ear against the cold face of the ice. Through the dense mass, he heard it: a dull, rhythmic thumping, like a massive hydraulic pump working behind a closed door. The water was there. Thousands of cubic meters of sub-glacial runoff, backed up in the natural cavern behind the weir, were pressing against the ice plug with twenty meters of hydrostatic head.

A sudden burst of static hissed from the two-way radio clipped inside Matteo’s parka. Alexandre’s voice tore through the speaker, distorted and frayed by atmospheric snow interference.

"...Matteo... pressure at... points eight bar... runner vibrating... if the siphon breaks... the housing will shatter... do you copy? You have two minutes before... shut down..."

Matteo didn't answer. An answer cost seconds he didn't have. 

"The lever," Matteo told Davide. "The six-foot pinch bar. Get it under the lintel."

Davide dropped the sledge and scrambled to unstrap the heavy crowbar from the side of his frame pack. Together, they drove the chisel point of the bar into the hairline fracture beneath the sagging granite lintel. Matteo threw his hip and chest against the cold steel, while Davide climbed onto the outer end of the lever, using his entire body weight as a counterpoise.

The stone didn't budge. The frost held it like lead poured into a mold.

"It won't move!" Davide yelled, his boots slipping on the glazed rock, his face gray with exhaustion. "Matteo, it's pinned by the whole mountain!"

Matteo looked at the boy. Davide's lips were bleeding freely now, dark drops freezing against the wool of his scarf. His eyes were wide, dilated with the terrible, animal realization that human muscles were nothing against four hundred million years of granite.

"Look at the water line, Davide," Matteo said, his voice dropping into the quiet, terrifying calm he had learned forty years ago under the collapsed belly of a freight locomotive. "The mountain isn't holding it. The ice is. When I tell you to jump, you jump with all your weight. You don't hesitate."

Matteo let go of the lever, seized the sledgehammer from the snow, and stepped onto the slippery lip of the flume apron. He raised the eight-pound hammer high above his head, every fiber of his arthritic knee screaming under the unbalanced load. He aimed not at the rock, but at the steel pin buried deep in the heart of the ice plug, right at the fulcrum where the trapped water was straining to break free.

"Now!" Matteo roared.

Davide leaped into the air and brought both boots down onto the end of the pinch bar. 

At the exact same instant, Matteo swung the sledgehammer downward with every ounce of strength remaining in his ruined shoulders.

The hammer struck the head of the drift pin with a deafening crack.

For a fraction of a second, nothing happened. Then, a sharp sound like a rifle shot snapped through the couloir. A spiderweb of bright white fissures detonated across the face of the green ice. 

The three-ton granite lintel shifted half an inch, grinding against the bedrock with a screech that vibrated through the soles of their boots. Behind it, the trapped hydrostatic head found the crack. A jet of dark, near-freezing water hissed through the fissure like high-pressure steam, catching Matteo across the chest and knocking him flat onto his back on the frozen apron.

"Matteo!" Davide screamed.

Beneath them, the entire rock shelf gave a sickening, hollow lurch. The ancient masonry beneath their feet groaned as the pressure of the glacial pool began to violently tear the remaining ice plug from the inside out, and the edge of the stone apron started to shear away into the abyss.
