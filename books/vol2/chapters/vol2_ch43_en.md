The whine in the shaft rose to a thin, glass-cracking pitch.

Lukas didn’t wait for the bone to tear. He twisted his pinned left arm hard against the ascender’s cam, using the climber’s own harness tension as a pivot, and drove his steel-toed right heel into the man’s knee brace. 

The joint yielded with a dull, plastic pop. The climber hissed through his neoprene balaclava, clutching his thigh as his balance went out over the sheer drop. For half a second, the commando’s weight hung entirely from the jammed teeth biting through Lukas’s glove. Lukas grabbed the climber’s tactical chest rig with his free right hand, yanked him inward toward the catwalk grating, and punched the manual release tab on the ascender housing with his thumb.

The cam slipped. Flesh and heavy glove leather ripped free together, warm and sudden in the freezing draft.

Lukas slammed against the galvanized handrail, cradling his mangled fingers against his ribcage. The skin had gone pale gray where the teeth had chewed through to the tendon, but the frostbite numbness took the sharpest edge off the shock, leaving only a hollow, throbbing pressure that pulsed behind his ears.

“Down!” Lukas barked, grabbing the radio clipped to his shoulder harness. His voice cracked with the sulfurous grit blowing up from the vent. “Matteo! The intake draft is reversing! The charge is five hundred meters down, wedged on the primary baffle! They’re cutting through the low inspection adit to reach the siding!”

Four hundred meters below, buried in the damp granite spine between the hydro-flume and the rail tunnel, Matteo Rossi heard the speaker squawk through a shower of static.

He didn't answer with words. He jammed the talk button twice with his taped thumb—*click-click*—acknowledging the call without wasting oxygen his lungs couldn't spare.

The air inside the manual switch-gallery smelled of thirty years of lime dust, oxidized brake shoes, and wet slate. Matteo leaned his weight against the iron pedestal of the diversion lever, his right knee throbbing where the scar tissue from the ’98 derailment caught against the joint. The cold down here wasn't the clean, needles-and-glass frost of the mountain pass; it was a heavy, stagnant chill that seeped up through the ballast stones and turned the diesel grime on his overalls into stiff, waxy armor.

Through the narrow slit of the blast-glass observation port, Matteo watched the lower inspection siding. 

Two pairs of halogen headlights cut through the gloom of the rock-cut gallery. The AlpSec extraction buggy—a low-slung, battery-powered maintenance chassis retrofitted with run-flat guide wheels—was barreling down the narrow-gauge rail from the shaft drain, tires screeching against the wet concrete shoulders as its flanged steel wheels bit the track. Four men clung to the roll cage, their night-vision gear glowing dull green, helmets spattered with gray slurry from the intake blow-back.

Behind them, deep in the mountain's throat, a muffled thump shook the floor. 

It wasn't a shattering detonation. The baffle concrete had held, but the overpressure had blown the water traps. A rolling piston of compressed air and dead steam roared out of the drainage culverts, chasing the speeding buggy down the three-percent grade toward the main Bussoleno gallery where three hundred valley residents were still staging blankets and soup cans behind the timber bulkheads.

If that buggy blew through the siding gate at forty kilometers an hour, the shockwave would carry the fuel fog straight into the shelter.

Matteo didn't have a rifle. He didn't have an ancestral trinket or a prayer. What he had was the muscle memory of sixteen thousand hours working the Bussoleno classification yard, where runaway coal hoppers had to be caught by hand before they sheared the passenger crossovers. In the winter of ’84, when the pneumatic yard lines had frozen solid, he had learned the exact weight of deceleration by spending six weeks running the manual sprag levers with a grease-stained notebook tucked into his collar.

He checked the chalk mark he had dragged across the lever frame ten minutes earlier.

The arresting rig wasn’t a weapon; it was pure Newtonian arithmetic. Suspended from the arch of the inspection vault hung an eighty-ton deadweight sled—two decommissioned ballast hoppers loaded with washed river gravel, linked by triple-braided maritime mooring cables to a woven steel apron resting flat beneath the tracks. 

The buggy hit the eighty-meter approach marker. The electric whine of its twin motors screamed against the down-grade. The driver saw the locked siding switches ahead and cut his wheel hard toward the service apron, banking on the maintenance turnout to skirt the timber barricade.

*“Now, old man,”* Matteo whispered to his own stiff tendons.

He kicked the mechanical dog out of the quadrant notch.

The primary trip-arm dropped. 

A deafening clatter of counterweight chain unspooled through the ceiling sheaves. Overhead, thirty tons of lead-shot counterbalances plunged down their vertical grease-tubes, snapping the steel trip-wires taut.

Across the entrance to the inspection siding, the steel mesh net didn't fall from above—it whipped up from the ballast bed like a striking serpent. 

The heavy, double-woven wire lattice snapped vertical, locked into spring-loaded stanchions on either side of the rock cut. The front bumper of the extraction buggy hit the dead center of the net at thirty-five kilometers an hour.

The impact was not an explosion; it was the ferocious, grinding scream of friction.

The net didn't break. As the buggy’s front chassis deformed against the heavy mesh, the trailing steel cables hauled the two eighty-ton gravel sleds across the steel friction plates mounted on the gallery floor. Sparks cascaded in ten-meter curtains through the blackness, lighting the rock vault with jagged, strobing orange flashes. The sound was an unholy shriek of steel chewing steel as the sleds dragged across the sacrificial oak ties.

The buggy’s rear wheels lifted two feet off the rails, tires smoking as the inertia was swallowed, foot by foot, by eighty tons of moving river stone.

Ten meters from the timber barricade that sealed the civilian staging bay, the buggy shuddered to a dead, absolute halt.

The frame was twisted, the roll cage dented inward, but the passenger compartment remained entirely intact. The four contractors were thrown forward against their four-point harnesses, helmets cracking against the padded crossbars, wind knocked from their lungs in a simultaneous, ragged gasp. The buggy's headlights flickered, sputtered, and died, leaving only the dull red glow of cooling steel along the friction plates.

In the cab of the switch-tower, the lever kicked back with a violent recoil that Matteo absorbed with the heel of his palm, his bad knee buckling slightly under the lateral shove. He caught the handrail, coughing hard until the taste of iron filled his mouth, his ribs aching from the damp draft.

He wiped his mouth with the back of his canvas-taped thumb.

Silence fell over the siding, thick and sudden, broken only by the hiss of leaking battery coolant and the steady, rhythmic *drip-drip* of condensation falling from the tunnel crown onto the hot metal of the net.

The radio crackled on the shelf beside the brass lever quadrant.

“Matteo.” Lukas’s voice was thin, ragged, struggling through the wheeze of the intake draft above. “The blast... did it vent?”

Matteo picked up the handset, thumbing the lever with a thumb that shook only slightly. “Baffles caught the worst of it. Air piston blew out the drain culverts. Siding’s holding. Buggy is wedged in the net forty yards short of the timber bulkhead.”

A pause on the line. Lukas drew a sharp, whistling breath through his teeth. “The men on it?”

“Alive,” Matteo said, looking down through the blast glass. The contractor in the passenger seat was groaning, his gloved hands fumbling weakly at the quick-release buckle of his harness. The other three were slumped against the webbing, dazed, their rifles pinned harmlessly under the collapsed steel mesh that had wrapped itself around the hood like a wet canvas tarp. “Bruised ribs. Sprained wrists, most likely. They aren't going anywhere until we bring the torch to cut the cable.”

“Understood,” Lukas breathed. The sound of metal scuffing rock came through the small speaker as he pulled himself along the upper gantry. “Swiss federal transits are holding at the crest. The laser line to Valais just locked back on sixty percent parity. But Matteo...”

Matteo stopped, his hand resting on the chilled iron of the arrestor lever. “What is it?”

“The intake chimney isn’t clear,” Lukas said, his voice dropping into the hollow flat tone of a man reporting an avalanche he can hear but cannot see. “The commando sergeant—he dropped his personal locator beacon before he went over the lower baffle. It’s pinging an emergency asset-recovery frequency straight into the military band at Susa.”

Through the rock walls, so faint it could only be felt through the soles of Matteo’s boots, the high-pressure diesel horns of the heavy recovery locomotives down at the valley marshaling yard began to blow.
