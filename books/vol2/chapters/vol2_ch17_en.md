The climbing skins hissed against the hard wind-crust, a dry, rhythmic rasp that Davide timed to the thud of his pulse. 

Every thirteen strides, he shifted the balance off his left heel. It was an old habit, an instinct bred of metal: three surgical pins held his tibia together just above the ski boot’s plastic cuff, a souvenir from a downhill training crash at Sestriere when he was seventeen. When the air dropped past fifteen below zero, the steel inside his leg turned into an icicle, radiating a dull, thrumming ache into his marrow. To kill the nausea of the cold, he chewed on dried larch resin he’d scraped off the bark near the tree line, its bitter turpentine tang keeping his tongue from sticking to the roof of his mouth.

Behind him, Nina didn’t break stride. She was eighteen, two years younger than Davide, her frame wiry and compact under three layers of boiled wool and windproof ripstop. Before the commercial freight routes across the Maurienne were choked off by bankruptcy liens and border checkpoints, she had been a junior ski-mountaineering champion in Modane. Now, she moved like a pack mule on the steep grade of the Col de la Roue, her breath coming in short, disciplined snorts through a fleece neck gaiter caked white with frozen spit.

Between the two of them, their mountaineering rucksacks weighed seventy-eight kilos. 

Neither carried a weapon. Their ordnance was sealed against their kidneys in insulated neoprene sleeves, warmed by the raw friction of their own sweat: forty-eight ampoules of liquid cephalosporin and amoxicillin, nestled in sheep’s fleece so the glass wouldn’t clink, and thirty tins of infant formula Davide had packed into waterproof dry-bags until his knuckles bled. If the antibiotic vials froze at two thousand six hundred meters, the suspension would crystalize, the glass would shatter, and the infants coughing their lungs out in the damp air of the locomotive roundhouse in Bussoleno wouldn't make it to Tuesday.

"Hold," Davide murmured, halting at the crest of a wind-scoured moraine. The gale coming off the Massif du Thabor caught his shoulder, shoving him half an inch toward the cornice. 

Nina pulled up beside him, planting her carbon poles with a muted crunch. Her goggles were clear double-lens Lexan, but frost had rimmed the foam vents, framing her dark, squinting eyes in an oval of ice. 

"Ridge is clear?" she asked. Her voice was flat, stripped of inflection by the roar of the wind.

Davide didn't answer immediately. He reached into his chest pocket, his fingers stiff inside their heavy leather mittens, and pulled out a small optical monocular. He focused the lens down into the gouged trough of the high valley on the Italian side. 

Four kilometers east, where the rail line cut through the granite cliffs above Bardonecchia, the night should have been pitch black. Instead, two sodium floodlights burned at the mouth of the service tunnel, casting long, jaundiced daggers of amber across the fresh snow. 

"They moved the checkpoint up from the highway," Davide said, squinting through the glass. "That's not the Carabinieri. Green chassis. Twin-track utility rig with an auxiliary plow."

"AlpSec," Nina spat, wiping a crumb of frost from her lower lip. "They aren't guarding the rails. They’re watching the avalanche chutes. They know nobody’s stupid enough to walk through the Frejus tunnel on foot."

"They know we’re coming," Davide corrected. He lowered the glass, his jaw tightening until the larch resin cracked between his molars. "The nursery in the roundhouse ran out of dry feed yesterday noon. Signora Ferrero wouldn't have called the French co-op over the laser mesh if there were another way. The whole valley heard the packet bursts bounce off Rocciamelone before the clouds rolled in."

Nina glanced up into the swirling gray murk overhead. The mountain was entirely invisible, swallowed by a thermal inversion that turned the sky into a churning ceiling of slate. Somewhere up on that three-thousand-meter peak, an infrared laser was desperately pulsing digital manifests through the snowstorm, losing half its light to atmospheric scattering, trying to synchronize the valley’s ledger with the Swiss trusts in Valais. 

Down here, physics didn't care about ledgers. Down here, physics was sixty-five liters of dry powder pressing into Davide’s lumbar spine, three frozen titanium screws in his shin, and the fact that a human body at this altitude burned four thousand calories a day just staying warm enough not to die.

"We don't take the summer path," Nina said. It wasn't a question.

"The summer path is scrubbed clean by the wind," Davide replied, pointing his ski pole south toward a jagged cleft where the mountain ridge broke like a shattered tooth. "Hard blue ice. We’d have to crampon down the first three hundred meters. In this wind, carrying forty kilos each, we’d blow off the face and land in the gravel pits by midnight."

"The Couloir du Loup, then."

Davide looked at her. Her face was deadpan, but the muscle along her jaw was twitching. 

The Couloir du Loup was a forty-degree throat of loose powder boxed in by twin granite ribs. It was fast, steep, and completely sheltered from the border cameras. It was also an avalanche funnel. Two seasons ago, a slide there had swept a maintenance hut clean off its concrete pilings, carrying the cedar timbers all the way to the valley floor.

"Skins off," Davide said. 

He didn't wait for an argument. He stepped out of his rear heel-locks, knelt in the knee-deep drift, and ripped the mohair skins from the bases of his skis with two violent, practiced jerks. The sticky glue hissed as it peeled away. He stuffed the frozen skins directly inside his jacket to keep the adhesive pliable against his ribs, his teeth chattering uncontrollably as the sub-zero draft invaded his base layers.

Beside him, Nina did the same, her small, gloved hands working with mechanical precision. She didn't complain about the cold. Her father had spent twenty-eight years fixing high-head water valves for the French national grid; she knew that the moment you started feeling sorry for your fingers was the moment you dropped a binding screw into sixty centimeters of untracked snow.

"Keep fifty meters between us," Davide said, his breath pluming thick and gray between them. "If I trigger a wind slab, you don't chase me. You turn into the granite wall and dig your edges into the bedrock. If the packs get buried, the glass breaks. Understand?"

"I'm not digging you out twice this winter, Davide," she muttered, snapping her boots back into the downhill pins with a sharp, metallic *clack*. "Move."

Davide stood, adjusted the chest harness that clamped the formula tins against his sternum, and pushed over the lip.

Gravity took him instantly. 

The wind dropped away the second he dropped beneath the rim of the col, replaced by the violent, tearing rush of slipstream air against his helmet. The snow was knee-deep, heavy and slab-bound from the morning's drift. His skis cut through it with a muffled hiss, his quads instantly screaming as he dropped his center of gravity to absorb the brutal, uneven chop beneath the surface.

Left turn. The titanium pins in his shin screamed as the boot tongue drove into his lower leg. 

Right turn. The rucksack lurched, its dead weight threatening to yank his shoulders backward into the snow. He leaned forward, fighting the leverage, forcing his tips down into the fall line. Behind him, he heard the sharp, rhythmic *shhk-shhk* of Nina’s shorter racing turns, tight and controlled in his wake.

They were four hundred meters down the throat, entering the narrowest choke of the couloir where the granite pinched to less than twelve meters across, when Davide saw the wire.

It wasn't an avalanche fence. 

Stretched taut between two steel eye-bolts drilled into the rock faces on either side of the couloir was a braided steel winch cable, suspended four feet above the snow line—chest-height for a skier running the fall line at fifty kilometers an hour. It had been painted matte gray, invisible against the granite until his eyes caught the tiny, shivering teardrops of rime ice hanging from its underside.

Fifty meters ahead. Closing at fifteen meters a second.

A mechanical interlock. The private patrol hadn't bothered chasing them up the mountain; they had simply locked the couloir’s gate from below.

"Wire!" Davide roared, the word torn from his throat and lost instantly to the mountain. 

He didn't have room to hockey-stop; the slab snow under his edges would shear instantly, sweeping both of them into the cable like sheep through a wire slicer. 

He dug his uphill edges into the crust, throwing his entire weight into a violent, sideways skid, his trailing hand reaching back to slap Nina’s ski tip as she dropped out of the spray. 

"Drop!" he screamed.

Davide unweighted his hips, threw his body sideways onto the frozen crust, and let the sheer momentum of his pack drag him into the snow, sliding belly-down on the ice as the steel cable flashed toward his face.
