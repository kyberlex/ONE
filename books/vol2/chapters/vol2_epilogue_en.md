The dark did not fall; it struck. 

When the high catenary severed above track three, the snap cracked through the valley like a naval gun, dropping forty yards of live copper into the fresh powder. It whipped through the drifts with a furious, spitting hiss, showering the gravel in emerald sparks before dying into silence. Above them, the Cenisio headrace was gone. The rhythmic shudder that had vibrated through the marshaling yard’s frozen ballast for five days ceased entirely, leaving only the thin, dry scream of the wind pouring down from the glacier.

Three miles up the gradient, the twin red marker lamps of the armored carrier burned through the squall, squatting directly across the mainline portal like a dead weight.

"Battery isolation!" Matteo barked into the cab. He didn't shout out of fear; his voice was a rasp honed by coal grit and early silicosis, scraping hard against the back of his throat. His thumb, wrapped tight in blackened canvas tape where the skin had split to the meat, shoved the manual disconnect lever down until the brass dogs seated.

Under their boots, deep in the locomotive’s welded steel belly, the secondary cell banks answered. There was no thunderous surge, no whistle of steam—only the low, heavy drone of solid lithium-iron slabs waking in the cold. It was the sound of stored labor, gathered trickle by trickle from the mountain before the flume was cut.

"The portal’s plugged," Davide whispered. The apprentice was nineteen, his face smudged with molybdenum grease, his hands shaking inside wool mittens that had worn through at the thumbs. He smelled of cold sweat and stale chicory coffee. "Matteo, AlpSec has thirty tons of plate iron sitting on the frog."

"They’re on the high line," Matteo said, wiping a rim of frost from the cab glass with the greasy heel of his glove. His right knee gave an audible click as he leaned his weight against the reverser. "They don't know the spur to the old avalanche gallery still has points. Pietro—get down on the ballast. Hand-crank the four-way switch before their spotter picks up the motion."

Pietro didn't speak. The retired rail machinist had spent forty-two years listening to the iron of the Fréjus line complain under frost; he simply pulled his rabbit-fur flaps down, grabbed a two-foot steel bar from the tool bench, and stepped backward into the blizzard without a word.

Giulia’s fingers were hooked so tightly into the torn lining of her wool coat that her knuckles ached. In her left pocket, the river stone from the Cenisio streambed was slick with her palm's sweat. Beside her, Chiara Leone adjusted the strap of her canvas medical kit, her face gray with three nights of hypothermia triage, her breathing shallow and measured. 

The brass drum on the platform sat empty now. The odd parity held: three drawn by lot—Davide, Chiara, Pietro—and Giulia to carry the manifest. Four valley lives, an indivisible balance against the deadweight of the old world.

"Clear!" Pietro's voice came from the dark below, muffled by snow. A dull, mechanical *clack-thud* traveled through the frame as the ancient iron switch-blades ground through an inch of frozen mud and locked.

Matteo didn't touch the horn. He eased the notch controller forward one click. The open-source traction inverters groaned, a high-frequency whine that vibrated through their teeth, and forty tons of modified Swiss mountain shunter crept forward into the dark, slipping off the illuminated mainline and into the unlit, dripping bore of the gallery.

Behind them, the ten thousand voices of the Susa encampment did not cheer. They stood in the freezing murk along the tracks, their breath rising in a unified white bank of steam against the red glare of the blocked portal. They simply watched the blue electric silhouette of the shunter disappear into the mountain, holding the line with their bodies so the valley could not be swallowed from behind.

***

The gallery was a tomb of weeping shale and dripping limestone. For four miles, the only light came from the dull amber diode on the locomotive’s instrument shelf. 

Friction rode with them on every wheel revolution. Outside the heavy cab walls, the temperature hovered at sixteen below zero. The air lines to the air brakes kept weeping condensation that threatened to freeze the valves shut; every twelve minutes, Davide had to crouch in the unheated gangway with a copper mallet, tapping the drain cocks until a spit of slush hissed into the dark.

"Laser link is fluttering," Chiara said, her eyes fixed on the flat telemetry slate bolted above the throttle. The little green dot, routed through the high free-space optical lens on the peak of Rocciamelone, pulsed erratically. "Snow density is eating forty percent of the beam. Geneva is asking for the ledger hash."

"Let it drop," Giulia said, her voice steadying as the rhythm of the rails took hold of her blood. The terror that usually gripped her throat at the thought of closed doors—the phantom stink of Le Vallette's holding cells—receded before the sheer physical enormity of the mountain pressing down on the roof. "We don't transmit the balance until the frontier is under our feet. If they intercept the signatures before we clear the cantonal post, the commercial court in Frankfurt will have a writ on the Swiss tracks before breakfast."

Pietro climbed back up into the cab, smelling of creosote and sulfur matches. He wedged himself into the corner beside the handbrake wheel, pulling an old mechanical pocket watch from his waistcoat. The glass was cracked across the face.

"Two miles to the border culvert," Pietro muttered, his rheumy eyes watching the tie-markers flicker past the lower footplate. "Swiss federal border guards won't shoot. But they’ve got three diesel snowplows sitting at Saint-Gingolph. If the cantonal prosecutor has signed the asset-freeze order, they’ll derail us into the gravel traps before we see the water."

Matteo didn't look back from the windshield. The wipers were struggling, shuddering against an inch of packed rime. "The Swiss haven't signed it."

"How do you know?" Davide asked, his mallet resting against his thigh, his breath puffing in quick white bursts.

"Because Favre is in Martigny," Matteo said grimly. "And Favre doesn't sleep."

***

They broke out of the northern portal forty minutes later, not into sunlight, but into the vast, slate-blue twilight of the Valais. 

The storm had broken here into long, ragged streamers of mountain fog drifting through the pines. Below them, five thousand feet down through the tears in the cloud, Lake Geneva lay like an immense sheet of beaten lead, flat and cold and ancient.

On the instrument panel, the optical link suddenly went solid. 

The green dot flared to brilliant emerald. The line-of-sight laser had found the alpine relay on the Grand Combin. A single burst of data chirped through the cab’s audio terminal—a packet no larger than a grain of sand, yet carrying the whole five-day accounting of the Susa Valley: every kilowatt-hour scraped from the glacier, every sack of shared flour, every rail-spike driven under the mercenary searchlights.

And then, other signals began to seed the screen.

Chiara leaned forward, her raw, chapped fingers hovering over the glass. "Look."

From the west, a low-bandwidth packet pinged through an air-freight relay: *Detroit Node. Water board receivership broken. Twelve sortition delegates airborne over the Atlantic.*

From the south: *Andean Hydrological Union. Upper Marañón flumes restored under communal lock. Ten delegates clearing customs in Marseille.*

And from the east, faint and fragmented through maritime satellite cut-throughs: *Malacca Strait Tanker Assembly. Four container-hulled power stations anchored off Singapore under mutual charter. Three delegates traveling by rail across the Anatolian corridor.*

They were not alone. The world was not empty. The small fire lit in the freezing mud of Susa was only one hearth in a continent of embers waking beneath the ash.

The grade steepened as the locomotive hit the lakeside descent. Matteo cut the traction power entirely, letting gravity pull the forty tons of steel down toward the water. The regenerative brakes hummed, feeding their own momentum back into the floorboards, warming the cab by three fragile degrees.

At kilometer ninety-eight, the track swung around the rocky shoulder of the cliffs near Bouveret. 

The lake opened before them in its full, terrifying breadth. Two miles offshore, the gray spires of the United Nations complex at Geneva were invisible under the low ceiling of mist, but the shoreline road was choked.

Giulia pressed her hand against the cold side-window. 

The border crossing was not manned by the familiar green wool of the Swiss cantonal police. Across the railbed, half a mile ahead where the tracks met the lakeside quay, three matte-black mobile command centers bearing the crest of the European Financial Stability Authority were parked hub-to-hub across the ballast. Beyond them stood sixty tactical marshals in gray high-altitude gear, their riot shields locked into an unbroken wall of polycarbonate across the rails. 

Behind the shield wall, an array of vehicle-mounted directional searchlights struck the locomotive's cab, blinding them in an instantaneous, freezing glare of ten thousand candlepower.

On the roof of the center carrier, a hydraulic ram hissed as a heavy steel derailment wedge was winched into place across the left-hand rail.

Inside the cab, the radio terminal screeched once—a dry, legalistic voice cutting through the static in flat administrative French:

*"Locomotive 402. You are operating an uncertified traction unit subject to cross-border maritime lien and judicial sequestration under the Frankfurt Commercial Protocol. Cut your air brakes immediately. If you pass the signal gantry at the water's edge, your kinetic energy will be arrested by mechanical interlock."*

Matteo did not reach for the brake lever. His hand stayed clamped on the dead-man's pedal, his knuckles dead white beneath the dirty canvas tape.

"Giulia," he said, not turning his head. "The manifest."

Giulia took the heavy roll of paper from her coat. It was not digital. It was three hundred yards of linen-rag printout, stamped with five thousand thumbprints of walnut oil and carbon ink, signed by the odd-numbered assemblies of thirty-two mountain valleys.

Out on the gray water of the lake, three distinct signal flares—one copper green, one deep river red, one brilliant maritime white—rose through the fog, curving upward toward the clouds.

"Davide," Giulia said, her tremors vanishing as she pulled the heavy steel cab latch open to the freezing wind. "Dump the sanders. Matteo—notch three."
