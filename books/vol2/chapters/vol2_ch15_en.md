The zinc roof of the locomotive roundhouse shuddered beneath the downdraft of the north wind, rattling three thousand square feet of frosted corrugated iron like a loose drumskin. 

Alexandre Favre pressed the heels of his hands into his eye sockets, trying to crush the migraine blooming behind his right optic nerve. The headache had the texture of broken bottle glass, grinding each time the pale halogen work lights fluctuated. Around him, the cavernous maintenance shed smelled of half a century of cold grease, spilled battery fluid, and the raw, fungal earth they had trucked into the inspection pits to bed the aeroponic roots. Two dozen valley residents moved between the raised wooden beds, stringing salvaged irrigation lines with hands swollen red from the damp chill. No one spoke above a whisper. Everyone moved with the brittle caution of people whose fuel oil had run out three days ago.

On the workbench before him, the mechanical chart recorder began to click. 

Alexandre pulled his hands away from his face. The spring-driven brass arm was jerking erratically, its inked stylus dragging violently across the paper drum. Hydrostatic pressure from the upper Cenisio intake was oscillating—sharp, spiking dips followed by savage pulses of back-pressure. 

High up on the rock apron, Matteo and the boy had hit the ice. Or the ice had taken the rock.

Before Alexandre could reach for the field telephone, the ruggedized sat-relay terminal on the corner of the bench emitted a flat, double-tone chirp. 

It was not the short, chirping ping of the local mesh network. It was an encrypted commercial trunk line, forcing its way through an unlisted Frankfurt gateway via an emergency maritime satellite link. The screen did not show a name, only a routing prefix that Alexandre had spent eight years of his adult life trying to erase from his retina: *VCF-DE-CORP-EXEC-01*.

His thumb hovered over the cutoff toggle. His pulse drummed against the tight tendons of his throat. He had not heard that digital signature since the restructuring meetings in Geneva four years ago, when the fund had liquidated three municipal water concessions with the stroke of a fountain pen.

He picked up the handset and clamped the hard plastic shell against his ear.

"You're thirty seconds from losing line-of-sight to the commercial transponder, Alexandre."

The voice had not aged. It carried neither anger nor triumphant malice. It possessed only the dry, climate-controlled stillness of a corner suite overlooking the river Main, where the hum of high-efficiency particulate air filters muted the sirens of Frankfurt below.

"Luc," Alexandre said. The cold air burned his throat as he spoke the name.

"You have soot on your collar, little brother. I don't need a satellite photograph to know that. You always smelled of cold iron when you were running away from something."

Alexandre rubbed the ridge of his nose, feeling the sharp indentations left by the wire frame of his glasses. As boys in the Haute-Savoie, whenever their father’s timber mill had slid closer to receivership, Luc had been the one who methodically ironed their school shirts with a damp linen cloth, insisting that an uncreased placket was the only barrier between a respectable family and the gutter. Luc had believed in solvency the way medieval monks believed in the Holy Trinity: as an absolute metaphysical armor against the chaos of the world.

"How did you route this line?" Alexandre asked, keeping his voice level.

"The fund owns thirty-four percent of the orbital transponder leasing syndicate, Alex. Don't ask questions beneath your intellect. We have forty-two minutes before the Turin Commercial Tribunal enters the default decree on the rail corridor's traction assets."

A dry rustle of bond paper came through the speaker. Luc was turning a page. In the background, the discreet chime of an elevator arriving on an executive floor registered with crystalline clarity.

"I’m looking at your European Arrest Warrant draft," Luc continued, his cadence steady, measured, almost tender. "Section Four: endangerment of critical cross-border energy corridors. It’s signed by the deputy prosecutor in Susa, but it’s sitting in my inbox for pre-clearing. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means your risk committee is worried about sovereign blowback if you seize the rails while the valley is freezing," Alexandre said.

A brief, pitying sigh traveled down six hundred miles of copper and orbit. 

"It means I haven't clicked *transmit* to the federal police in Rome, Alex. Because if I click transmit, you aren't an errant engineer doing unauthorized civil defense in a derelict shed. You are a designated infrastructure saboteur. You will spend five years in Le Vallette before your counsel even gets a hearing on bail conditions. You have no legal foundation that can withstand a cross-border debt attachment."

"We filed the public trust deed under the Valais registry yesterday," Alexandre said, his fingers tightening on the receiver until the plastic creaked. "Article Eighty. Perpetual asset lock. The water rights belong to the municipal basin, Luc. Not the bondholders."

"The Swiss notary who stamped your filing was suspended by the cantonal oversight board two hours ago," Luc said smoothly. "His license is under administrative review for irregular handling of distressed assets. Your filing is dead paper, Alexandre. It has no standing in an Italian commercial chamber."

The air in the roundhouse seemed to drop another three degrees. Across the floor, a young mother wrapped in two woolen coats was tucking a five-year-old into an insulated railway crate lined with straw. The child was coughing, that dry, hollow rasp that came after forty-eight hours of breathing damp, unheated air.

"Listen to me," Luc said, his voice dropping into the quiet register he had used when Alexandre was twelve and had broken his collarbone falling from the hayloft. "There is a civilian registration Eurocopter sitting at the airfield in Chambéry. It has clearance for an emergency humanitarian transit to Geneva. The pilot has your name on the manifest. In two hours, you can be across the border. No warrants. No tribunal summons. A private advisory desk at the Zurich infrastructure fund. Twelve months of technical analysis on municipal heat recovery, completely severed from this mess. Your debts in Milan will be settled out of our regional restructuring contingency."

Alexandre closed his eyes. The migraine throbbed like a dull axe blade striking bone. Zurich. A heated room. Clean sheets that didn't smell of diesel exhaust and wet concrete. Hot water coming out of a brass tap without having to calculate whether the Pelton wheel had enough head to keep the resistance coils from burning out.

"And the valley?" Alexandre asked.

"The valley will enter standard judicial administration," Luc replied without missing a beat. "The provincial gas grid will be restored as soon as the receivership team takes physical custody of the intake flume and rail switches. Emergency fuel tankers will be dispatched under Carabinieri escort by midnight. Order will be re-established. The people will be warm, Alex. Not because of a fantasy cooked up in an abandoned depot by a dozen insomniacs, but because the European clearing mechanism works. It always works, if you stop throwing gravel into the gears."

"You cut the gas on Tuesday, Luc."

"The municipal distributor failed its liquidity ratio by sixteen percent!" Luc’s tone hardened, the polished executive veneer finally cracking to reveal the granite underneath. "Do you think energy is free? Do you think the Norwegian offshore platforms pump gas on good intentions? If we don't enforce contract settlements, the entire secondary bond market for regional utilities collapses. If that collapses, seven million pensioners in Bavaria and Lombardy don't receive their winter stipends. You think you're defending human dignity? You're defending a romantic collapse into the fourteenth century!"

Alexandre looked down at the chart recorder. The brass arm had stopped jerking. It had leveled out—not at the baseline of failure, but climbing steadily up the grid. 

Matteo had done it. The ice had shattered. The dark water of the high Cenisio was pouring down the 600-meter vertical drop of the steel penstock right now, hitting the runners of the turbine in the lower chamber. Beneath the concrete floor, through the soles of Alexandre's salt-stained work boots, a deep, sub-audible vibration began to hum. It wasn't the clean, silent perfection of a Frankfurt trading floor; it was the heavy, thumping, iron-hearted torque of seventy tons of water meeting nineteenth-century steel.

"You never understood the math, Luc," Alexandre said softly.

"What math? I have twenty-two analysts looking at the balance sheet—"

"The math of the cold," Alexandre interrupted. "When you shut the valve from an office sixty stories in the air, you only see a column of numbers turning red. You don't smell the damp straw. You don't see what happens to a man's fingers when he has to hit a drift pin with a sledgehammer at twenty below zero just to keep his neighbors from freezing in their beds."

"Alexandre—"

"Cancel the helicopter," Alexandre said. 

"If I hit transmit on this warrant, you cannot come back," Luc said, his voice dropping into a flat, terrifying chill. "There will be no family dinners in Annecy. No holidays. You will be a fugitive on a federal ledger until you are fifty."

"Then don't look at the ledger, Luc. Iron your shirt instead."

Alexandre reached down and flicked the battery isolation switch. The satellite terminal went dead with a soft, collapsing pop of static.

For three seconds, the roundhouse was completely silent except for the low, rising drone of the Pelton wheel spooling up beneath the bedrock. 

Then, high in the rafters, an amber warning strobe began to pulse. 

Giulia Bellini came bursting through the heavy blast doors of the lower turbine gallery, her hair plastered to her skull with spray, her wool coat soaked black. Her breathing was ragged, her face pale with an adrenaline that had pushed her past exhaustion.

"Alexandre!" she yelled, her boots slipping on the frozen slag of the rail bed. "The water’s through the bypass, but it’s not clean! AlpSec—they didn't try to stop Matteo on the ridge. They dropped three remote-detonated ballast cars across the lower rail interlock at the switchyard. The line to the Swiss border is physically crushed, and the Carabinieri tactical van just pulled up to the outer gate with the tribunal's seal."
