The magnesium glare high on the crags did not cut the penstock. It severed the upper aeration bypass, and thirty seconds later the mountain punished them for it.

A hollow thud, like an iron anvil dropped into deep peat, shuddered down six hundred meters of buried steel pipe. Inside the powerhouse, the high-tensile vibration hit Matteo through the soles of his boots before the sound arrived. The concrete foundation—poured in the reign of Umberto I, four paces thick—shook with a rhythmic, sickening stammer. 

"Intake surge!" Matteo yelled, lunging through the turbine hall’s iron doorway, the blast of subterranean air stripping the breath from his teeth. 

The dual-runner Pelton unit was not humming its clean, high-frequency tenor. It was groaning, a jagged, tearing rasp that rattled the glass in the high clerestory windows. The sudden vacuum surge from the severed vent line had pulled dead-bottom moraine straight out of the glacial basin. Thousand-pound pulses of pulverized granite, ground fine as flour by millennia of ice, were tearing down the chute at ninety meters per second. 

At the base of the left casing stood Davide Viana. He was nineteen years old, the son of a Bussoleno tunnel excavator, built like an ash stave with oversized wrists and prominent collarbones that poked against the collar of a salt-stained canvas boilermaker’s jacket. His woolen cap was pulled so low it folded his red, frost-bitten ears flat. He held a three-foot bronze spanner in both hands, his knuckles white as tallow, staring at the needle-valve actuator like a man watching a venomous viper coil on his kitchen table.

"It won't close, Matteo!" Davide screamed over the hydro-pneumatic roar. "The hydraulic piston dumped! It's dead at forty percent!"

Matteo hit the floor plates with a sliding skid, his right knee—the one reconstructed with steel pins after the 1998 freight derailment at Modane—barking in jagged, white-hot agony. Silicosis caught him in the throat; a dry, racking cough doubled him over until he spat gray phlegm against the wet tread plate. He didn't have his inhaler; he had left it on the kitchen table in San Didero next to a jar of pickled chicory he hadn't had the stomach to finish that morning.

"Don't force the hydraulic ram!" Matteo gasped, dragging himself up by a support stanchion. "You'll snap the linkage! It’s not the cylinder! It’s the nozzle!"

The Pelton needle—a four-hundred-pound spear of forged chrome-steel that slid forward to choke the jet or back to unleash it—was frozen. Glacial silt, supercooled by the -8°C air tearing through the fractured draft tube, had packed into the annular seat like wet emery stone. If the needle stayed jammed at partial stroke, the deflection flaps couldn't divert the water cleanly. The unbalanced jet would strike the outer lips of the spinning bronze runner buckets, tearing them off their hub one by one until the runner self-destructed through the casing wall.

"Grab the brass drift and the four-pound sledge," Matteo barked. "The lead one, not the steel. You hit chrome with steel and we’ll never hold pressure again."

Davide fumbled at the shadow-board on the brick wall, his hands shaking so violently the heavy drift pin clattered twice against the floor before he pinned it against his hip. "We’re going inside the pit? Under the spray?"

"There’s no spray if the deflector holds forty percent," Matteo shouted, unlatching his heavy sheepskin coat and dropping it onto a grease-drum. Beneath it, his ribbed wool sweater was damp with sweat that had turned freezing cold against his ribs. "If the deflector gives, the jet cuts us both in half before we feel the wet. Don't touch the linkage. Move!"

Matteo kicked the square inspection hatch in the casing base. A blast of supercooled mist erupted into the hall, carrying the bitter, mineral stink of pulverized slate and wet rust. The air down there was six degrees colder than the hall, chilled by the raw glacial torrent ripping through the bypass flume two feet below the iron grating.

He went down backward into the pit, his boots slipping on the slick, black scum that lined the iron rungs. His arthritic knee refused to bend past ninety degrees; he had to swing his right leg out wide, jamming the heel into the corner of the angle iron to brace his weight. His split thumb, bound in sticky electrical tape over greasy linen, burned like an open flame when the icy mist soaked through the weave.

Davide dropped down beside him, his breath coming in short, panicked sobs that fogged the beam of Matteo’s battered headlamp. 

The inspection chamber was barely four feet high. Above them, enclosed in the cast-iron housing, the seven-foot Pelton wheel spun in an invisible blur, a dark, roaring wheel of phosphor-bronze cups drinking two thousand liters of mountain runoff every second. Just ahead of them, jutting into the chamber like the barrel of a siege gun, was the main nozzle. The chrome-plated spear was jammed three inches short of its seat. Packed into the clearance gap was a gray, greasy collar of frozen quartz sand and shale flakes, wedged tight as lead caulking.

"The water bypass is leaking through the gland packing," Davide yelled, shielding his eyes from the needle-sharp needles of high-velocity spray bouncing off the housing. "The cold is freezing the silt inside the sleeve!"

"Give me the drift!" Matteo ordered.

He jammed the flat bronze nose of the bar directly against the rim of the nozzle sleeve, setting his shoulder against the slimy iron bulkhead for leverage. "Take the hammer. Two hands. Hit the bar square. Don't glance it off the chrome!"

Davide lifted the lead-headed sledge. In the cramped, freezing crawlspace, he had no room for a full swing; he had to choke up on the hickory handle, his shoulders hunched against the vibrating ceiling plate. 

"Now!" Matteo bellowed.

The boy struck. The soft lead mallet thudded against the brass drift with a dull, heavy shock that jarred through Matteo’s collarbone. The gray ring of silt didn't move. 

"Again!"

Another strike. A spray of pulverized stone dusted Matteo’s cheeks, scouring the skin like coarse sandpaper. 

"Again! Harder! Put your hip into it, Davide, you aren't tenderizing veal!"

The boy grunted, his face twisted in feral, terrified concentration, and swung with everything he had. The hammer took the drift true, and Matteo drove his weight forward. 

A sharp, metallic *crack* cut through the roar of the water. 

A wedge of packed shale, brittle under the freezing spray, split and tumbled into the lower drain. The chrome needle leaped forward a half-inch under the residual hydraulic pressure, then jammed again with a screech that set Matteo’s fillings on edge. 

"It’s binding on the lower guide rail!" Matteo yelled, feeling the housing with his bare left hand. The vibration was wilder now—an eccentric wobble that was beginning to loosen the five-inch hex nuts securing the nozzle body to the penstock flange. A mist of hydraulic oil was beginning to weep from the secondary seal, smelling of burnt animal fat and warm zinc. "The silt’s down inside the keyway! Reach in with the scraper—the flat scraper in my back pocket!"

Davide dropped the sledge and lunged behind Matteo, his freezing fingers clawing at the stiff denim of Matteo’s work trousers. "I got it! I got it!"

"Scrape the lower flat! Keep your knuckles clear of the bucket tips! If your sleeve catches the air draft off the runner, it’ll pull your arm out of the socket!"

Davide went down on his stomach into the freezing puddle on the pit floor. He slid his arm into the six-inch gap beneath the nozzle body, his jaw clenched so hard his molars ground together audibly over the water noise. Matteo watched the boy’s eyes widen in absolute terror as the air displacement from the spinning bronze cups rippled the fabric of his jacket sleeve like a flag in a gale.

"I feel it," Davide choked out, his voice cracking into an adolescent register. "It’s... it’s not just stone, Matteo."

"Clear it! Don't talk, scrape!"

"It’s not just stone!" the boy shrieked, wrenching the scraper back toward his chest with a violent jerk of his shoulders. 

He rolled onto his side in the freezing slush, coughing and gasping, his sleeve drenched to the shoulder in black, grit-choked water. He held his palm open under the weak yellow puddle of the headlamp.

Lying in the boy's red palm was a crushed cylinder of blue-anodized alloy, no longer than a cigarette butt, sheared diagonally by the needle’s advance. Trapped within its hollow core was a clump of bright, luminescent orange polymer beads, each no larger than a grain of mustard seed, completely impervious to the freezing mountain water.

Matteo picked the crushed alloy tube up with his tape-wrapped thumb. It wasn't river debris. It was a weighted tracer core from a hydraulic inspection bypass—the kind used exclusively by regional pipeline concessionaires to map flow capacity from high-altitude maintenance dams. 

Someone hadn't just taken a torch to the upper flume to frighten Marshal Moretti at the road barrier. They had dumped commercial line-marking obstruction plugs into the high intake pond at Cenisio an hour before the torch ever touched the gantry. They had timed it so the silt-laden sludge and the synthetic plugs would hit the turbine nozzles the exact moment the local gendarmes were distracted at the gate.

Above them, the Pelton runner let out a high, singing scream as the balance shifted again. The needle slipped another inch into the collar, then shuddered to a full stop.

"Matteo," Davide whispered, his eyes locked on the ceiling plate above their heads. A thin, perfectly straight hairline crack was opening across the gray iron casting of the turbine shroud, weeping a steady, pressurized jet of water straight onto the boy’s chest. "The casing is splitting."
