The wind on the northern spur of the Cenisio ridge did not merely blow; it carved. It stripped the heat from Lukas’s cheekbones until his skin felt like dry parchment stretched over wire. 

He hung four hundred meters above the rail line, anchored to the rusted structural ribbing of Ventilation Shaft Three by a single nylon lanyard. Below him, the darkness was absolute except for the furious, bioluminescent froth of the torn penstock. Five miles down-canyon, millions of liters of diverted glacial melt were hammering into the limestone canyon floor, sounding like a freight train derailment that never stopped hitting the earth.

His radio hissed in his ear, choked with static and the howl of the gale.

*“—Lukas! The turbine is cavitating—pressure down to zero-point-four—if the exhaust dampers drop, the gallery suffocates in eight minutes—”*

“I see them,” Lukas whispered into the collar of his parka. His lips were split and stiff; each syllable tasted of salt and frozen iron. 

Above him, three figures in white insulated assault suits were working their way down the guide cables of the vertical exhaust chimney. Their crampons bit into the hoarfrost with rhythmic, metallic crunches. They didn't move like demolition men in a hurry; they moved with the grim, calculated economy of alpine mercenaries whose transport had vanished and whose bank tokens were being flagged one by one across the European clearing system.

Lukas flexed his left hand against the frozen steel of the catwalk. The sensation in his index and middle fingers had never returned after the cable snap in the Oberland three winters ago; in this cold, they were just two wooden pegs taped together inside his leather mitten. Whenever the terror spiked, his chest reverted to the same suffocating rhythm he remembered from childhood—the claustrophobic panic of being trapped inside his uncle’s empty grain silo during an autumn blizzard, listening to the kernels shift against the tin siding like dry bone.

He reached down to the heavy iron manifold at his boots.

Shaft Three was a vertical lung, twenty meters across, plunging straight down through seven hundred meters of solid gneiss into the crown of the Fréjus rail tunnel. If AlpSec blew the trunnion pins on the primary intake flap, forty tons of counterweighted cast iron would shear free and plunge down the bore like a piston, smashing the centrifugal fans below and permanently corking the northern draft. 

Ten thousand people in the dark below would exhaust their oxygen before dawn.

The lead climber dropped onto the maintenance gantry twenty paces from Lukas. The man’s face was hidden behind an amber-tinted storm visor, his chest harness weighed down with rectangular blocks of olive-drab resin wrapped in heavy thermal adhesive. No rifles. In a six-hundred-meter vertical chimney with rising air currents, a ricochet or a muzzle flash in an enclosed gas pocket was suicide. Instead, the point-man carried an industrial pneumatic fastening gun clipped to his shoulder and a five-foot titanium pry-bar.

He spotted Lukas immediately. The amber visor locked onto him.

The contractor didn't shout a warning or offer a demand. He simply brought the pry-bar up into a two-handed defensive guard and gestured with his left thumb toward the mountain stairs.

*Clear out.*

Lukas stood his ground on the perforated steel grate. The wind screamed through the intake slats behind him, producing a low, vibrating drone that rattled in his teeth. 

“You drop that flap,” Lukas yelled into the storm, his voice instantly swallowed by the gale, “and you seal your own descent! The service gondola is anchored to the lower counterweight!”

The mercenary stepped forward, his spiked soles scraping over the rime. Through the comm patch on the man’s tactical collar, a speaker crackled, wet and tinny:

*“Command is offline, Miller. Swiss police seized the Saint-Jean rail switch. The clearinghouse has voided our transit bonds. If this bore isn't plugged, the liability falls on the contractor pool. Drop the pins.”*

The man with the amber visor didn't answer the radio. His breathing came through the collar grille in steady, ragged gasps. He wasn't a fanatic; he was a master sergeant whose company pension was vanishing into a Hague receivership, trying to execute the final clause of an asset-denial contract that might keep his syndicate out of bankruptcy court.

Lukas didn't lunge. He couldn't outfight three trained climbers on a four-foot-wide gantry. 

Instead, he reached his numb left hand into his utility pocket and pulled out the brass pressure-release pin from the secondary de-icing manifold.

The AlpSec sergeant saw the movement and closed the distance in two lunging strides, raising the pry-bar to pin Lukas against the handrail. 

Lukas dropped to his knees. The iron bar shrieked across the galvanized pipe above his helmet, sending a shower of rust and ice into his collar. 

With his right hand, Lukas jammed the release key into the pneumatic purge valve for the high-altitude spray loop—the closed-circuit line meant to blast forty bars of heated glycol and compressed air onto the intake louvers during deep winter freezes.

He didn't turn it toward the man. He threw the bypass lever all the way into the emergency flush position.

A deafening, pneumatic crack echoed down the shaft. 

Two hundred meters of pressurized air and super-cooled slurry roared through the overhead nozzles, not in a directed stream, but in a blinding, hyperbaric whiteout. The velocity of the venting air caught the second climber as he hung suspended from his descending rig forty feet up the chimney wall. The blast slammed the man against the granite casing, shattering his visor against the rock and ripping his gear pack from his shoulders.

The heavy nylon rucksack—packed with thirty kilograms of demolition block—tumbled into the abyss. It spun down the dark throat of the shaft, vanishing toward the distant floor.

The sergeant on the catwalk stumbled, blinded by the fog of frozen glycol. He swung the pry-bar wildly, the titanium tip gouging into the concrete parapet six inches from Lukas’s ear.

Lukas kicked out with his right boot, catching the sergeant’s front knee precisely on the hinge of the crampon strap. The metal buckle gave way with a sharp *snap*. The mercenary lost his footing on the slick, glycol-coated grate, his leg buckling outward as he slammed against the exterior mesh barrier.

Behind him, the third AlpSec climber reached the platform, dropping into a low crouch. He wasn't reaching for a tool; he had a handheld inductive detonation controller strapped to his forearm, its digital display pulsing with a steady amber interrogation sequence. 

The dropped pack.

They hadn't just brought cutting charges for the hinge. They had pre-primed an altimetric timer on the pack that had just fallen into the shaft.

Lukas lunged forward on his belly, crawling across the freezing grate. The glycol burned through his canvas work trousers like acid, the cold biting directly into his marrow. 

The third climber raised his boot to crush Lukas’s fingers against the catwalk rail.

Lukas didn't pull back his hand. He thrust his frost-deadened fingers straight into the serrated teeth of the climber’s trailing ascender clamp, jamming the camming teeth with the thick leather of his mitt and the dead flesh beneath it. 

Pain didn't come—only the dull, sickening crunch of bone against steel. But the cam jammed.

The climber jerked back in shock as his safety harness seized on the deadweight of Lukas’s arm. The sudden torque pulled the man off balance, his forearm controller striking the steel edge of the hatch coaming. The glass cover shattered.

The amber light on the timer didn't extinguish. It locked into a rapid, high-frequency red strobe.

*Arming cycle initiated.*

The fallen rucksack was five hundred meters down, wedged somewhere in the structural baffling directly above the main intake fan. If it detonated against the concrete apron while the penstock water was dead, the overpressure wave would blow the tunnel's inner acoustic seals like paper bags, directing twenty thousand cubic meters of toxic subterranean smoke straight toward the crowd of families huddled on the tracks at the Bussoleno portal.

The sergeant with the broken crampon was dragging himself up by the rail, his breathing a wet rattle behind his mask. He looked down at the strobing red sequence on his teammate's shattered arm unit, then down into the yawning black bore of the shaft.

His visor cleared for a second. His eyes were wide, bloodshot, and hollow with the realization of what had just been triggered.

“The timer,” Lukas gasped, pinned to the grate by the jammed ascender, his left hand crushed inside the clamp. “The override... where is the manual abort?”

The sergeant didn't answer. He looked at the severed line, then across the valley toward the French border, where the blue emergency beacons of the Swiss federal patrol were already cresting the high snowfields of the pass. 

There was no abort. The system was fail-deadly.

Down in the black throat of the chimney, four hundred meters below their boots, a sharp, metallic click echoed up the granite walls, followed by the high, singing whine of a capacitor charging to peak voltage.
