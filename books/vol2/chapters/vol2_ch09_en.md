The windshield wipers of the blue Alfa Romeo had frozen solid against the glass twenty minutes ago, leaving two blinding half-moons of crust through which Marshal Moretti had to squint to see the crossing.

Behind him, idling on the single-lane mountain access road, the diesel engine of an unmarked, tracked armored carrier churned up clouds of sulfurous, black exhaust. Its broad rubber tracks bit into the packed rime with a rhythmic, sickening crunch. Moretti didn’t need to look in his rearview mirror to know who sat in the jump seat of that private rig: three men from AlpSec Tactical, bundled in sterile anthracite windbreakers with non-standard ceramic plates, their fingers tapping on composite stocks. They were private rail contractors legally certified as asset-recovery bailiffs by the Turin Commercial Court, but they smelled of contract work in the Balkans and looked at Moretti’s five-man Carabinieri detachment like an administrative nuisance to be endured until the shooting started.

Moretti rubbed his knuckles against his wool overcoat. His knuckles were swollen, red from forty winters in the valley and an old scaphoid fracture that always ached when the barometer plunged. His radio, clipped to the breast of his reflective vest, spat static that tasted of iron.

*“Station Three, this is Susa Command,”* the dispatcher’s voice drifted through a wash of atmospheric noise. *“Prefecture confirms the writ of repossession. Clear the level crossing. Vanguard’s technical team requires direct access to the powerhouse switchyard before the penstock gates freeze open. Acknowledge.”*

Moretti picked up the hand-mic, the coiled cord stiff as a frozen branch. "Command, the municipal council declared the valley grid under local emergency shelter protocols an hour ago. The clinic in Bussoleno is drawing line voltage through their transformer right now. If we pull the switches, the clinic goes dark."

*“The court order is executive, Marshal,”* the voice crackled back, flatter now, drained of any local accent. *“You are instructed to enforce the clearance. The private recovery team will secure the machinery. Do not debate the warrant on an open band.”*

Moretti let the mic drop. It swung against the heater vent, clicking like an erratic pendulum.

Beside him in the passenger seat, young Carabiniere Castagna sat rigid, his boyish cheeks pale beneath his service cap. The boy’s family ran an apple orchard three kilometers down the riverbed; his grandfather’s dialysis unit was currently humming on the very current coming off the old Pelton wheel.

"Marshal?" Castagna whispered, his voice catching in his dry throat. "What do we do when they tell us to clear the gate?"

"We get out," Moretti said, reaching for his flat-brimmed cap and hauling his heavy wool collar up against his ears. "And we stand between the tracks."

The wind slammed into Moretti the moment his boots hit the slush. The level-crossing arm had been cranked down by hand, its weathered red-and-white stripes buried beneath a thick fringe of icicles. Beyond the striped bar, where the spur line curved toward the black silhouette of the roundhouse, the world was pitch-black except for a single yellow halogen beam mounted on the roof of an old track-inspection cart.

A figure emerged from the darkness of the tracks.

It was Matteo Rossi.

Matteo walked with the lurching, heavy step Moretti had watched for thirty years—the ruined right knee dragging slightly through the drift, a souvenir from an uncoupled hopper car in the winter of ninety-eight. Over his padded rail-worker’s coat, Matteo had tied a bleached flour sack to the end of a long iron shunting pole. The cloth whipped violently in the gale, snapping like a sail in a squall, catching the headlights of the idling Carabinieri cruisers.

A white flag.

Moretti stepped past his car’s front bumper, his boots sinking six inches into the frozen gravel of the road shoulder. He raised an open palm toward the tracked carrier behind him, signaling its driver to kill the high-beams. The carrier ignored him, keeping its halogen array trained directly on Matteo’s chest.

Matteo stopped on the rail side of the wooden barrier. His face was gray with exhaustion, the stubble along his jaw frosted white with his own frozen breath. His right thumb was wrapped in greasy canvas tape, stiff and blood-spotted where an iron lever had bitten him.

"You’re thirty minutes late, Moretti," Matteo said. His voice was raw, frayed at the edges by the silicosis that lived in his lungs like ground glass, but it was steady.

"The grade up from Exilles is iced over," Moretti said, his hands remaining outside his coat, broad and bare, open to the sleet. "You should be inside, Matteo. You look like hell."

"I look like a man trying to keep three thousand people from freezing to death in their beds because a German hedge fund wants to sell the turbine for scrap bronze."

"The writ came from Turin, Matteo. Signed by the magistrate. It’s an executive order."

Matteo let out a dry, rattling cough that shook his shoulders. He didn’t drop the pole. "You know who signed the petition for that writ? A legal clerk who hasn't set foot outside the Corso Vittorio Emanuele in ten years. Look down the valley, Marshal. Look behind you."

Moretti didn’t turn around. He didn't need to. He knew that if he looked east along the gorge, the yellow squares of kitchen windows in Bussoleno and Chiomonte were glowing. For three days, those windows had been dead, frosted over while the regional utility rationed power down to zero to balance the secondary bond defaults. Tonight, the valley was alive with warm yellow light because the old Pelton turbine was spinning under the pressure of the Cenisio flume.

"The law doesn't run on sentiment, Matteo," Moretti said quietly, his voice lost under the roar of the river fifty yards below the trestle. "The order names the plant an unlawful conversion. If you don't raise this gate, the men behind me aren't going to ask for a receipt. They have tracked clearing plows and authorization to remove mechanical obstructions."

"Let them try to plow eighty tons of iron ore," Matteo rasped. He gestured with his chin toward the roundhouse siding. Through the driving snow, Moretti could see the squat, menacing outlines of two gravity-hopper wagons parked nose-to-tail across the switch point, their brake shoes locked against the wheels with welded steel wedges. "They hit those cars, they break their tracks. And then they sit out here until morning, just like the rest of us."

The door of the armored carrier behind Moretti swung open with a heavy pneumatic hiss.

A man stepped down into the rime. He didn't wear a uniform, only a knee-length tactical shell with no insignia, a heavy ballistic headset resting around his bull neck. He carried a clipboard inside a clear waterproof sleeve and walked with the impatient, measured swagger of someone who measured human lives by contractual penalty clauses.

"Marshal," the man called out, his Italian clipped, northern, entirely devoid of mountain inflection. "Why are we talking to the squatter? The order gives us thirty minutes to secure the primary bypass before the turbine housing fractures. Step aside and let the winch team take the gate."

Moretti didn’t step aside. He shifted his weight, his worn service boots planting firmly on the centerline of the asphalt, directly between the private contractor and the red-and-white barrier.

"This is a Carabinieri perimeter, sir," Moretti said, not raising his voice, keeping the flat, bureaucratic weight of thirty years of civil authority in his chest. "You will return to your transport until I conclude the identification protocol."

The contractor stopped, three paces back. A thin, incredulous smile touched his lips. "Identification? You’ve known that mechanic since you were in grade school. The magistrate gave us immediate right of entry. If you obstruct the execution of this warrant, you are personally liable for the liquidated damages on the municipal bond."

"I am the senior public safety officer in this commune," Moretti said, his hand dropping unconsciously to rest atop his leather duty belt, nowhere near his holster, but firm. "And until the district attorney arrives to verify the cross-border jurisdictional filing, this crossing is a restricted safety zone under the alpine road code."

Matteo leaned across the iced barrier. From his pocket, with fingers that trembled with cold, he pulled a damp sheet of thermal paper, folded twice. The ink was slightly smeared, but across the top ran the embossed header of the Canton of Valais registry, stamped with an international Hague trust seal transmitted via the high-altitude optical link not twenty minutes before.

"Give it to him, Moretti," Matteo said, thrusting the paper forward. "Show him the registry. The asset isn't under the Turin bankruptcy anymore. It’s held in trust for the public utility of the watershed under Swiss civil code. He touches that gate, he’s not breaking a local picket. He’s violating a bilateral public trust."

The contractor didn’t even look at the paper. He reached inside his parka, pulled out a small satellite phone, and pressed it to his ear, his eyes locked on Moretti with cold, predatory certainty.

"Operations," the contractor spoke into the mouthpiece, turning his back to the wind. "The local gendarmes are refusing to execute. Authorize the secondary perimeter breach. Bring the winch cat around the northern service spur. Cut the flume cables."

Matteo’s face went dead white. "The flume? You touch the flume at this temperature, the penstock will collapse under vacuum! The whole mountain will tear itself off the rock face!"

Before Moretti could draw breath, a violent mechanical shriek echoed down from the crags above them—a scream of stressed steel vibrating through the rock beneath their boots.

Up on the vertical face of the mountain, a brilliant blossom of white magnesium flame tore through the blizzard, illuminating the great iron ribbing of the high-pressure water pipe. Someone was already on the cliffside gantry with a cutting torch.

The contractor pocketed his phone and smiled at Moretti. "Ten minutes, Marshal. Then the water stops, the lights go out, and we all go home to a warm bed."
