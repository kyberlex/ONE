The draft under the clinic shed’s eaves smelled of tallow, damp cedar wool, and the sharp, medicinal tang of iodine. 

Giulia dropped the canvas flap behind her, shutting out the roar of the Cenisio wind. Her left thumb twitched against her thigh, a rhythmic, maddening tremor born of two nights without sleep. In the corner, huddled beside an iron woodstove that rattled with every mountain gust, three children from the lower hamlet slept under coarse railway blankets. Chiara was across the room, wrapping a strip of clean muslin around an old woman’s frostbitten heel, her breath pluming faintly in the unheated air.

Giulia moved toward the metal locker where the climbing harnesses and headlamps were stowed. She reached behind the stack of clean linen rolls, feeling for the cold zinc frame of the high-pressure flume harnesses. 

Her fingers brushed something that did not belong.

It was not cold zinc. It was warm. Faintly, imperceptibly warm, radiating the faint heat of a potted lithium cell nested inside a molded rubber housing no larger than a tin of pipe tobacco. 

Giulia’s breath hitched. She withdrew her hand slowly, bringing the object into the amber halo of the hurricane lantern. 

A twin-pin magnetic base. A potted antenna stub shaved flat against the polymer casing. A pulse-emitting diode, pulsing in an invisible infrared cadence that only military-grade airborne optronics could harvest from across the valley. It had been wedged behind the crate of sterile saline bags—directly beneath the clinic’s south-facing window, marking the coordinates of the roundhouse’s sole medical station.

Her vision tunneled. A cold, metallic sickness surged into the back of her throat—the phantom reek of disinfectant and damp concrete from the holding cells at Le Vallette. For five seconds, the shed vanished, replaced by the clanging of iron gates and the suffocating realization that the walls had eyes.

The floorboards creaked behind her.

Giulia didn't whirl. She slid the beacon into her mackinaw pocket and turned with the deliberate, heavy slowness of someone whose joints were calcifying in the mountain freeze.

Pietro Vota stood by the pantry curtain. He held a kettle of boiled meltwater in two hands, his knuckles raw and split from hauling deadwood. He was fifty-two, an electrician who had spent thirty years repairing overhead cantenary lines for the regional rail until the private concessionaires liquidated his maintenance depot. A quiet man who chewed dried bay leaves to stave off hunger and spoke only when asked the tension on a copper cable.

Now, his gaze dropped to Giulia’s right hand, still resting inside the pocket of her coat.

The kettle trembled in his grip. A single droplet of water spilled over the lip, hissing as it struck the hot iron of the stove.

"You dropped your gloves near the linen chest, Pietro," Giulia said. Her voice was flat, carrying none of the shaking that rattled her ribcage. "An hour ago. When you brought in the clean blankets."

Pietro swallowed. His throat worked convulsively, the tendons standing out like dry cordage against his weathered neck. "Giulia—"

Chiara froze across the room. The roll of muslin paused mid-turn in her hands. She looked from Pietro to Giulia, her eyes widening as the ambient silence in the room stretched until it groaned like pack ice.

"The children are sleeping," Giulia said quietly. "Outside. In the tool annex."

She stepped past him. Pietro did not run. He could not. His boots felt like they had been cast in slag, his shoulders stooped as if an invisible beam had landed across his spine. He followed her through the low partition into the unheated annex where spare shovels and hydraulic hose coils hung from rusted hooks.

Giulia shut the interior door. The cold in the uninsulated annex bit through her wool trousers immediately.

She reached into her pocket, pulled the black beacon out, and laid it on the scarred surface of an oak workbench between them.

Pietro stared at the device. His face was the color of curdled milk. "They have my sister's house in Bussoleno," he whispered, the words tumbling out in a ragged, breathless rasp. "The municipal marshal... he came with two AlpSec bailiffs on Tuesday. An administrative eviction notice. Because of the unpaid grid connection fees. She’s on oxygen, Giulia. The concentrator takes two hundred watts continuously. If they pull the main breaker at the street, she has forty minutes on the internal battery. AlpSec said... they said if I placed the transponder where the clinic reserves were stored, they’d certify her account as critical infrastructure. They promised the line wouldn't be cut."

The air between them was so cold their respirations mingled in a single grey cloud.

Giulia felt the rage prickling behind her temples—a hot, blinding impulse to strike him, to smash the kettle, to shout for the yard crew to drag him into the snow. The old reflex of survival: isolate the traitor, crush the leak, purge the circle.

Her fingers slid into her vest pocket. They found the cold, knurled brass casing of the pocket stopwatch—the mechanical one with the cracked crystal and the split-second flyback lever. 

She did not draw a bolt. She did not yell.

She pulled the stopwatch out, placed it beside the tracking beacon on the oak plank, and clicked the crown down with a sharp, metallic *snick*.

*Stage 1.*

"Five minutes," Giulia said. Her jaw ached from clenching. "The Covenant gives you five minutes of uninterrupted speech, Pietro. No shouting. No interruptions. You tell me everything: what you told them, what they promised you, and where the dead drops are. When the sweep hand hits the twelve, I speak for five minutes. And you listen."

Pietro blinked, his eyes rimmed in red. He looked at the ticking brass hand sweeping through its sixty-second circuit. He expected a fist, or the cold barrel of a hunting rifle, or a rope. Instead, there was only the indifferent, mechanical metronome of the balance wheel.

He broke.

The words came in a violent, weeping torrent. He hadn't given them the penstock access codes; he didn't have them. He had only told them which shed held the medical morphine and where the children were sleeping so AlpSec's tear gas canisters wouldn't be launched through those specific windows. He gave them the frequency of the tractor radios. He had taken the beacon from a dead-drop beneath the railway bridge at kilometer fourteen, wrapped in a wax-sealed parcel with five hundred euros in cash—cash he had thrown into the river because his hands felt dirty touching it.

The sweep hand completed its fifth circle. The brass gear tripped. *Click.*

Silence fell over the annex, broken only by Pietro’s ragged, snot-choked breathing.

Giulia laid both palms flat on the oak bench. The adrenaline tremor in her left hand had stopped. The cold had numbed it into stillness.

"You risked forty lives to save one," Giulia said. Her voice did not rise. It cut like a chilled file against iron. "If that beacon had guided an alpine tactical squad into the yard while the generators were switching over, the children you tried to protect would have suffocated in their bunks when the flume blew. You know that. You know how high-head hydro works. You spent thirty years on the catenaries."

Pietro pressed his forehead against the rough timber of the wall, his shoulders shaking silently.

"Here is what happens now," Giulia said. "Under the Covenant, there are no cells here. There are no locks on the outside of any door. The path down the mountain toward Bussoleno is open. You can take your coat, your boots, and walk down into the valley right now. No one will touch you. No one will raise a hand against you."

Pietro raised his head slowly, looking at her as if she were speaking an unknown tongue. "You're... you're letting me leave?"

"I am telling you that you are free," Giulia said evenly. "Invariant Six. No carceral detention. But if you choose to stay, you enter Stage Two. A twenty-four-hour restorative cooling break. Communicative silence. You do not touch a radio, you do not enter the clinic, and you do not speak to the assembly. You report to Lukas Keller at the penstock de-icing winch on the upper flume. You will haul steel cable and break ice shoulder-to-shoulder with him until the morning shift. Tomorrow at noon, the brass drum will spin, and a mediation panel of three arbiters chosen by sortition will decide how your debt to this community is settled."

Pietro looked down at his cracked, grease-stained knuckles. The wind outside slammed against the corrugated iron siding of the shed, rattling the hanging shovels until they sang like dull bells.

"Keller needs a man on the brake lever," Pietro whispered. "The ice on the guide pulleys... it shears the cable if you don't feather the tension by hand."

"Then get your sheepskin jacket," Giulia said.

She picked up the AlpSec beacon from the workbench. With a heavy steel ball-peen hammer hanging from the tool rack, she struck the potted casing once, cracking the lithium cell from its housing and severing the antenna trace before the tracking pulse could complete another cycle. She swept the dead fragments into her pocket.

She stepped back into the clinic shed. Pietro followed three paces behind, his eyes fixed on the floorboards.

Chiara stood near the stove, watching them with a taut, pale face. She did not ask questions. The rhythm of the yard had taught her the grammar of restraint.

"He's going up to the flume to assist Lukas," Giulia said to Chiara. "Give him two dry wool biscuits and a thermos of chicory. The wind on the granite face is forty knots."

Chiara hesitated for half a second, then nodded. She reached into the pantry crate, pulled out the wrapped rations, and handed them to Pietro without hostility, but without warmth. Just the cold, necessary sustenance of survivors who shared the same finite air.

Pietro took the bread, dipped his head once toward the sleeping children, and stepped out through the canvas flap into the freezing dark.

Giulia walked to the north window and scraped a circle of frost off the glass with her thumbnail.

High above the roundhouse, clinging to the sheer, black ice of the Rocciamelone wall, the tiny, red sparks of the mercenaries' thermal cutting torches flared against the granite. They were still cutting through the mounting brackets of the high-pressure penstock bypass valve. Down in the valley, the armored trucks had stalled, but up there on the ice cliff, two hundred meters of pressurized water hung suspended above their heads like an unsheathed guillotine.

A sudden, sharp shudder vibrated through the floorboards beneath her boots. 

It wasn't the wind. It was a water hammer—a violent shockwave traveling down six hundred meters of steel conduit, signaling that the valve seal three thousand feet above was beginning to tear.
