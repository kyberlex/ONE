The jagged aura in Alexandre’s left eye was no longer a fringe of silver sparks; it had blossomed into a blind grey circle that ate every third line on the monitor.

His skull felt as though a dry iron wedge had been driven between the parietal plates. Beneath his heavy wool socks, the granite floorboards of the roundhouse mezzanine still hummed with the dying reverberation of the penstock shock, an ominous, deep-bellied groan that spoke of mountain ice and tortured steel three miles up the valley.

"Lukas!" Alexandre slammed the transmit key on the shortwave set, the cold metal casing burning the heel of his palm. "Lukas, do not touch the mount until the drift settles! The steering servos are hunting blind—you’re forty milliradians off the Swiss repeater!"

A blast of static hissed like thrown gravel through the speaker cone, followed by the jagged, tearing intake of breath from two hundred feet above on the hydro-tower platform.

"I can’t... see the horizon, Alex," Lukas’s voice came back, stripped thin by the squall, each syllable clipped by the violent chattering of his jaw. "The squall came over the ridge like a collapsed wall. Everything past the safety rail is white milk."

Alexandre rubbed the serrated edge of an old brass tram token he kept buried in his watch pocket—a nervous tic he had carried since his apprentice days drafting patent filings in Neuchâtel, when the sheer weight of institutional malice had first made his stomach sour. The cold brass bit into the pad of his thumb, anchoring him against the nausea rising in the back of his throat.

"You don't need to see the mountain," Alexandre shouted into the grille, his voice cracking against the dryness of three sleepless nights. "The Mount Rocciamelone relay is locked on thermal coordinates. But the snow scatter is eating the beam alive. We’re sitting at forty-five percent packet loss. The outbound buffer is choking!"

On the portable terminal rested on a pair of upturned grease drums, the transfer bar had frozen at seventeen percent. Inside that compressed cryptographic payload lay the entire architectural foundation of their defense: the certified Hague trust declarations, the signed affidavits of eight hundred valley residents claiming customary water rights under Swiss civil code, and the verified mirror archives destined for the cooperative servers in Zurich and the union halls in Detroit. If the link severed before the final block synced, the regional tribunal in Turin would rubber-stamp the bankruptcy liquidation by breakfast, declaring the valley a designated infrastructure vacuum—abandoned, lawless, and subject to immediate military clearance.

"The dome is blinded," Lukas gasped. His voice cut out for two long seconds as a gust slammed the tower ironwork, a distant metallic shriek penetrating even the double stone walls of the turbine hall below. "Freezing fog. It hit the lens housing. The heating coil isn't keeping up with the rime—there's an inch of hard glaze over the emitter window!"

Alexandre lunged toward the narrow arched window overlooking the penstock yard. He shoved the sash upward, squinting into the teeth of the blizzard.

At two thousand meters, the wind didn't blow; it hammered like an ocean tide laden with powdered glass. High above the slate roofline, jutting into the roaring darkness, the skeletal lattice of the decommissioned hydro-tower stood encased in a sheath of white hoarfrost. Halfway up the external ladder, a solitary yellow dot—the reflective harness on Lukas’s heavy canvas coat—clung to the steel rungs like a spider anchored against an open sluice.

"Come down!" Chiara yelled from the bottom of the mezzanine stairs, her dark hair plastered to her cheeks with melting snow from her rounds down at the emergency clinic. "Alexandre, tell him to step off the ladder! The wind sheer is hitting seventy knots! If he loses his grip with those dead fingers, no harness in Piedmont will catch him before he strikes the penstock flange!"

Alexandre gripped the radio mic until his knuckles turned the color of lard. He knew about the two deadened fingers on Lukas’s left hand—the souvenir of an icy rigging failure in the Valais years ago that Lukas never spoke of, except by the way he tucked that hand into his armpit whenever the mercury dropped below zero.

"Lukas," Alexandre said, dropping his voice into the radio, trying to hold it steady against the throbbing pressure behind his temple. "Chiara’s right. The squall is peaking. Back down to the catwalk. We’ll wait for a thermal window."

"There is no window," came the reply, ragged and sharp with an anger born of raw exhaustion. "The regional magistrate’s courier left Susa twenty minutes ago with the injunction. If that Hague registration isn’t mirrored in Zurich before the dawn shift enters the Swiss court registry, they don’t need tactical police to clear the valley. They just shut the water valves from Milan and let us freeze in the dark."

The radio clicked twice.

Through the salt-crusted glass, Alexandre saw the yellow dot move. It didn't descend. It hauled itself upward, rung by freezing rung, toward the exposed upper crow's nest where the optical transceiver cylinder sat bolted to the high-tensile mooring plate.

"He's out on the service spar," Alexandre whispered, his migraine-blind eye pulsing in rhythm with his chest.

Down in the lower gallery, the hum of the Pelton wheel altered pitch—a subtle, grinding drag that told Alexandre the mountain intake screens were choking with slush. The power was dipping. On his monitor, the terminal backlight flickered, the voltage needle twitching toward the amber band. Finite water. Finite battery. Every second spent idling under forty-five percent packet loss was burning down their stationary cells.

"Alex!" Lukas screamed over the squall, the sound torn by the whistling lattice. "I'm at the gimbal! The ice... it’s clear glass. Solidified spray from the bypass flume. My scraper broke!"

"Use the reserve!" Alexandre barked, leaning out into the freezing draft until his spectacles flecked with needles of ice. "In your breast pocket—the broad brass spatula from the battery kit! Brass won’t spark, and it won't gouge the quartz glass!"

"I can’t... feel the clasp!"

Alexandre closed his eyes. The white wall of his migraine flared violently. In the dark behind his eyelids, he pictured the exact layout of Lukas's canvas rigger coat: the brass zip, the heavy storm flap secured by two horn toggles, the narrow pocket behind the wool fleece lining where the soft-metal tools were stowed to keep them warm against the ribs.

"Left hand under the collar, Lukas," Alexandre murmured into the mic, his cadence slow, rhythmic, almost clinical. "Don't use the fingers. Drive your whole forearm into the fold. Wedge the elbow against the safety lanyard. The brass is there. It doesn't freeze. Feel for the broad blade."

Ten seconds of howling void answered him. Nothing but the shriek of the alpine wind shredding itself against the high-voltage lines running parallel to the flume.

Then, a dull, rhythmic metallic scraping reached the microphone.

*Chhk. Chhk. Chhk.*

It was the sound of soft metal biting into hard rime ice sixty meters above the gorge.

On the mezzanine console, the packet stream suddenly stuttered. The scatter gauge—a jagged line of red spikes—dropped by five percent. Then eight.

"The bottom half of the dome is clearing!" Alexandre shouted, his eyes locked onto the green terminal screen. "Keep going! The beam is hunting the secondary carrier! Give me twelve more inches of clear aperture!"

*Chhk. Chhk. Sclump.*

A slab of ice broke away, tumbling down through the darkness to shatter against the roundhouse slates with the crack of a pistol shot.

"The alignment!" Lukas gasped, his breathing now a terrifying, dry whistle. "The gimbal moved when the ice sheared! Alex, I lost the mechanical index! Where is the Swiss beacon?"

Alexandre threw himself over the terminal, his left hand blindly scrabbling across the manual azimuth keys. The migraine stole his central sight; he had to tilt his head sideways, using the narrow crescent of clear vision in his right eye to read the incoming optical telemetry.

"Azimuth plus two degrees," Alexandre read, his voice dropping into the steady, mechanical cadence of an engineer at a lathe. "Elevate three tenths. Gently, Lukas. The wind is pushing your elbow. Compensate toward the valley wall. Three tenths up... hold. Hold!"

A high-pitched, warbling tone suddenly broke from the audio monitor—a clean, resonant sine wave that cut through the shortwave static like a church bell across water.

The scattered infrared laser had struck the repeater prism on the crest of the Mont-Cenis pass.

On the screen, the transfer progress bar leapt: twenty-two percent... forty... sixty-eight. The data was leaving the local buffer in a massive, high-throughput torrent, slicing through the storm along a beam of invisible light that no terrestrial jammer could touch without physically scaling the ridge.

"Lock confirmed!" Alexandre yelled, the brass token slipping from his sweaty fingers onto the ledger desk. "Seventy-eight percent... eighty-nine... packet verification coming back through the Valais loop! Zurich has the trust document! Detroit has the affidavit chain!"

The bar hit one hundred percent. A green cryptographic seal flashed once, twice, and stabilized into an immutable timestamp.

"Lukas, it’s through! Get down! Clip to the inside ladder and get down!"

The radio crackled. No voice answered.

Alexandre gripped the sill, his breath catching in his throat. Through the whirling snow, the yellow dot on the crow's nest was no longer upright. It had slumped over the cross-arm, pinned flat against the transceiver housing by the screaming wind, one arm dangling limp through the open steel grating above the empty darkness of the gorge.

And from down the mountain road, echoing through the howling sleet from the lower valley gate, came the unmistakable, mechanical crunch of tracked armored vehicles grinding through the snowdrifts toward the substation perimeter.
