The xenon beams crawled like pale needles across the high snowfields of the Cenisio ridge.

From the shuddering iron gangway of the relay tower, Alexandre watched the tracked carriers churn through the drifts three thousand meters above the valley floor. The diesel grind was so distant it felt less like sound and more like a vibration in the bridge of his nose, competing with the thrumming migraine behind his temples. He pressed the frozen cuff of his jacket against his tearing left eye, fighting the optical blur. 

They weren’t dropping toward the Fréjus railhead. They were angling toward the exposed siphon bridge where the high-pressure steel flume leapt the gorge—the artery feeding the valley’s twin hydro runners.

"Intake flumes," Alexandre wheezed into the chest mic, his throat raw from the lye-cold blizzard wind. "Matteo. They brought thermal rigs. They’re going for the headgates."

Static crackled, chewed up by the gale blowing off Mount Rocciamelone, before Matteo’s voice broke through—hollow, punctuated by a rasping wet cough. 

"Understood. Platform is clear. Get down here."

Alexandre swung onto the vertical ladder. The steel rungs bit through his split leather mitts like dry ice, each drop jarring the bruised ribs he’d earned hauling the optical gimbal into alignment. Below him, the Fréjus marshalling yard looked like an overturned hive of amber fire. Ten thousand valley people had packed the ballast and concrete sidings between the decommissioned freight sheds, their collective breath rising into the freezing mountain night in an enormous, undulating cloud of steam. 

Down on the central loading apron, Giulia Bellini stood braced against an iron stanchion, her left hand wedged deep into the pocket of her oilskin coat, her fingers fiercely kneading the smooth, flat river pebble she’d carried sewn into the lining ever since the winter her mother died of pneumonia in an unheated flat in Susa. The small, rounded quartz gave her an anchor while the adrenaline tore through her nerves. The roar of the assembly was deafening—a chaotic mixture of valley dialect, French from the border towns, and the metallic clatter of workboots on frozen ballast.

Matteo shoved his way through the press of bodies, his heavy sheepskin collar turned up against the sleet. He wasn’t looking at the celebrating crowd. His gaze was pinned to the cast-iron pressure dial mounted on the exterior brick of the old transformer station. The needle was fluttering.

"The water head is dropping," Matteo muttered, pulling a small tin from his pocket and biting into a bitter knot of dried spruce resin to quiet the burning rattle in his chest. "Two bars down already. Those bastards have dropped an anchor plate into the upper bypass. If they torch the siphon expansion joints, the water hammer will split the flume from the summit to the turbine floor. We lose the runners, we lose the batteries before dawn."

"They don't want a bloodbath at the tunnel mouth," Chiara Leone said, coming up behind them. She smelled of mutton tallow and strong, bitter chicory; she had spent four unbroken hours packing wool rags and petroleum jelly over the frost-cracked heels of valley children in the baggage depot. Her knuckles were dark with chimney soot from the potbelly stoves. "A legal eviction is messy on international news. A burst flume is an industrial accident. They starve the grid, the cross-border relays drop from cold-kill, and the courts sign the liquidation order while everyone is freezing to death."

"We have thirty minutes before the head drains," Lukas Keller said. The young Swiss rigger was kneeling over an open trunk of salvage tools, his stiffened fingers methodically checking the tension pins on three heavy brass cable-clamps. When the mountain wind whipped down off the rock faces, Lukas had an unconscious domestic habit of humming three notes from an ancient Valais herding song—a rhythmic, low drone that kept his breathing steady. "Less, if they cut the bypass bleeders."

A sudden, sharp mechanical chime rolled from the public-address horns mounted along the shed rafters. 

It was not an alarm. It was the low, resonant chord of the air-gap terminal, echoing down from the mountain optical mast. The Swiss ridge link was holding.

Across the yard, the wall of human voices fell instantly silent. The hush was startling, almost violent in its speed, leaving only the whistling of the wind through the catenary towers and the rhythmic *thump-thump* of the remaining hydro runner spinning deep in the machine hall beneath their boots.

On the roof of the central freight shunter, a single green lantern swung twice.

The convergence signal. 

From the shadows of the baggage bay, four retired switchmen emerged, carrying an octagonal brass drum mounted on an ash-wood sawhorse. The cylinder was two feet across, its curved panels dull from decades of railway service, stamped with the old raised seals of the Alta Valle rail cooperative. It had been used to assign winter shovel gangs during the avalanches of the seventies; now, packed inside its perforated shell, were hundreds of turned beechwood spindles, each inscribed with the name and commune of an enrolled worker.

Class Zero parity: three delegates. An odd number. No tie votes. No proxies. No executive privilege. The alpine bioregion’s voice in the Continental sortition hall in Geneva.

"We don't have time for a three-hundred-man assembly," a young mechanic shouted from the front rank, his hands trembling as he pointed up at the black peaks where the xenon lights were still cutting through the storm. "They’re cutting the water! We should take the service trucks up the access road and push their carriers off the ledge!"

"With what?" Matteo snapped, stepping up onto the timber curb of the platform, his voice cutting through the damp chill with the flat authority of forty years on the rails. "Hunting rifles? Crowbars against armored tracked recovery units? You throw a wrench at a tactical gendarme and you give the prefect the exact riot declaration he needs to roll the military brigades through the valley. You want to save the flume? Then we finish the sovereign covenant right now. The moment the Geneva registry seats those three names, this infrastructure shifts under the Hague trust convention. If they cut the water after that, it’s not an industrial dispute—it’s an international siege of a recognized public sanctuary."

Giulia stepped forward, her boots crunching on the cinder dust. Her face was bloodless, but the tremor in her hands had stopped, replaced by a cold, sharp stillness. 

"Turn the drum," she said.

Lukas reached out, his thick forearm bunching against the cold wood handle, and spun the brass cylinder. 

The sound of tumbling beechwood rolled across the silent crowd—a dry, wooden rattle like hail falling on an orchard shed. The drum spun five times, its brass hinges gleaming under the yellow yard lamps, before Lukas caught the rim with his palm.

He unlatched the top inspection gate.

"Rule of lot," Chiara called out, her voice clear and carrying over the thousand upturned faces. "Three names from the valley roll. To ride the emergency locomotive through the Fréjus bore into the Swiss sector. To deliver the physical seed-keys to the Geneva sortition."

She reached into the drum without looking, her arm buried to the elbow among the cold wooden spindles. Her fingers closed around the first turn of beechwood. She pulled it out and read the stamped lead inset:

"Pietro Vercelli. Oulx. Millwright."

A gray-haired man in an oil-stained canvas cap exhaled a plume of gray steam, looked down at his calloused boots, and took three steady paces toward the platform stairs.

Chiara reached in a second time. The wood rattled.

"Eliane Rodier. Modane. Telecom lineman."

From the western side of the track, a tall woman in an orange safety smock nodded once to her companion, untied her heavy rigging harness, dropped it onto a ballast pile, and climbed the platform timbers.

Two. One more to establish parity.

The mountain above them groaned. 

It was not thunder. It was the terrifying, hollow *boom* of a hydraulic water hammer echoing down through five hundred meters of solid gneiss. The pressure gauge on the brick wall dropped five bars in an instant. High on the mountain, a brilliant violet flash illuminated the underside of the clouds—the tactical engineers had tripped the high-voltage interlocks at the dam substation with a thermite charge, severing the remote telemetry.

"Third lot!" Matteo urged, his hand gripping the cold iron rail. "Draw it, Chiara!"

Chiara’s fingers plunged into the brass cylinder, fishing through the rolling wood. She seized a spindle, withdrew it, and squinted at the worn letters in the dim lamplight.

"Giulia Bellini. Susa."

Giulia’s heart hammered against her ribs like a trapped bird. She looked at the crowd—at the thousands of eyes watching her from beneath wool beanies and frozen scarves. She felt the small river stone through her pocket, cold against her thigh.

"Platform clear!" Lukas yelled from the locomotive cab behind them. 

The twin-cab alpine shunter gave a low, electric hiss as its pneumatic brake shoes released. Inside its steel belly, the lithium traction pack hummed, drawing the last reserves of the dying Pelton turbine.

Before Giulia could take a step, the overhead catenary wire above track three snapped with the sound of a rifle shot. 

A shower of blinding white sparks rained down over the ballast as the severed copper line recoiled, writhing like a dying snake. Up on the Cenisio rim, the high flume was dead. The water pressure dial dropped to absolute zero. 

The lights across the Fréjus marshalling yard flickered once, twice, and then plummeted into pitch-black darkness, leaving ten thousand people standing under the howling mountain blizzard—illuminated only by the frantic, twin red tail-markers of the armored recovery carrier that had just blocked the Swiss tunnel portal three miles ahead.
