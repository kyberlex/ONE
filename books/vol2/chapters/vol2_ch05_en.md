The warble of the dead laser link cut like a rusty serration through the powerhouse. On the iron catwalk above the spinning Pelton runner, Giulia did not look at the ceiling dials; she felt the shudder through the soles of her boots. The granite foundations were vibrating at a pitch that turned her saliva sour. 

Six hundred meters up the Cenisio crags, through three miles of blinding sleet, someone was burning through the penstock’s hydraulic control line with an industrial torch. If that high-head valve slammed shut under the full drive of the reservoir, the resulting shockwave would rip the riveted steel flume out of the mountain like a split artery.

"Lukas!" she screamed into the stairwell, her voice shredded by the turbine’s roar. "Is the bypass open?"

"Signal’s dead!" his reply came back down the iron shaft, muffled by the blizzard whipping through the upper vents. "Packet loss forty-five percent—the snow’s scattering the infrared beam! I’ve got no telemetry on the upper reservoir! Nothing!"

Down on the generator floor, Matteo wasn't looking at screens. He was kneeling in a puddle of grease and black sleet, hauling fifty meters of braided copper grounding cable off a rusted wooden drum. His canvas work jacket was stiff with frost at the cuffs, his jaw set so hard the tendons corded against his stubble.

"Help me lift the carriage," he barked, not looking up. His split thumb, bound in blackened electrician’s tape, left dark smears on the copper braids. 

"Matteo, stop." Giulia caught the collar of his coat, her left hand trembling with a vicious, cold-induced tremor she couldn't suppress. "Listen to the revs. The load is drifting. If you cross-connect the switchyard down here while the line is unbalanced, you blow the substation transformers from here to Susa."

"I’m not balancing the line," Matteo grunted. He shoved a three-foot steel pry-bar through the reel’s axle and heaved it toward the blast doors. His bad knee cracked—a dull, wet pop beneath his trousers. "I’m shorting the rail points at the turnout. I’m dropping the ballast hoppers across the spur."

"That’s an act of rail piracy!" She threw her whole weight against the heavy wooden drum, arresting its roll. The rough timber bit into her hip through her woolen coat. "The moment you weld those frogs, the Turin prosecutor doesn't file an injunction for civil trespass. They invoke the cross-border critical infrastructure protocol. It’s military jurisdiction. Le Vallette. Special regime. No bail, Matteo. No phone calls."

"They have a cutter on the mountain, Giulia!" he roared into her face, flecks of spit freezing on his chin. "Look at the penstock manifold! Look at the glass!"

She glanced back. The thick sight-glass on the governor manifold was shuddering. Inside, tiny bubbles of cavitating water danced like boiling grease. The men on the ridge didn't want to steal the water; they were cutting the hydraulic pilot line to drop the emergency shut-off gate by gravity. If that gate slammed down with the Pelton drawing four megawatts, the vacuum would collapse the upper pipe, and the rebound pressure would blow the turbine casing through the river wall.

"Five minutes," Matteo rasped, hauling the cable again, dragging her two paces across the slick concrete. "The hydraulic oil is thick as lard up there in this cold. Takes five minutes for the pressure cylinder to bleed dry once the copper line is severed. When that ram bottoms out, the master sluice drops. We have five minutes before the water hammer hits us."

"Then we trip the needle valves!" Giulia lunged forward, grabbing the bronze wheel of the manual deflector lever. "We drop the deflector plates! We dump the jet into the tailrace!"

"You dump the jet, Bussoleno goes black!" Matteo lunged, his grease-blackened palm slamming down over hers on the bronze wheel, pinning her fingers against the cold metal. "Two thousand homes just caught pilot. The clinics in the lower village are pulling current for the blood warmers. You trip that wheel, the whole valley drops into hypothermia, and the utility company re-locks the grid keys from Milan before sunrise! We don't drop the jet!"

"If the pipe splits, we drown them anyway!" Her voice cracked, shrill and ragged against the metallic whine of the generator. Her fingernails tore into the stiff canvas of his sleeve. "You want to fight a war with an arc welder, and you’re going to bury fifty families under six thousand tons of mud! Let go of the wheel!"

"Get the battery pack!" he shouted, his face an inch from hers. His breath smelled of bitter chicory coffee and stale tobacco. "Giulia! I can’t haul the ground clamps and the portable striker alone! The switch is fifty yards out in the drifts! Either you carry the return lead, or the ballast cars stay parked, and their armored trucks drive straight down the haul road into this turbine pit!"

"It’s twenty years in a concrete cell!" she screamed, but the sight-glass beside them gave a sharp, metallic *ping*. A hairline fracture branched across the quartz pane. 

A high-pressure jet of cold reservoir water—needle-fine and sharp as a scalpel—shot through the crack, slicing through the hem of her coat and striking the concrete floor with the sound of a pistol shot.

Matteo didn't flinch. He just held her gaze, his gray eyes sunken, the deep lines around his mouth frozen into trenches of soot and sweat. He wasn't an ideologue. He was a retired line mechanic who had spent thirty years clearing frozen switches with a kerosene torch while managers in Rome checked ledgers. He knew the precise shear strength of every bolt in the valley, and he knew that law was merely what people wrote down after the cold had already killed the weak.

"Lead or deflector, Giulia," he said, his voice suddenly dropping beneath the scream of the water needle. "Decide. Four minutes."

Her stomach heaved. The phantom smell of the Turin courthouse holding cells—floor bleach, wet wool, and human fear—surged into her throat. She had spent ten years believing that paper was a shield, that a stamped petition with the Hague seal could hold back a bulldozer or an extraction crew. But the water jet hissed against the stone, carving a white groove into the wet moss.

She let go of the bronze wheel.

"The ground clamp," she spat, her boots skidding on the slime as she lunged for the secondary cable drum. "Move, you stubborn old bastard. Move!"

Matteo shoved the heavy steel blast door open. 

The alpine night hit them like a wall of wet cement. The gale swept through the gap between Mount Rocciamelone and the valley floor, screaming through the lattice of the high-voltage transmission towers. Snow swirled in dizzying, vertical sheets, illuminated only by the faint amber glow spilling from the powerhouse doorway and the rhythmic, blinding pulses of the warning strobe atop the penstock gantry.

Giulia stepped out into the drifts, her boots sinking past the knee into powdered scree. The forty-pound copper grounding clamp dragged behind her, a dead iron serpent tearing through the crust. The wind punched the air straight out of her lungs; her wet coat immediately stiffened into a shell of brittle ice.

Ten yards ahead, Matteo was stumbling toward the rail siding where two rusted hopper cars, laden with gray basalt ballast, sat hulking in the dark. In his right hand, he dragged the twin welding leads; in his left, he clutched the heavy carbon-arc torch, its cables trailing back into the hum of the stationary battery bank housed in the lower turbine vaults.

"The switch point!" Matteo shouted back, his words ripped away by the gale. He pointed his pry-bar toward the frozen junction where the private spur met the state railway branch. "The manual lever is iced solid! If I can’t throw the points, the hoppers won't roll!"

Giulia fell into the snow, the sharp basalt underbed cutting through her trousers at the knee. Adrenaline washed through her limbs, hot and metallic, steadying her shaking fingers. She scrambled up, dragging the clamp through the drift, her hands freezing inside her soaked gloves. 

Above them, on the sheer face of the cliff, a brilliant blue spark flared against the low belly of the storm clouds. 

The cutting crew at the intake had punched through the hydraulic sleeve. 

A muffled, hydraulic groaning—felt rather than heard—rippled through the bedrock beneath their boots. The Cenisio penstock, six hundred meters of thick-walled steel pipe anchored to the mountain with concrete piers, began to shudder. The flow was choking. The water hammer was building its first backward pulse.

"Matteo!" she screamed, throwing the massive brass jaws of the ground clamp onto the rail head. The teeth bit into the rusted iron with a dull scrape. "The line is surging! You have thirty seconds!"

Matteo dropped to his bad knee beside the frozen switch mechanism. He jammed the carbon rod into the torch holder, his teeth bared against the wind. "Current!" he bellowed. "Close the breaker on the sled!"

Giulia lunged toward the portable contactor box resting on the timber ties. Snow had drifted over the switches; she wiped the frozen crust away with her bare palm, skin tearing slightly against the cold metal casing. She seized the insulated handle and slammed it down.

A blinding, violet sun erupted in the snow.

The arc hissed with the sound of tearing canvas. Smoke—thick, acrid, smelling of sulfur and vaporized rust—billowed into Giulia’s face, instantly whipped away by the gale. Matteo held the rod with both hands, his body violently shaking as four hundred amperes from the locomotive batteries surged through the steel tie-rod, melting thirty years of frozen grease and alpine ice in a fury of incandescent slag.

"Throw it!" he coughed, blind from the flare. "Giulia! Throw the lever!"

She grabbed the four-foot iron switch throw. It was slick with scalding water and freezing slush. She threw her entire weight backward, her boots slipping on the frozen sleepers. 

Nothing moved. The locking pin was still welded by ice inside the housing.

The penstock above them gave a tremendous, hollow *BOOM*—the first hydraulic shockwave hitting the intake manifold. The ground jumped beneath her feet.

"Pull!" Matteo screamed, dropping the dead torch and throwing his shoulder against hers on the iron bar. "Pull, damn you!"

Together, their boots clawing for purchase against the ballast, they leaned into the dark. The iron groaned. A sharp, vitreous *crack* echoed through the gorge as the last anchor of ice gave way. 

The lever slammed down into the gravel.

Fifty yards up the grade, the rusted handbrake of the lead hopper car snapped under the sudden shift in tension. 

With a deafening shriek of unlubricated iron journals, eighty tons of crushed basalt began to roll down the spur, accelerating directly toward the main haul gate—just as the twin beams of three armored tactical transports rounded the lower gorge road, their high-intensity searchlights cutting through the blizzard.
