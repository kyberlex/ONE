The concussive shudder from the south traveled through the soles of Matteo’s work boots before the sound ever reached his ears. 

When the roar finally arrived, it came as a dull, subterranean slap of displaced air that ruffled the wool fringes of thousands of coats and made the hanging catenary wires sing like bowstrings against damp rock. Behind them, the southern portal was sealed. Hundreds of tons of glacial runoff and ballast had slammed into the cut, hydro-locking the approach. There was no retreating down the Italian valley. The only sky left in the world lay six kilometers ahead, through the granite belly of Mount Cenis.

Matteo stopped on the ballast shoulder, his right hand clamped against his hip to take the weight off his grinding knee. Around him, the column did not panic; it compressed. 

Eight thousand people did not move like water. They moved like wet scree, slumping forward in heavy, hitching shuffles. Every pace sent a dry, limestone crunch through the darkness—ten thousand boots, shoes, and rubber-treaded galoshes grinding into the crushed granite bed. The air in the bore was four degrees above freezing, thick with the oily exhalations of century-old diesel runs and the iron-tasting mist that wept perpetually from the unlined ceiling vaults.

"Keep the carts centered!" Matteo rasped, his voice tearing against the dry tickle in his lungs. He cleared his throat, tasting the familiar metallic rust of rock dust. "Straddle the ties! If an axle drops in the drainage trough, leave the timber, take the batteries!"

Beside him, two shop apprentices from Susa pushed a flatbed maintenance bogie loaded with milk crates full of insulated lithium cells and sealed emergency rations. Their knuckles were raw, purpled by the mountain draft that sucked through the tunnel like breath through an iron pipe. 

Matteo wiped condensation from his brow with the greasy cuff of his chore coat. Whenever the dark closed in too tight, an old apprentice habit from forty years past rose in his throat: tapping his molars together to the four-four rhythm of an idling rail switcher. *One-two-three-four, one-two-three-four.* He had done it as a terrified boy of twelve, delivering lard-smeared gaskets to his uncle through the pitch-black classification yards of Bussoleno, terrified that a runaway hopper car would slice him in half. His uncle had told him that iron had a pulse, and if you timed your teeth to it, the yard couldn't surprise you.

Now, the mountain had a pulse, but it wasn't iron. It was the low, collective rasp of human lungs.

Ahead, where the tunnel bore curved slightly toward the international boundary, a constellation of amber and daylight-white work lanterns bobbed in the gloom. They weren't high-intensity halogen floodlights; they were low-draw LED strips lashed to mop handles with zip-ties, miner's headlamps dimming on their third hour of drain, and hand-cranked dynamos held by schoolteachers who turned the small plastic handles until their wrists seized.

"Matteo." 

Giulia was moving up along the outer clearance ditch, her canvas parka dusted with dry cement powder. She held a hand-drawn ledger tucked under her arm, wrapped in a plastic garbage sack to fend off the dripping seepage. Her skin looked gray under the battery glare, her lips cracked at the corners. She walked with her left hand wedged deep into her pocket, but Matteo could see the fabric twitching with the violent, rhythmic spasms that had plagued her since the rail yards in Turin.

"The French scouts crossed the midpoint three minutes ago," she said, leaning close so her words wouldn't carry into the ranks. Her breath smelled of stale coffee grounds and chicory. "The border marker is clear. But the draft has stopped, Matteo."

Matteo looked up. The mist, which had been shearing steadily northward toward Modane under the natural stack effect of the mountain, was beginning to pool around the vaulted light bays. The dangling safety ropes hung dead vertical. 

"The north portal dampers," Matteo said, the rhythm in his jaw stuttering.

"AlpSec hasn't breached the yard yet," Giulia said. "Favre jammed the blast gate with an iron head before they could drop it. But the French regional dispatch just killed the main intake blowers from the Chambéry substation. They’re starving the bore. In forty minutes, with eight thousand bodies burning oxygen in this tube, the carbon dioxide will settle into the track bed like standing water."

A child cried three rows back—a sharp, reedy wail that cut through the shuffle of boots. An old woman’s voice answered it, murmuring in the tight, clipped dialect of the high valleys, offering a piece of hard sugar. 

"We don't stop," Matteo said. He gripped Giulia’s forearm, feeling the rigid tension vibrating through her sleeve. "Pass it back through the marshals. Tell them to turn off every second lamp. Conserve the dry cells. Keep the pace at two kilometers an hour. If anyone sits down on the ties, two people lift them by the elbows. Nobody rests until we smell French snow."

She nodded once, a sharp jerk of her chin, and vanished back into the mist.

Matteo limped forward, forcing his stiffening joint through its tight arc of pain. The ballast was treacherous here; seventy years of freight traffic had ground the stones into round, rolling marbles slick with lubricating grease. Ahead, the gloom seemed to thicken, then fracture.

A point of yellow lantern light emerged from the fog sixty yards in front of him. Then another. Then forty.

They were not moving away; they were coming toward him.

A cold prickle ran across the nape of Matteo’s neck. He reached for the heavy iron shunting bar hanging from his shoulder loop, his fingers tightening on the cold, knurled metal.

"Who’s on the switch?" he called out, his Piedmontese accent harsh and thick in the confined vault.

From the fog came an answer, not in Italian, but in the flat, nasal cadence of the Savoyard rail lines:

"*Cheminots de Maurienne! Avancez!*"

A figure stepped into the glow of Matteo's headlamp. It was a man in an orange high-visibility vest stripped of its corporate patches, his face cross-hatched with grease and deep mountain crow’s-feet. He carried no club, no tear-gas canister. Slung over his shoulder was a wooden crate of crusty rye loaves, their floury crusts pale in the LED beam. 

Behind him came more. Hundreds of them. Men and women from Modane, from Saint-Michel, from the high timber communes of the Arc Valley. They were pushing their own maintenance trolleys, laden with five-gallon plastic thermoses of hot soup, wool blankets smelling of woodsmoke, and car batteries rigged to charging strips.

The two columns came to a halt three paces apart, separated only by a white painted band across the tunnel walls—the faded, soot-blackened border stripe where the sovereignty of the Italian Republic met the sovereignty of France, seven thousand feet beneath the ice fields.

For five heartbeats, there was only the sound of water dripping into the ballast trench and eight thousand people drawing cold air into their chests.

Then, from somewhere deep in the French ranks—carried by an old woman whose voice possessed the cracked, piercing clarity of church bells heard across a frozen gorge—a song began.

It wasn't an anthem of states or parliaments. It was the old Occitan air that shepherds had whistled across the passes before there were customhouses or railway concessions, a tune that belonged neither to Paris nor Rome:

"*Se chanto que m'en chanto...*"

A railway fitter three yards from Matteo, a man from Condove whose coat was held together with zip-ties, caught the melody in his throat. His baritone joined hers, thick and tremulous:

"*Chanto pas per me...*"

The sound expanded against the granite. It did not echo; the sheer mass of eight thousand bodies absorbed the reverberation, turning the singing into a dense, physical pressure that resonated in the hollow cavities of Matteo’s ribs. Voices rose from the darkness behind him, valley voices, Piedmontese voices, high-country French voices, intertwining under the low arch of stone. Lanterns were lifted high. The yellow beams crossed, knitting together in the mist until the damp rock ceiling glittered like an underground cathedral.

Hands reached across the white line. Matteo felt thick, work-roughened fingers close around his own. The French rail worker took Matteo's wrist, pulled him forward across the border stripe, and thrust a chunk of broken bread into his palm. The crust was still warm from a valley oven.

"Rossi?" the Frenchman asked, shouting over the swelling chorus.

"Rossi," Matteo choked out, his throat burning.

"Favre is at the northern mouth," the man said into his ear, his breath hot against the chill. "He says the gendarmes have stepped back from the switches, but the prefect called the garrison at Lyon. Rail transports are moving down the mainline. We have maybe thirty minutes before they cut the signal interlocks and drop the deadweight cars into the cut."

As if in answer to his words, the overhead catenary wire let out a sharp, metallic ping. 

The low, three-tone hum of the high-voltage carrier line suddenly dropped three octaves, falling into absolute, dead silence. 

Then, two miles ahead toward the French exit, came a hollow, pneumatic thud that traveled through the air column like a cannon shot. The draft reversed instantly, rushing back toward the south with the force of an oncoming gale, extinguishing a hundred flickering lantern wicks in a single breath.

The singing broke apart, jagged and ragged. 

Down the tunnel, an iron door slammed within the service galleries, followed by the terrifying, high-pressure screech of emergency nitrogen fire-suppression vents opening into the bore.
