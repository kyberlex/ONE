The iron floor of the switch-tower vibrated under Matteo’s boots, carrying the low, dull moan of the twin diesel pushers crawling out of the Susa yards four thousand feet below. It was not a sound heard through the ears so much as an ache registered in the teeth and the marrow of his damaged knee.

He clamped the manual dog on the arrestor drum, feeling the cable bite deep into the grooved iron. Outside the blast glass, twenty feet down in the drift of the siding, the captured inspection buggy hung motionless in the steel netting. The four contractors inside were no longer thrashing against their nylon harnesses; they were conserving heat, their breath puffing white against the plexiglass cab, pinned beneath forty tons of wet mountain ballast.

Matteo descended the spiral iron stairs into the penstock gallery, his boots clanging rhythmically against the open grating.

At the foot of the conduit, where the four-foot riveted trunk entered from the north face, Giulia stood beside the bypass wheel. She had taken off her down gloves to adjust the mechanical pressure needle. Her left hand was shaking—a rapid, stuttering tremor that caused the brass collar of the gauge to rattle against the mounting studs.

"The air valves held," Matteo said, his voice grating through the damp chill. "The siding took the slap."

She did not look up immediately. She had wedged an iron pry-bar between the spokes of the bypass wheel to keep the counterweight from creeping back against the ice. Her long wool coat, bought for climate-controlled concourses in Milan, was black with hydraulic mist across the shoulders, the fine hem frayed where it had dragged across crushed ballast.

"The beacon Lukas spoke of," she said. Her voice was too quiet, stretched thin over an edge of panic that seemed ready to snap her collarbones. "It’s transmitting on the emergency band. The court bailiff in Turin will have the military warrant stamped before those engines reach the lower viaduct."

"Let them stamp it," Matteo said. He set his oiler on a flat timber balk and knelt stiffly by the drain flange, inspecting the seal for frost heave. His breath came out in short, wheezing plumes, smelling of bitter chicory and the menthol tallow he rubbed on his chest. "Paper doesn't climb seven percent grades on wet rail. They’ve got to shovel sand under their drivers every yard from Salbertrand up."

"You don't understand what they brought with them," she whispered.

Matteo paused, a hand resting on the wet iron of the valve housing. "I know an EMD six-axle locomotive when its exhaust hits the gorge, Giulia. I’ve laid track in front of them and I’ve pulled spikes behind them."

"Not the locomotives. The covenants."

She turned to face him fully, and for the first time since the blast doors had slammed shut, the harsh sodium glare of the service bulbs caught her eyes. They were sunken, ringed with dark plum hollows. In the tight hollow beneath her jaw, a muscle jumped frantically.

"The secondary debt lien on the Cenisio headworks," she said, her words tumbling out with a desperate, clinical precision that sounded like a confession whispered through a mesh screen. "The legal vehicle that AlpSec is using to declare this gallery an ungoverned hazard. I wrote it."

The gallery fell silent, save for the steady, hollow *thrum-thrum* of sixty atmospheres of glacier water rushing through the steel plates beneath their feet.

Matteo slowly rose, his bad knee clicking audibly. "What did you say?"

"Six years ago," Giulia said, pressing her trembling knuckles against the cold stone rib of the cavern wall. "Before the restructuring fund bought out the northern grid. I was a junior partner at Vane & Holtz. We spent three weeks in an unventilated conference room on the Via Montenapoleone, living on espresso and saltines, running stress tests on municipal defaults. The valley councils were eighty million euros short on their hospital bonds. We showed the provincial administrator how to sever the water rights from the municipal charters and fold them into a Luxembourg special-purpose entity."

She swallowed, the motion dry and painful. Her chin trembled, but she forced herself to keep her gaze locked on his scarred face.

"My grandfather used to keep three milk cows in the meadows above Exilles," she murmured, the technical veneer finally cracking, letting through a raw, ragged grief that sounded decades old. "Every October, when the snow came down past the tree line, he would whitewash the stone boundary wall behind the stable to keep the salt from eating the mortar. When I was twelve, he let me mix the lime in a wooden trough with an ash paddle. And six years ago, sitting under fluorescent tubes that made my temples pulse, I put a market valuation of zero on that wall. I classified the entire irrigation runoff of the north slope as unmonetized corporate salvage. I gave them the knife they are using to carve out your lungs, Matteo."

She closed her eyes, waiting for the fury. She waited for him to point his calloused finger toward the blast door, to cast her out into the wet drift where the mercenaries were cooling in the steel net.

Matteo looked down at his own hands. The canvas tape across his split thumb was gray with graphite grease. He remembered the strike of 2003, when the state rail company had canceled the local freight shuttles, leaving three hundred families to burn fence posts in their kitchen stoves because coal wagons were stranded thirty miles away in the flatlands. He had thrown cobblestones at the riot shields outside the prefect's gate; he had spent four nights on the concrete floor of a holding cell in Susa, sharing a single woolen blanket with a retired signalman who wept for his pension.

He walked over to the bypass manifold, took a clean square of cotton rag from his pocket, and stepped into Giulia's space.

She flinched slightly, her shoulders curling inward.

Matteo did not touch her. Instead, he laid the rag over the greasy brass rim of the valve wheel where she had been resting her bare, trembling palm.

"My father," Matteo said slowly, his voice dropping into the deep, level cadence of the high passes, "sold twenty meters of our lower garden to the highway consortium in 1989. He took the money and bought a motorized tiller with an aluminum gearbox made in Japan. Two years later, the gearbox cracked when it hit a schist boulder, and the dealer had already gone bankrupt. The tiller sat behind our woodpile for twenty years, rusting into the nettles, while the trucks threw diesel soot all over my mother’s bean trellises."

Giulia opened her eyes, staring at the gray cloth on the valve wheel.

"The people down in the plains build systems that eat everything," Matteo continued, his eyes fixed on the condensation tracing lines down the penstock. "They make you believe that if you don't hold the pen, someone else will hold it and write your name into the debit column. You were twenty-five years old, girl. You thought the world was made of glass and paper because that was all they ever let you see."

"That doesn't make it clean," she choked out, a single, hot tear cutting a pale track through the soot on her cheek. "The ink doesn't wash off, Matteo. Those engines are burning diesel right now because my signature is on the collateral schedule filed in Frankfurt."

"The rock doesn't know Frankfurt," Matteo said, and for the first time, a faint, iron-hard ghost of a smile touched the corners of his mouth. "The mountain doesn't give a damn about a collateral schedule. When the Cenisio freezes, the ice expands at twenty thousand pounds per square inch, and it cracks their steel the same as it cracks ours. You aren't in Milan now, Giulia. You're five thousand feet up, standing on the granite that broke Hannibal's elephants. You're a child of this valley, whether you spent ten years forgetting it or not."

He reached out and closed his taped thumb over the iron spanner wedged in the wheel, gesturing for her to take the opposite spoke.

"TheRestorative Ladder," he muttered. "Article Eight. Cooling break is over. We have work to do before those diesels hit the snowshed."

Giulia stared at his weathered hand, then at the iron spoke. She drew a long, shuddering breath of the cold mountain air—tasting the copper, the wet slate, and the faint, bitter ghost of woodsmoke drifting through the upper intake. The wild fluttering in her chest did not vanish, but it shifted, solidifying into something cold, heavy, and purposeful.

She reached out, gripped the iron through the cloth, and leaned her weight beside his.

Together, their shoulders touching against the draft, they threw their strength into the bypass wheel. The frozen threads groaned, popped with the sound of a pistol shot, and then began to turn, venting four hundred gallons of pressurized glacial melt into the diversion flume to scour the lower culverts.

Then, from the acoustic pipe linked to the upper gantry, a sharp, rhythmic tapping broke through the hiss of water.

*Three shorts. Three longs. Three shorts.*

Matteo froze, his hand still on the wheel. "That’s not Lukas."

Giulia looked up, her pulse spiking. "The beacon frequency. They aren't tracking us by rail."

Through the floor plates, a sudden, sharp vibration traveled—not the slow rumble of the heavy locomotives down in the valley, but the vicious, high-frequency chatter of an electric pneumatic core drill biting directly into the granite roof above their heads.
