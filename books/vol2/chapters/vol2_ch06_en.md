The mountain did not echo; it absorbed. Two thousand meters of granite overhead squeezed every vibration into a dry, metallic rattle that Lukas felt through the soles of his boots before he heard it. 

He crouched on the rusted steel grate of the transverse catwalk, seventy feet above the floor of the Fréjus emergency ventilation gallery. The air tasted of ancient lime dust, damp slate, and the sour chemical trace of burning hydraulic oil carried inward from the haul-gate spur. Far down the concrete bore, behind three successive airlock bulkheads designed during the Cold War to survive a subterranean firestorm, something struck steel with the rhythmic, heavy concussions of a pneumatic ram.

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

Lukas pulled his wool watch cap lower over his ears. His left hand—the middle and index fingers deadened to pale, rubbery stumps from an old crevasse slip on the Jungfrau—clutched the knurled bronze spindle of a manual bypass valve. The chill of the iron bled through his goatskin work gloves, biting straight into the bone. He had spent fifteen years rigging high-tension aerial tram cables across open Swiss ridgelines, suspended between sky and cloud. Being sealed inside this blind stone throat made his throat tighten with a quiet, clawing claustrophobia he would never admit to Matteo or Giulia. Under the open sun, if you fell, you flew for three seconds; here, the rock simply waited to flatten you into a stain on the ballast.

A sharp hiss cut through the gloom four hundred yards south. 

Lukas leaned over the guardrail. Below him, the gallery stretched like an unlit artillery barrel toward the French border. Down at the auxiliary intake seal, a seam of brilliant orange incandescence flared against the dark. 

Thermite paste. 

They weren't using explosive breaching charges. Explosives in an unvented ventilation shaft would blow out eardrums, shatter the concrete baffles, and bring down loose shale from the unlined ceiling pockets. These men were professionals. AlpSec didn't hire sledgehammer thugs; they hired former mountain infantry engineers who knew the tensile tolerance of reinforced bulkheads and the exact legal boundary where property retrieval crossed into corporate manslaughter.

The orange seam crept downward, spitting white sparks into the damp air. 

Lukas reached into his canvas rig pouch, his numb fingertips fumbling past cold steel carabiners until they found the radio handset. He clicked the side switch twice. No voice. Voice carried down ventilation galleries like bugle horns through an empty church.

Two clicks returned through the receiver. Alexandre, two miles back at the turbine house, holding the line.

The airlock door buckled with a screech of shearing bolts. The heavy slab of two-inch rolled steel tipped inward, slamming into the gravel drainage ditch with a concussion that shook the catwalk beneath Lukas’s knees. 

Through the sulfurous haze, four silhouettes advanced into the gallery. 

They moved with the low, rolling gait of men accustomed to carrying sixty pounds of load-bearing kit over broken grade. Their helmets were matte grey, non-reflective, fitted with dual-tube night vision arrays that glowed with faint emerald halos. No shouts. No tactical posturing. Their carbines were slung across their chests on tight retention swivels, hands kept free for tools: a battery-powered hydraulic spreader, a thermal imaging scanner, and two orange composite cases containing fiber-splicing kits.

They hadn't come to gun anyone down. They had come to find the penstock telemetry trunk, sever the valley’s autonomous control loop, and re-anchor the high-head bypass to the regional rail grid dispatch center before morning court opened in Turin.

Lukas wiped sweat and slate dust from his forehead with the back of his forearm. His breath came fast, plume-white and thin in the draft. He knew the lead operator's posture. That wide, measured stride belonged to men who spent their winters clearing rail avalanches in the Valais—men who had cousins working the dams in Sion, men who paid into the same regional pension clearinghouses that the Frankfurt funds were currently liquidating. They weren't monsters. They were simply doing what men did when their contracts were backed by sovereign recovery warrants and their mortgages depended on the next wire transfer.

*Which makes them twice as dangerous,* Lukas thought. *They won’t run from a scare.*

The lead scout halted thirty paces below the catwalk, raising a gloved hand. The thermal scanner in his left fist swept the dripping ceiling, catching the steel web of the overhead utility cradle.

Lukas held his breath. He pressed his ribs flat against the cold planking of the gantry, drawing his legs in until his knees touched his chin.

The point man spoke—his voice low, flat, transmitted through a throat microphone, but the acoustic focus of the concrete arch lifted the syllables straight to Lukas’s ear.

"Line junction forty meters ahead," the man said in clean, unaccented Swiss-German. "Check the hydrostatic tap. The pressure line runs parallel to the service conduit. If they’ve locked the bypass mechanically, we drop the shear pins."

The second man, carrying the hydraulic spreader, stepped over the drainage trough. "Draft is picking up from the north adit. The gate above might still be open."

"Keep moving. Ten minutes to physical lock. We don't wait for the local police."

Lukas closed his eyes for two seconds. His father, who had spent forty years greasing the sluice gates at Mauvoisin, had taught him to gauge hydrostatic pressure not by the sweep of brass needle gauges, but by pressing the side of his skull directly against the steel penstock wall. If the steel sang a high, glassy C-sharp, the pressure was holding six hundred meters of head. If it dropped to a dull, grinding baritone, the intake was starving.

Right now, through the iron support stanchion bolted to the catwalk, the steel was screaming.

Matteo had cleared the frozen intake above. The water was down. Six hundred meters of vertical alpine head, four million gallons of meltwater backed up behind the upper mountain choke, waiting against the emergency divergence diverters.

Lukas slid his deadened left hand into the inner rim of the red cast-iron wheel. His right hand took the outer spoke. 

Between his boots and the catwalk decking lay three inches of open space. Directly below the catwalk ran the high-velocity fog manifold—a bank of twenty-four solid-brass atomizing nozzles originally installed to suppress tunnel diesel fires by converting five hundred gallons of water a minute into a wall of impenetrable, micro-droplet mist.

He didn't have a weapon. He didn't want one. A bullet in a concrete bore did nothing but ricochet until it found a human eye.

The lead AlpSec operator stepped directly into the crosshairs of the fog array.

Lukas set his boots against the catwalk’s angle iron. He didn't pull with his arms—his frozen fingers had no grip left to trust. He locked his elbows, dropped his hips, and threw his entire seventy-kilo frame backward into the dark.

The bronze spindle groaned. For half a heartbeat, the calcium crust inside the sixty-year-old threads resisted, biting into the brass like ground glass. Lukas felt a ligament in his right shoulder tear with a sensation like hot wire being drawn through grease.

*Crack.*

The valve wheel spun a quarter turn.

The water did not rush; it struck.

A sound like an incoming artillery shell tore through the gallery as six hundred meters of hydrostatic head smashed through the manifold seat. The atomizing nozzles screamed—a high, deafening, supersonic shriek that obliterated every human sense in a microsecond.

A blinding wall of hyper-compressed white fog exploded into the bore.

It was not a jet. It was an absolute, opaque blizzard of aerosolized glacial water driven at three hundred atmospheres. Within three seconds, the ambient temperature in the gallery plunged from four degrees above freezing to an icy, bone-stripping zero. The emerald phosphor of the AlpSec night vision goggles blew out instantly into blinding sheets of fractured glare as the micro-droplets scattered every photon of ambient infrared light.

The four men didn't curse; they couldn't draw breath. The sheer kinetic displacement of the mist created a localized hurricane that sucked the air from their lungs, filling their throats with freezing aerosol. 

Lukas clung to the vibrating guardrail, soaked to the skin in seconds despite his oiled canvas coat. Below him, through the screaming whiteout, he saw the tactical flashlights flick on—desperate, narrow spears of amber halogen cutting into the fog, illuminating nothing but a swirling, violent wall of milk.

The lead operator went to his knees, clutching his chest, his thermal smock instantly saturated and freezing stiff against his body armor. The man with the hydraulic spreader dropped the forty-pound tool into the muck, both hands flying to his faceplate as hypothermic shock seized his diaphragm.

"Fall back!" a voice rasped—ragged, choked with water, stripped of all tactical discipline. "Airlock! Back to the seal!"

They crawled. They didn't run; you couldn't run through eighty yards of zero-visibility concrete when your fingers were locking into claws and your lungs felt like they were lined with crushed glass. They dragged each other backward by their harness straps, their boots slipping in the torrent now rising three inches deep along the drainage trough.

Lukas watched from the catwalk, his chest heaving, his dead fingers locked frozen around the valve wheel. He didn't crank it further. He held the position, shivering so violently his teeth clicked against each other.

Through the shrieking mist, the heavy outer airlock door clanged shut. The mechanical latch threw home with a muffled, hollow thud from the outer access drift.

They were out. Safe, cold, alive—and locked on the mountain side of the seal.

The gallery fell into an eerie, mechanical roar, filled only with the deafening hiss of the fog bank washing the rock clean. Lukas reached out with his good right hand to ease the bypass closed, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird.

Then his gaze snagged on the concrete floor below.

Near the ruptured airlock sill, abandoned in the churn of freezing silt and water, the orange fiber-splicing case lay open. But it wasn't a splicer. 

Bolted into the shock-absorbing foam of the case was an industrial core-drill transponder, its carbide bit anchored six inches deep into the mountain’s bedrock. On its faceplate, shielded beneath a waterproof glass dome, a single amber diode was pulsing at one-second intervals.

Lukas leaned over the rail, his blood running colder than the mist.

It wasn't transmitting to the rail dispatch center in Turin. 

The low-frequency antenna was angled forty-five degrees upward—aimed straight through three thousand meters of alpine gneiss, locked directly onto the trans-European military satellite array over Frankfurt. 

And beside the pulsing diode, a digital counter was quietly running down from three hundred seconds.
