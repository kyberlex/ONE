The smell of boiled chicory and wet goat wool always brought back the winter Chiara’s father had lost his middle finger to the planer mill in Condove. That same greasy, lukewarm vapor hung heavy in the drafty belly of the roundhouse, clinging to the brick arches and the sweat-stiffened collars of thirty valley refugees huddled over the cast-iron drum stove. 

Chiara adjusted the wool blanket swaddling six-month-old Pietro, tucking the frayed border tight against the infant’s neck. Her thumbs were cracked along the cuticles from four days of washing muslin in freezing snow-melt, the skin stinging each time she touched the rough fleece. Outside, the northern gale groaned against the corrugated roof, driving drifts against the bay doors until the hinges gave off dull, metallic groans.

Across the oil-stained gravel of the engine pit, the silence didn't break; it shattered.

"I won't let you throw their names into an unvetted beam!" Giulia’s voice hit the high vaulted brick like a chipped chisel striking granite. She stood over the zinc trestle table, her left hand clamped in an iron grip around her own forearm to arrest the violent flutter of her nerves. Dark crescents hollowed her eyes, bruising her pale face after two nights without sleep. "The moment that registry leaves this valley, the prosecutors in Turin don't just see a relief register. They see criminal syndication under the cross-border directives. They will freeze every municipal pension from here to Susa by dawn!"

Lukas didn't flinch. He sat on an upturned wooden crate, his parka zipped to his chin, slowly flexing the frost-stiffened tips of his left hand against the coarse weave of his work trousers. He had taken to chewing on dry matchsticks to keep his jaws from locking against the cold, a habit picked up during his early shifts on the high cable spans. He spat a sliver of sulfur-scented wood onto the floor.

"And if we don't send the names," Lukas said, his voice flat, gravel-scratched, and mercilessly low, "the distribution depot across the crest has zero proof of caloric need. They won't allocate the formula tins or the pulmonary meds. They’re running their own triage on the Swiss side, Giulia. They aren't clairvoyant. Without the ledger heads, the pack mules stay in the barn."

"It’s not just numbers, Lukas! It’s Pietro’s mother. It’s the rail retirees on the second floor!" Giulia surged forward, her knuckles striking the galvanized tabletop with a sharp clang that startled three toddlers around the stove into whimpering. "If AlpSec intercepts that burst, they won't even need a court bailiff. They will walk into the valley with a warrant list and seal the clinic doors before noon!"

"Then we starve in legal purity!" Lukas stood up so fast his bench toppled back into the drainage trench. "You’re running this shelter like a courtroom, Giulia! Out there, the mercury is ten below. The cloud deck is dropping onto Rocciamelone. In two hours, the cloud will scatter the optical transceivers to nothing—we’ll have forty percent packet loss through the blizzard, then total blackout! If we don't burst-fire the manifest right now, while the laser still cuts the ridge, we don't eat on Tuesday!"

"You touch that transmitter switch," Giulia hissed, stepping until her face was three inches from his, her spit catching the amber glare of the safety lantern, "and I will personally sever the power lead at the busbar."

"Try it," Lukas snapped, his jaw muscles knotting like twin cords. "I’ll tie you to the bench."

"That is enough!" 

Chiara didn't shout; she drove the words from her diaphragm with the blunt, practiced authority of a triage nurse who had pulled seven drowned sheep from an autumn irrigation canal. She passed the sleeping child into his mother’s shivering arms, stood upright, and wiped her chapped hands on her apron. 

The two of them froze, chests heaving, their breath forming rapid, ragged plumes of gray fog in the freezing depot air. Between them lay the brass stopwatch—its glass face cracked across the second track—and the bound pages of the valley compact.

Chiara walked between them, her leather soles crunching sharply on the cinders. She looked at Lukas’s blanched, stubborn face, then at Giulia’s twitching eyelid and the bloodless white of her clenched fists. The first-stage five-minute active listening rule had clearly evaporated ten minutes ago, swallowed by panic and exhaustion.

"Look at each other," Chiara said, her voice dropping into the quiet cadence that usually calmed panicked livestock. "Look at what you’re doing."

Giulia turned her face away, her teeth digging into her lower lip until a bead of dark blood welled against the dry skin. Lukas stared fixedly at the iron rail beneath his boots, his breath whistling slightly through his broken nose.

"The stopwatch didn't hold," Chiara said, tapping the cracked glass with an unpainted fingernail. "You’re past logic. You’re trading fear for spite."

"He wants to expose everyone," Giulia whispered, her voice trembling with a mixture of terror and bitter fury.

"She’d rather freeze sixty people than take a calculated operational risk," Lukas muttered through his teeth.

Chiara reached out. With a single, fluid motion, she picked up the small brass chime hanging from the roof pillar by a length of orange paracord and struck it twice with an iron bolt. 

*Clang. Clang.*

The clear, vibrating note cut through the cavernous gloom of the roundhouse, silencing the murmur of the stove circle. Several old men looked up from their card game near the locomotive tender.

"Article Four, Stage Two," Chiara said evenly. "The twenty-four-hour break. Right now."

Giulia’s head whipped around. "Chiara, no. The laser window closes in two hours—"

"Then it closes," Chiara cut in without an ounce of warmth. "The Covenant does not bend because you’re terrified, and it does not bend because Lukas is impatient. You two do not speak a syllable to one another until tomorrow's noon bell. Not a word. Not a nod. Not a written note."

Lukas swore under his breath, his shoulders slumping under the heavy duck canvas of his coat. "We have twenty tons of slush clogging the high penstock screens. If the intake chokes, the head falls, the turbine trips, and the battery dies anyway. Who clears the rack?"

"You do," Chiara said, pointing a finger at his chest, then shifting it to Giulia. "Both of you. Together."

Giulia recoiled slightly. "I have legal briefs to prepare for the cantonal ombudsman—"

"The ombudsman is asleep in Sion," Chiara said bluntly. "And your fingers are shaking so badly you couldn't write your own surname without tearing the paper. You want to preserve this shelter? You earn it with your backs. Grab the scrapers. Go to the headrace flume. You will clear the intake ice side-by-side until midnight. In silence."

Neither moved for three heartbeats. In the drafty rafters, a pigeon fluttered, its wings slapping dry against the pine trusses. 

Then Lukas reached down, grabbed his thick deerskin mittens from the dirt, and turned toward the tunnel portal leading to the mountain intake without another word. Giulia lingered for a single, agonized second, her eyes pleading with Chiara for an exception, a reprieve, some bureaucratic loophole. Chiara met her gaze with an immovable, stony calm. 

Giulia turned and followed him into the dark passage, the heavy oak door thudding shut behind her.

Chiara let out a slow, ragged breath that made her collarbone ache. She sat down on the crate Lukas had vacated, her knees trembling beneath her heavy skirt. Her boots were soaked through from the morning's meltwater; her toes felt like dead wooden pegs. She picked up the brass chime, setting it back on its hook, her hand lingering on the cold metal. 

Beside her, Matteo Rossi shuffled out of the shadow of the diesel crane, a stained canvas mug of steaming chicory in his grease-blackened hand. He didn't offer a platitude. He just held out the mug, his right knee clicking audibly with every shift of his weight.

"You pushed them hard, Chiara," he said quietly, his voice raspy from decades of breathing fine stone dust in the rail cuts.

"They were going to rip the room apart," Chiara murmured, wrapping both hands around the hot tin, savoring the scorched heat that seeped into her stiff palms. "If the people see them break, the panic spreads to the stove. Then someone opens the gate to the first bailiff who promises a loaf of bread."

"Aye," Matteo agreed, leaning his hip against the zinc table. "Lukas was always stubborn. Even as an apprentice on the line, he’d rather skin his shins to the bone than admit a cable was rigged backward."

Before Chiara could lift the cup to her lips, the red pilot lamp above the air-gapped terminal flickered once, died, and then flared with an erratic, violet hum.

The optical receiver on the roof was screaming.

Matteo frowned, setting his spanner down on the wood. "That isn't the Swiss burst. It's too wide."

Chiara jumped up, setting the mug aside. The terminal screen didn't display the clean, synchronized pulse of the Valais relay. Across the cathode sweep, a jagged series of telemetry spikes appeared—not data, but raw, physical disruption. The carrier frequency was dropping, its wavelength stretching into chaotic white noise.

Something up in the couloir had just severed the line.

Outside, above the howling wind, a sharp, concussive crack echoed down the granite throat of the gorge—not the deep thunder of a natural snow slide, but the unmistakable, whipping whip-snap of high-tensile steel cable shearing under sudden, catastrophic load.
