The sleet striking the corrugated zinc roof of the Modane marshalling yard sounded like shovelfuls of coarse salt flung against sheet metal.

Alexandre Favre dug his thumbnail into the crease behind his right ear, pressing until the sharp, localized pain momentarily eclipsed the blinding migraine behind his eye. The neon tube buzzing overhead in the switchman’s shanty flickered at a sickening fifty hertz, turning the snow outside into a strobe of gray static. To keep his stomach from heaving, he bit into a dried sliver of gentian root he kept in his watch pocket—a bitter, woody tonic his grandmother had sworn by for alpine sickness, tasting of turned loam and cold river stones.

Beside him, the iron stove glowed cherry-red around its lower draft vents, reeking of burned chicory and sulfurous briquettes. 

"The optical pulse dropped three frames out of ten," Alexandre said, his voice raspy from twenty hours of cold drafts. He pointed through the salt-crusted glass at the infrared transceiver bolted to the portal lintel. Its housing was caked in rime, shivering as sixty-knot gusts swept down from the Col du Fréjus. "The beam is scattering in the spindrift off Rocciamelone. We're losing packet parity with the Italian valley."

A man in a grease-stained canvas coat—stitched across the shoulder with the faded red hexagon of the national rail company—stepped up to the frostbitten pane. Jean-Luc Veyrat did not look like a conspirator; he looked like forty-two winters of grease-gun shifts on the mountain line between Chambéry and Turin. His knuckles were permanently swollen, blue-black around the cuticles from decades of coal slag and cold bronze.

"Doesn't matter what the light beam says," Jean-Luc spat into the coal scuttle. "The rails are talking."

He laid a gloved palm against the window casing. Even through the timber sills, the vibration was unmistakable: a low, harmonic shudder humming up through twelve kilometers of granite from the belly of Mount Cenis. Not the rhythmic thrum of an electric freight locomotive with dynamic braking engaged, but the chaotic, thudding resonance of ten thousand boots striking timber ties in unison, preceded by the sharp, metallic clang of open-end track wrenches beating against the cold steel web of the rails.

Beyond the shanty, gathered in the throat of the northern portal where the double-track line vanished into the dark maw of the mountain, four hundred men and women stood in the blizzard. 

They wore yellow safety tabards, oilskin cloaks, thick woolen hunting jackets from the high hamlets of Aussois and Fourneaux. Some held railway kerosene lanterns that smelled of sweet petroleum; others gripped heavy steel shunting poles. Behind them, idling on the bypass siding with its headlights cutting clean yellow cones through the falling sleet, sat an eighty-ton shunter engine, steam venting softly from its auxiliary heater pipes.

Fifty meters up the approach track, blocking the access lane from the departmental road, stood three blue vans of the *Gendarmerie Nationale*. 

Their roof-mounted beacons threw slow, rhythmic sweeps of sapphire across the wet limestone ballast. Major Duclos stood in the slush before the lead van, his heavy winter cape buttoned to the chin, a clipboard tucked beneath his arm inside a clear vinyl waterproof sleeve. He was a Maurienne native whose father had retired from the very aluminum smelter that used to draw power from the valley's penstocks. He had not drawn his sidearm. He simply stood with his gloved hands clasped behind his back, flanked by six young gendarmes whose breath rose in panicked, ragged puffs beneath the rims of their riot visors.

Alexandre pushed open the shanty door. The frozen wind hit his face like wet canvas, shocking his lungs. He walked down the ballast bank, his boots crunching through the crusted snow, Jean-Luc keeping pace half a step behind, a seventy-millimeter switch spanner swinging easily at his knee.

"Favre," Major Duclos called out as they reached the perimeter sawhorse. The officer’s tone carried no theatrical malice, only the hollow, dead-eyed exhaustion of a public servant caught between ministerial panic in Lyon and four hundred neighbors he saw every Sunday at the market in Saint-Jean. "The prefect signed the emergency decree twenty minutes ago. Total closure of the international bore under the trans-alpine security protocol. You cannot bring this crowd past the yard signals."

"There are five thousand people walking through the dark toward this portal right now, Major," Alexandre said, fighting the tremor in his jaw as the wind sliced through his woolen scarf. "Elderly pensioners from Bussoleno. Children from the lower valley. If you drop the automated blast curtain at Kilometer Zero, the draft reverses. You’ll back up the ventilation shafts with damp exhaust and suffocate them in the gallery."

Duclos looked toward the black throat of the tunnel. The metallic rhythm of wrenches striking iron was louder now, echoing out of the stone like a bronze bell tolling underground.

"I have direct orders from the regional transport directorate," Duclos said, his voice tightening. "The catenary power on the Italian side was severed five minutes ago. Bardonecchia reports unauthorized assembly. The commercial operator in Frankfurt has invoked asset-preservation status on the rolling stock and the right-of-way. We are required to secure the infrastructure until federal marshals arrive from Chambéry."

"With what?" Jean-Luc stepped forward, raising his wrench until the heavy jaw of the tool hovered at chest height. "You going to drive those three light vans onto the rails, Julien? The ballast is knee-deep. You couldn’t turn a steering wheel ten meters past the switch frogs."

"Jean-Luc," Duclos said softly, the professional glaze cracking to reveal a sick, hollow dread. "Don't do this. I have eighteen men. If the ministry classifies this as an illegal border breach, they will send the mobile squadrons from Grenoble by sunrise."

"Then let them come by sunrise," Alexandre said. "By sunrise, the register is through."

From the yard control tower above the track ladder, an electric horn began to wail—a shrill, automated warble. High on the concrete portal lintel, the red indicator lamps for the automated fire-isolation shutter turned amber. With a grinding scream of ungreased planetary gears, a five-ton corrugated manganese-steel curtain began its descent from the masonry vault, sliding down heavy vertical channel guides toward the railhead.

"The dispatch center in Lyon," Jean-Luc swore, lunging forward. "They’ve taken remote override on the safety interlocks!"

"The manual winch chamber," Alexandre barked, sprinting past the gendarmerie sawhorse before Duclos could raise a hand. 

His migraine vanished behind a sudden spike of adrenaline that burned cold in his veins. His boots slipped on the icy timber of the crossing, but he caught himself against the masonry buttress of the portal. Jean-Luc was already on his heels, followed by three young track workers in grease-soaked Carhartt jackets.

Duclos yelled something behind them—a frantic, legalistic warning lost in the howling wind—but neither his men nor his boots moved across the rail line.

Alexandre hit the low steel access door of the winch chamber with his shoulder. The latch yielded with a crack of sheared frost. Inside, the chamber smelled of mineral oil, dead mice, and seventy years of pulverized brake dust. 

Above their heads, the massive drum of the counterweighted descent mechanism was spinning, unwinding thick, grease-roped steel cables as the blast curtain dropped toward the tracks outside. The steel was already down past the three-meter mark; through the lower opening, Alexandre could see the yellow flares of the Italian marchers flickering in the deep interior curves of the tunnel.

"Dog-clutch!" Jean-Luc roared, throwing his entire weight onto the counterweight release lever. "Lock the dog!"

The lever kicked back like a mule, catching the older railwayman in the ribs with a dull thud that drove the air from his lungs. He fell to his knees on the concrete, gasping.

Alexandre grabbed the track wrench from the floor. He lunged beneath the spinning teeth of the secondary gear train, smelling the scorching friction of the bronze brake bands. There was no schematic to consult, no time to calculate load ratings. He drove the solid manganese head of the seventy-millimeter tool straight into the meshing teeth of the bull gear.

The impact sounded like an artillery shell striking an anvil. 

The wrench bit into the gear teeth. Iron shrieked. The drum shuddered violently, throwing off a cloud of oxidized bronze dust that filled Alexandre’s throat with the taste of pennies. The cable snapped taut with a twang that shook the chamber walls, and the descent stopped dead—the blast curtain frozen two meters above the railhead, dangling like a guillotine blade caught on its pins.

Alexandre backed out of the machinery pit, his palms scraped raw, his chest heaving against the sulfurous mountain air. 

Outside, a mighty roar rose from the throat of the tunnel.

The Italian vanguard had reached the portal mouth. Through the two-meter gap beneath the suspended steel curtain, lantern light spilled out onto the snow in long, triumphant golden fingers. The first figures emerged not as an invading army, but on foot, pushing handcarts laden with woolen blankets and wooden crates of ledger drives: women in heavy parkas, weathered rail workers from Piedmont, their faces gray with rock dust and tunnel soot, their eyes wide with disbelief.

From the northern yard, the French cheminots did not wait for orders. They dropped their sawhorses, bypassed the motionless gendarmes, and ducked beneath the hanging steel barrier, reaching out with gloved hands to pull the Italian carts across the border switch. 

Hands found shoulders. Loaves of crusty bread from the valley bakeries were thrust into frozen fingers alongside flasks of scalding mountain coffee. In the blue sweep of the gendarmerie lights, the frontier ceased to exist; it dissolved into a dense, breathing tapestry of wool, steam, and shared bread.

Alexandre leaned against the damp granite lintel, watching the lines converge. Beside him, Jean-Luc leaned on his good knee, his hand pressed against his bruised ribs, laughing silently into the snow.

Then, from deep inside the mountain bore, high-voltage static hissed through the overhead catenary. 

A sharp, repeating crack echoed down the tunnel from the Italian south—the distant, mechanical sound of hydraulic plows tearing through ballast ties, followed by the dull, rolling concussive boom of an alpine bypass gate slamming shut under high hydrostatic head. 

The line had been severed behind them. The pass was closed. There was no going back.
