Matteo moved before his lungs could draw the freeze. 

There was no speech in it, no shouted warning to startle the crush of women and shivering children filtering through the half-raised steel door of Bay 2. He seized a five-foot track chisel resting in the snow against the door jamb, took two dragging, uneven strides on his bad right knee, and brought the flattened steel wedge down like an axe into the slush. 

The blade bit through the trailing orange cord, driving it three inches deep into the gravel bed between the ties. 

The man in the road-crew parka spun around. For half a second his eyes met Matteo’s—not wide with panic, but calculated, cold, measuring the distance between Matteo’s raised iron bar and the severed end whipping harmlessly in the draft of the open bay. The infiltrator did not reach for a holster. He didn’t scream. With the quiet, practiced economy of a man paid by the hour to survive, he wheeled into the blizzard, melted past the corner of the substation wall, and vanished into the swirling white gloom of the switchyard.

Matteo didn't chase him. His chest contracted, an involuntary, hacking spasm tearing through his windpipe that tasted of rust and ash—the familiar bite of silicosis reminding him that three running steps at sub-zero temperatures was an extravagance his scarred bronchial tubes could barely afford. He spat thick phlegm into the churned snow, leaning heavily on the chisel until the world stopped spinning.

Behind him, the crowd continued to shuffle inward, drawn toward the faint steam rising from the ballast ditches where the turbine’s tepid runoff kept the air a fraction above freezing.

A gloved hand caught Matteo’s shoulder, steadying him before his knee buckled.

Lukas was there, his knitted wool cap pulled low over his forehead, his face pale under grease smudges and rimed with hoarfrost. His left hand—the two middle fingers stiffened and yellowed from an old frostbite sustained on the high catenary towers—gripped Matteo’s canvas sleeve with awkward, clumsy strength. Lukas smelled of damp wool, bitter chicory, and the pungent clove oil he rubbed into his aching joints before every shift.

"Tracks," Lukas whispered, his breath puffing white against Matteo’s cheek. He pointed toward the southern approach. "Feel that?"

Matteo dropped the chisel, dropped to one knee despite the sharp protest of his arthritic joint, and laid his bare palm directly onto the icy crown of the eastern running rail.

The cold was immediate, burning through the skin like a hot plate. But beneath the frost, the iron was singing.

A low, rhythmic harmonic hum vibrated up through the web of the steel rail into his wrist bones. It wasn't the slow, wallowing vibration of an empty passenger coach drifting down the gradient. It was dense, dead weight. High axle-load. Steel wheels rolling under forced diesel power, churning against frozen gravel.

"Hi-rail loaders," Matteo rasped, pushing himself back up with an iron groan. "AlpSec. They aren't trying the highway gates. They’re running straight up the spur from the mainline siding."

"They've got ballast plows mounted to the front bumpers," Lukas said, his voice flat, stripped of fear by sheer exhaustion. He had spent four straight hours rigging optical lasers on the roof in a twenty-knot gale; his eyelids fluttered with the heavy, narcotic pull of deep fatigue. "If they hit the throat switch at twenty miles an hour, they’ll shear the point rods, force the points open, and wedge the bay doors before we can dog down the roll-latches. Once they punch the envelope, the warm air vents out. The floor ditches freeze in twenty minutes."

Matteo looked toward Bay 2. Inside, fifty people—shopkeepers from Susa, track laborers, two families with swaddled infants who hadn't eaten warm food since the power lines snapped at dusk—were settling onto pallets laid across dry cross-ties. A breach wouldn't just scatter them; the cold would kill the weakest before dawn.

"We don't hold the throat with wood," Matteo said. He reached into his coat pocket, pulling out a small strip of dried cedar bark he kept to chew when the craving for tobacco became violent, wedging it between his back teeth. "Siding four. Move."

"Siding four?" Lukas blinked through snow-crusted lashes. "The scrap siding? Matteo, those are dead iron."

"That’s why they’re going to work."

They crossed the apron at a half-run, keeping low beneath the beam of the sodium floodlights mounted to the roundhouse eaves. The wind off the Cenisio was howling now, driving pellets of hard graupel that stung the exposed skin of their faces like birdshot. Every yard they traversed was a battle against the elements: the snow was already ankle-deep over the guide rails, hiding frozen grease traps and discarded switch levers that could shatter an ankle in the dark.

Siding four branched away from the main roundhouse lead, curving up a subtle three-degree grade into an open-ended storage spur used for seasonal snow clearance equipment. Parked bumper-to-bumper on the dead track were two decommissioned diesel-electric shunting locomotives built in the early 1970s. Their orange and green livery had long since faded to a matte chalk; their traction motors were stripped, their fuel tanks dry, their air systems dead and unpressurized for over a decade.

Eighty tons of cast-iron engine block, plate-steel frames, and solid steel wheelsets apiece. One hundred and sixty tons of unyielding kinetic inertia parked sixty yards uphill from the junction switch.

"The air brakes are bled out," Lukas shouted over the screaming gale, lunging toward the rear bogie of the lead locomotive. "The triple-valves are frozen solid! How do you release the handbrakes?"

"Manual wheel in the cab!" Matteo swung himself onto the rusted iron ladder of the first locomotive, his boots slipping on the rime-covered rungs. The cold of the handrail bit through his canvas work mittens, leaching the warmth straight out of his palms. "Lukas! The brake shoes on the outside wheels! They’re frozen to the treads with melt-ice. Take the sledge! Crack the shoes free!"

Lukas didn't argue. He unhooked a twelve-pound cross-peen sledgehammer from the wall rack by the sand house. He stepped between the rails, swinging low and hard with his good right arm. The steel face of the hammer struck the cast-iron brake shoe of the third axle with a flat, ringing *clink* that echoed off the granite walls of the valley. Ice shattered into diamond dust. He hit it again. The shoe fell away from the wheel rim with a rusty squeal.

Above him, inside the cab, Matteo was fighting his own war.

The locomotive cab smelled of forty years of stale lubricating oil, dead mice, and damp horsehair insulation. The small glass dials on the control stand were spider-webbed with cracks, opaque with frost. Through the forward windscreen, smeared with grit, Matteo could see the twin halogen headlamps of AlpSec’s lead armored loader emerging through the blizzard at the far end of the throat.

The vehicle was a monster: an eight-ton industrial front-loader fitted with steel track-guide wheels and a heavy V-plow bolted to its hydraulic arms. It moved forward steadily, shoving a two-foot wave of dirty snow and severed signal cable ahead of its snout, the roar of its turbocharged diesel engine bouncing hard off the valley gorge.

Behind it, half-obscured by the snow, rolled two troop-carrying crew trucks on rail gear, their windows shielded with welded steel mesh.

Matteo threw his weight against the iron handbrake wheel mounted beside the driver’s pedestal.

It didn't move. The dog clutch was seized with dried tallow and road salt. 

He dug his heels into the ribbed floor plates, locked his elbows, and wrenched with his entire torso. A tearing pain lanced through his right shoulder, down into the ribs where his silicosis had thinned the tissue. He gasped, choking on the freezing air, his vision tunneling into gray pinpricks.

*One hundred and sixty tons.*

Down below, Lukas was swinging the sledge with mechanical, desperate rhythm, clearing the last bogie of the second engine. "Matteo! The switch! The ground-throw lever is frozen! I have to kick the points over before they roll, or they’ll divert into the snowbank!"

"Crack the shoes!" Matteo roared from the cab door. "I’ve got the wheel!"

He unhitched a three-foot length of steel pipe he had dragged up the steps—an old locomotive injector pipe—and slipped it over the spoke of the brake wheel, creating a cheater bar. He planted both feet on the steel bulkhead, threw his shoulders back, and heaved.

The iron screamed. 

A sharp, explosive *crack* echoed inside the cab as the rust shear-pin broke. The wheel spun violently beneath his hands, throwing the cheater bar against the windscreen, shattering the frosted glass.

Beneath his feet, the eighty-ton steel frame shuddered.

Gravity, silent and patient, took hold of the dead iron.

The two locomotives groaned as their journal boxes turned for the first time in ten years. Sluggishly, reluctantly, the heavy wheels began to roll down the three-degree grade toward the throat.

"Lukas! Jump clear!" Matteo yelled, scrambling down the rear steps into the whipping snow. He hit the ballast hard, his bad knee buckling, sending him sliding across the icy gravel.

Lukas was already at the throat switch. He had wedged the sledgehammer under the manual ground-throw handle, throwing all sixty kilos of his frame onto the lever to force the rusted switch points against the stock rail. The mechanism fought him, packed with frozen snow. Lukas screamed—a raw, high sound of sheer physical exertion—and slammed his boot down on the head of the hammer.

The iron point snapped shut with a hollow *thunk*.

Lukas rolled sideways into the ditch just as the front pilot of the lead diesel ground past him.

The dead locomotives gained momentum with terrifying silence, coasting downhill like two blind iron whales. Thirty tons of unpowered mass hit fifteen miles an hour, rumbling through the switch points and veering diagonally across the throat of the yard.

At the exact same instant, AlpSec’s lead armored loader entered the diamond.

The impact was not an explosion, but a horrific, low-frequency folding of heavy structural steel. The V-plow of the loader punched into the locomotive’s front cowcatcher, crumpling the loader’s hydraulic rams like tin foil. The massive kinetic energy of the two diesels didn't stop; they shoved the eight-ton front loader sideways, derailing its front rail-gear and driving its massive rubber drive-tires off the ballast into the frozen drainage ditch.

With a deafening shriek of sheared flange metal, the second diesel locomotive jackknifed behind the first. Its heavy rear bogie jumped the frogs, slewed sideways across the adjacent spur, and dropped off the railheads with a concussive thud that shook the foundations of the roundhouse.

The throat was gone. 

Both dead locomotives lay twisted across the tracks in a sixty-foot barricade of immovable scrap, wedged tight between the high granite retaining wall of the valley and the reinforced concrete footing of the bridge pier. The switch rails beneath them were bent into pretzels, ripped clean from their oak ties.

AlpSec’s loader engine stalled out with a wet, dying rattle of belts and escaping steam. The high-intensity halogen beams of the crew trucks behind it painted the wreckage in harsh white light, illuminating the armored doors of the personnel carriers as they clicked open.

From the dark ditch, Lukas hauled Matteo to his feet by his belt.

"The spur is locked," Lukas breathed, his face grey, spitting blood where his lip had cracked against the ballast. "Nothing on wheels is getting through that gap for a week."

Matteo leaned against Lukas’s shoulder, chest heaving in shallow, ragged rasps, watching the dark silhouettes of AlpSec commandos dropping from the trucks onto the snow fifty yards away, their hands gripping mechanical hydraulic shears and thermal torches.

Then, from five hundred meters straight up the mountain face, above the tree line where the high-pressure penstock pipe descended from the reservoir, a new sound cut through the howl of the blizzard.

A dull, rhythmic clanking. The rapid, mechanical chatter of high-pressure pneumatic impact wrenches echoing off the cliffside rock.

Matteo looked up into the swirling darkness, his stomach dropping like lead.

"They aren't trying to breach the yard anymore," Matteo whispered. "They’re cutting the penstock flume."
