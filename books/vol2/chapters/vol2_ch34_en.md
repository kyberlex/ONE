The frost had worked its way into the seams of Marshal Vincenzo Moretti’s service boots, turning the stiff leather around his ankles into bands of frozen iron. At forty-nine, after twenty-two winters stationed between the lower Dora Riparia and the high gaps of the Cenisio, he knew the exact progression of alpine cold: first the dull ache in the shins, then the phantom numbness along the outer toes, followed by the sour burn of half-curdled espresso in an empty stomach. 

Above them, through the pine branches weighted down with rime, the Rocciamelone gave off a long, groaning shriek. It wasn't the wind. It was water—thousands of tons of high-head glacial runoff, ripped from its steel flume three thousand feet up the sheer rock face, hitting the sub-zero night air and freezing into falling daggers of mountain ice. The spray settled as a fine, stinging diamond dust over the muddy railbed, glazing the hoods of the three dark blue Carabinieri Alfa Romeos and the matte-black tactical vans belonging to AlpSec.

A glove struck the roof of Moretti’s cruiser.

"They're stalling, Marshal," said Stranieri, the private security coordinator assigned by the Turin receiver’s office. Stranieri wore an insulated alpine shell with no insignia, only an embroidered radio harness and ballistic goggles pushed up over a neoprene balaclava. His breath smelled of mint lozenges and stale tobacco. "The injunction is absolute. Asset seizure, Article 814. Every minute that penstock runs wild, the infrastructure loses twenty percent of its residual recovery value. We don't wait for the court's dawn courier."

Moretti did not shift his boots. He kept his hands buried deep in the wool-lined pockets of his greatcoat, his fingers working a smooth wooden rosary bead his mother had pressed into his palm during his first posting to Calabria thirty years ago. 

"Look at the ridge, Doctor," Moretti said, his voice scraped raw by the draft cutting down the gorge. "The bypass flume peeled off the granite five minutes ago. If you push men onto that apron, you aren't conducting a recovery. You’re recovering corpses from under an icefall."

"My riggers know their business," Stranieri snapped. He signaled toward the two armored transports parked across the narrow-gauge spur. Six men in slate-grey load-bearing vests stepped down into the slush. They weren't carrying standard service arms; their hands rested on stubby, multi-chambered rotary launchers, the orange bands on the barrels identifying the canisters nested in the drums. "We breach the freight doors on the eastern siding. Three rounds of CS micro-powder through the lower ventilation louver. The intake draft will pull the agent through the machine pits in forty seconds. They’ll come out crawling."

Moretti turned his head slowly. The cold seemed to slow even the movement of his neck. "There is no exhaust running in that pit, Stranieri. The main turbine is off its governor. If you drop chemical irritant into a sunken cellar with seventy bars of pressurized water spraying across the deck, the gas won't clear. It will settle in the inspection sumps."

"Then they should have vacated when the bailiff posted the gate," Stranieri said, pulling a laminated operational order from his chest pocket and holding it inches from Moretti’s frosted collar pins. "Signed by the deputy prosecutor at midnight. Full authorization for riot suppression within the zone of vital transport infrastructure. You have four patrolmen here. Either they establish the gas perimeter, or AlpSec takes the breach and your detachment faces Section 328 for failure to perform an official duty."

Moretti looked past the laminate. 

Through the cracked industrial glass of the roundhouse’s eastern dispatch gallery, forty paces through the sleet, a paraffin lantern flickered. Behind the pane, framed by the iron crossbars, Chiara Leone was kneeling beside an upturned wooden crate. She was sixteen, her shoulders pinched beneath an oversized railway uniform coat, her breath forming small, rapid clouds in the frigid air as she adjusted an aluminum foil blanket around an infant. On the masonry jamb beside the doorframe, hung from two rusted spikes, was a white linen bedsheet marked with a crude, hand-painted red cross.

Beside her stood Matteo Rossi, leaning his bad hip against an eighty-ton iron ore hopper, his split thumb wrapped in greasy canvas, a heavy mechanical dog-wrench resting against his knee like an old soldier’s cane. He wasn't manning an barricade; he was watching the ceiling, where the water pipes shivered with the rhythmic thud of air pockets slamming through the penstock.

"They have nursing children in that hall," Moretti said. He spoke without raising his pitch, but the gravel in his throat sounded like river stones grinding together.

"They have trespassers in that hall," Stranieri countered. "And saboteurs who just severed the high-pressure link to the valley grid. Frankfurt took a margin failure ten minutes ago because the hydro credits stopped routing through the Swiss switchyard. Every second that gate remains closed, the pension clearinghouses are liquidating secondary debt. We aren't discussing social services, Marshal. We are clearing a critical utility."

The private contractor raised his right hand toward the squad in slate grey. "Team two, load canisters. Target the lower louvers beneath the timber staging."

The metallic clatter of rotary cylinders snapping into place echoed off the wet rock of the cutting. The two lead contractors racked the slides of their gas launchers with a sharp, hydraulic *clack-clack*.

Moretti didn't draw his weapon. In thirty years between the barracks of Palermo and the mountain passes of Piedmont, he had pulled his Beretta from its holster four times, and each time had left a scar across his ribs that ached whenever the barometer plunged. Instead, he stepped out from the shelter of the cruiser's door frame, his heavy boots sinking three inches into the half-frozen ballast.

He walked out into the narrow lane between the mercenary line and the iron doors of the roundhouse. 

"Carabiniere Scarlatti," Moretti said, his voice flat, carrying across the whistling wind.

From the front passenger seat of the lead squad car, a young corporal with ears red from the heater's failure leaned out into the weather. "Sir?"

"Log the time," Moretti instructed. "Zero-two-forty-one hours. Temperature minus seven. High-pressure hydraulic failure on the upper face, falling ice exceeding forty kilograms per fragment."

Stranieri took three quick, aggressive steps through the snow, closing the distance until his tactical vest nearly brushed Moretti’s wool shoulder. "What the hell do you think you’re doing, Vincenzo?"

"I am observing the conditions," Moretti said. He reached inside his coat, not for a sidearm, but for his hardbound service notebook, bound with black oilcloth and held shut with an elastic band. He slipped his pencil from the spine. "I am recording that the private security detail contracted by AlpSec Tactical intends to discharge concentrated 2-chlorobenzylidene malononitrile into an unventilated basement housing non-combatants, beneath a pending structural collapse, in direct violation of Article 51 of the Penal Code and the international conventions governing designated civilian triage stations."

"This is not a war zone!" Stranieri hissed, his spit flecking Moretti’s cheek and freezing instantly against the stubble. "This is an industrial eviction!"

"Then you are bound by the civil safety protocols of the Ministry of the Interior," Moretti replied, writing with steady, deliberate strokes, the graphite scratching loud against the damp paper. "Which forbid the dispersal of respiratory irritants in enclosed subterranean spaces where egress is blocked by physical debris. That door is warped by the frost, Stranieri. If you flood that pit with gas, those children will choke on their own vomit before they find the latch."

"The court order covers us," Stranieri said, his fingers tightening on the grip of his sidearm holster. Behind him, the six operators hesitated, their launcher barrels dipping slightly toward the mud as they looked between their paymaster and the uniform of the state. "You don't have the jurisdiction to countermand a prefectural seizure, Marshal."

"I am not countermanding the seizure," Moretti said. He snapped the elastic band back around the notebook and slid it back into his pocket, buttoning the wool flap over it with cold, stiff fingers. "I am executing my duty under Article 40 of the Code of Military Discipline. An order that constitutes a manifest crime against human life requires immediate, formal refusal."

He stepped another yard forward, placing the broad, six-foot-two frame of his greatcoat directly in front of the lower air louvers. 

Behind the glass, Chiara’s eyes were wide, white circles against the dark grime of the maintenance pit. Matteo had stepped up beside her, his hand flat against the cold glass, not moving, not shouting, just waiting to see which way the valley would break.

"Corporal Scarlatti," Moretti called out again, not looking back.

"Marshal?"

"Move the cars across the apron. Block the siding. If these gentlemen fire a single gas canister toward that timbering, you will arrest every man on the line for reckless endangerment and aggravated assault against public officials."

A heavy, suffocating silence dropped over the cutting, broken only by the frantic wailing of the Pelton wheel inside the mountain and the rhythmic *drip-drip-drip* of thawing slush falling from the eaves. 

Stranieri’s face had drained of blood, leaving his skin the color of dirty river ice. His radio chirped twice—a rapid, panicked sequence of digital squawks from the Turin dispatch desk, demanding an update on the bypass gate. He didn't answer it. His eyes were locked on Moretti's chest, where the faded ribbons of two decades of mountain rescue and rural policing sat pinned over the wool.

"You're finished, Moretti," the contractor whispered, his voice shaking with the suppressed fury of a man whose margins were vanishing into the dark. "The prefecture will strip those stripes by noon."

"Then by noon I’ll be sleeping," Moretti said quietly. "Until then, the law stands on this side of the rail."

A crack like an artillery shell split the air above them.

High on the north face, three hundred feet above the gallery roof, the frozen plume of atomized penstock water gave way. A slab of black-veined ice the size of a freight engine sheared away from the granite shoulder, tumbling out into the empty night air directly above the roundhouse skylights.
