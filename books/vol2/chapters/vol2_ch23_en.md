The serrated halo of a fresh migraine was already chewing into the edge of Alexandre’s vision when the news broadcast broke over the valley’s pirate frequency. 

High in the former control gallery of the roundhouse, perched on a rusted iron catwalk thirty feet above the dormant turbine pit, he kept his fingers moving across the mechanical keyboard. Down in the snow outside, the tracked crawlers were idling at the switchback, their twin halogen beams slicing through the blizzard like paired broadswords. They hadn't charged the perimeter gate yet. They were waiting.

Then the small cathode-ray monitor beside his switchboard crackled, its tube warming to reveal the slick, fluorescent-lit studio of Tele-Piemonte in Turin.

"A catastrophic loss of municipal water pressure across the Lower Susa basin," the anchor was saying, her voice pitched with carefully modulated civic dread. Behind her, a graphic flared in urgent amber: a stylized map of the Cenisio reservoir with an arrow showing the lake’s volume draining away into an ominous red void. "Regional emergency coordinators report that an rogue cadre occupying the decommissioned rail workshop has seized the high-head penstocks. By diverting the winter reserve into private generation, the commune has compromised the drinking supply of forty thousand citizens, threatening local hospital heating systems during the coldest freeze of the decade."

Alexandre let out a sharp, ragged breath that smelled of the dried larch needles he had been chewing since midnight to stave off nausea. In the Jura valleys where he had grown up watching his father true brass escapements by candle flicker, men didn't tell lies about water. You could cheat on wood tariffs or forge a timber stamp, but water belonged to the stone; you could not pretend a river had vanished when every ditch still ran downhill. 

He snatched the hardwired copper intercom horn off its wall bracket. "Giulia. Do you have the monitor on?"

"I hear it," Giulia’s voice came back through the horn, thinned by copper wire but taut as a piano string. The distant clatter of iron scrapers sounded behind her—Matteo and Lukas desperately shoveling slag against the south doors. "They’re building the pretext. The Carabinieri at the valley depot won’t cross the railway embankment on a corporate eviction warrant, but an emergency public health order? Alexandre, if the prefect signs a water diversion decree, the tactical crawlers will ram the doors in twenty minutes, and the whole valley will cheer when they drag us out."

"They're lying about the mass balance," Alexandre said, pressing the cold horn against his temple to numb the throbbing behind his left eye. "A Pelton turbine doesn't drink water. It steals velocity, not mass. We drop the volume six hundred vertical meters, slam it into the bronze buckets, and dump it straight out the tailrace flume into the Cenischia torrent. Every drop that hits the runner returns to the river before it reaches the town intake."

"Tell that to the prefect," she snapped, the tremor in her hands audible in the rattle of the handset. "The morning papers are already printing the syndicate’s press release. The local mayors are calling for our heads. If you can’t prove the river is full, Alexandre, those crawlers are coming through the sheet metal."

The intercom clicked dead. 

Alexandre dropped the horn. A fresh spasm of visual distortion flared across his retina—jagged zigzags of violet light that turned the room’s iron columns into wavering reeds. He reached into his coat pocket, pulled out two bitter needles stripped from a frozen bough, and crushed them between his molars. The sharp, resinous bite shocked his nerves back into focus. He couldn't afford darkness now. Not when the entire political weight of the province was being forged out of thin air.

On his primary drafting terminal, the river was an unspooling line of white light. 

Beneath the concrete floor of the roundhouse, where the discharge flume emptied back into the open gorge, three buoyant mechanical flow-meters bobbed in the rushing, ice-black water. They were primitive, deliberate devices—paddles turned by the current, spinning shielded magnets past copper coils to generate simple, rhythmic electrical pulses. No proprietary software, no corporate cloud, just a continuous tally of kinetic displacement: liters per second, water head, return volume.

Alexandre pulled the intake log from the upper mountain weir, brought down via the mountain's fiber sheath before the lines had been cut, and laid it beside the lower flume discharge.

The numbers matched to the third decimal place.

Six hundred and forty liters per second entering the intake tunnel beneath the frozen crest of the mountain; six hundred and thirty-eight liters splashing violently through the tailrace flume at the bottom, the tiny two-liter discrepancy accounted for by ice formation along the concrete apron. 

The commune hadn't taken a single glass of water from the valley’s drinking supply. The Pelton wheel was simply a watermill with the force of an alpine cliff behind it. The corporate syndicate had pinched the municipal supply lines downstream at their own automated pumping station in Bussoleno, deliberately strangling the valley floor to manufacture an artificial panic—and pinned the empty taps on the roundhouse.

"They want a phantom," Alexandre muttered to the empty gallery. "We give them the stone."

He opened the local public broadcast packet, bundling the raw telemetry: the continuous stream of inflow, discharge velocity, hydrostatic head, and the downstream hydrological sensors confirming the river was at normal winter baseflow. He added the physical equations, stripped of jargon, translated into simple graphical curves that any high school teacher in Susa could verify with a handheld ruler and a stopwatch at the public footbridge.

He reached for the transmit key to sling the data across the Free Space Optics transceiver mounted on the roof.

The terminal flashed amber. *Link Quality: 14%.*

Alexandre’s chest seized. He leaned forward, squinting through the violet glare of his migraine. 

The blizzard had deepened over the last half hour. The invisible infrared laser beam linking the roundhouse roof to the high relay station on Mount Rocciamelone—the single air-gapped conduit bypassing the Italian telecommunication kill-switches to reach Geneva—was striking a wall of wet, heavy alpine snow. The thick flakes were scattering the light. Nearly half the data packets were dissolving into the swirling white before they could cross the gorge.

The transmission stalled at nine percent.

Down on the switchback, the lead crawler gave a long, pneumatic hiss. Its steel tracks clanked against the frozen ballast as it shifted into low gear, creeping fifty meters closer to the inner boundary fence. The megaphone mounted on its cab crackled to life, a recorded voice booming through the snow squall with sterile legal authority:

*"In accordance with Regional Public Safety Ordinance forty-seven, all occupants are ordered to vacate the premises immediately. Water resources are being returned to public custody by order of the Prefect of Turin."*

"Not yet," Alexandre whispered, his throat raw.

He threw on his heavy sheepskin coat, shoved his frozen fingers into oilcloth mittens, and hauled open the iron hatch that led out onto the gallery roof.

The wind hit him like a slab of wet slate, driving needles of sleet into his face and tearing away his breath. The cold went straight to his teeth. He scrambled across the corrugated iron roof, keeping low to avoid the blinding sweep of the crawler’s searchlights sweeping the upper eaves. 

At the northern edge of the parapet stood the optical transceiver: a cast-aluminum housing mounted on a heavy surveyor's tripod, wrapped in insulating lagging. The glass lens at its snout was caked in a gray crust of frozen slush. The fine heating filament inside the glass had failed, chilled past its limits by the sub-zero gale.

Alexandre dropped to his knees in the drift, the ice soaking instantly through his trousers. His right shoulder throbbed where he had bruised it against the turbine casing two nights ago. He tore off his right mitten with his teeth, spat it into the snow, and pressed his bare palm directly against the frozen lens.

The shock of the ice was agonizing, a white-hot burn that made him cry out against the roar of the blizzard. But the heat of his skin softened the crust. He pulled a dry cotton rag from his inner pocket, scrubbing frantically at the glass until the sapphire-coated optic was clear.

Through the clearing lens, five miles away and three thousand meters above the valley floor, the faint, pinprick pulse of the Rocciamelone beacon flickered through the murk—a dim, red eye winking through the mountain storm.

The alignment was off. The wind had nudged the tripod by a fraction of a millimeter, throwing the beam wide of the mountain receiver.

With trembling, half-frozen fingers, Alexandre gripped the knurled brass micrometer screws at the base of the mount. Every half-turn was a battle against seizing grease and shivering muscles. He leaned his cheek against the icy metal casing, sighting along the optical tube into the black void of the peak, adjusting for wind drift, his breath coming in ragged, steaming gasps.

*One click east. Half-click north.*

On his wrist, the small field monitor taped to his forearm beeped—a sharp, clear chime that cut through the wind.

*Link Quality: 89%.*

"Go," he gasped. "Run."

The data burst evacuated the buffer in a single, high-density optical flash. 

Down in the gorge, the lead crawler hit the perimeter chain-link fence, the steel wire screaming as the tracked chassis tore it from its concrete moorings. 

Alexandre scrambled back through the hatch, slamming the iron door against the howling wind, and slid down the ladder to the gallery floor. He threw himself into his chair, his bare right hand mottled red and dead to the touch, and looked at the regional monitor.

The broadcast had not stopped, but the ticker along the bottom of the Tele-Piemonte feed suddenly froze. 

A moment later, the screen cut back to the anchor in Turin. Her polished confidence had slipped; she was touching an earpiece with a manicured hand, her eyes darting off-camera toward an unseen producer. 

"We are... we are receiving unconfirmed technical data from the valley mesh," she stammered, her voice losing its broadcast varnish. "Direct stream telemetry from the Cenischia gorge appears to show... zero net extraction. Independent hydrological sensors verified by the cantonal authorities in Martigny indicate the municipal shortage is located entirely below the railway works, at the syndicate's own pumping gate..."

Alexandre slammed his good fist onto the console. "Look at the water, you bastards. Look at the river."

Down below, the crawler’s engine revved high—then abruptly cut back to a low, idling rumble. The searchlights flickered, swinging away from the roundhouse doors and turning back toward the gorge road. The prefect’s signature had vanished from their grasp; without the narrative of stolen water, an armed assault on an active cooperative was a criminal raid on open television.

Giulia’s voice crackled through the intercom, breathless with relief: "Alexandre! They're stopping at the perimeter! The Carabinieri patrol cars just pulled up behind them with their blue lights on—they’re blocking the road!"

Alexandre let his forehead drop against the cool edge of the switchboard, his breath rattling in his chest as the room spun in the dying grip of his migraine. 

Then, through the vibrating concrete beneath his boots, he felt a new, unfamiliar shudder.

It wasn't the crawlers. It was a deep, metallic thud, echoing up from the dark heart of the mountain, deep inside the bedrock where the intake penstock gripped the cliff.

A high-frequency scream of tearing steel cut through the floorboards, followed by the immediate, sickening silence of water pressure dying in the high-head feed pipe.

Alexandre lifted his head, his skin turning to ash. 

The crawlers hadn't come to break the doors. They had come to keep everyone looking at the front gate while a demolition charge went off in the mountain flume behind them.
