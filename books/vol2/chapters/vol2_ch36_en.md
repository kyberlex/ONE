The diesel engine down in the gorge didn't roar; it grunted, a heavy, wet churning that vibrated through the steel ladder under Matteo’s work boots. 

He gripped the rungs of the signal gantry with stiff fingers, his woolen mittens long since soaked through with sleet. Below him, the switchyard of San Didero lay drowned in absolute, ink-black alpine darkness, save for the blue exhaust fumes curling off the lead vehicle crawling up the ballast spur. It was an armored twin-axle riot carrier, fitted with heavy flanged guide-wheels to ride the rails, its hull plated in dull ceramic composite. Atop its cab sat the twin high-pressure water turrets, their hydraulic elevation rams glistening with hydraulic fluid as they traversed toward the penstock substation gate.

Beside Matteo, perched on the narrow catwalk twenty feet above the frozen ties, Lukas Keller let out a ragged puff of white vapor. Lukas wasn’t humming one of his mountain ballads tonight. He had a piece of dry elderberry twig clamped between his back molars, biting down on the bitter bark to keep his jaw from rattling against the freezing wind.

"Range?" Matteo whispered. His lungs tasted of copper and ash, the old coal-dust cough clawing at his windpipe, but he swallowed it down until his throat burned.

"Eighty meters," Lukas said, his voice flat, stripped down to bare bone. He didn't look up from the junction box strapped to the gantry railing. His left hand, clumsy where the frost had deadened the tips of two fingers three winters ago, worked the brass knurling of a direct bypass switch. "They're rolling at walking pace. Trying to keep the spray from freezing in the nozzles before they reach the main yard."

Matteo leaned over the rusted safety chain. The cold mountain wind swept down from the Cenisio pass, carrying the smell of pine resin stripped raw by snow and the sharp, alkaline sting of commercial road salt. Through the gloom, the dark silhouette of the armored carrier looked less like a machine and more like an iron beetle crawling along the spine of the valley. AlpSec wasn't taking chances with court orders anymore. With the Frankfurt accounts frozen, the tactical contractors were operating on whatever emergency retainer remained in their Swiss escrows, and their orders were brutally simple: clear the turnout, force the valve gates, and restore the head-pressure to the valley grid before the morning markets opened.

A dull metallic *thunk* echoed from the vehicle. The port-side water cannon cleared its manifold with a short, coughing burst of pressurized spray. The mist hit the outer chain-link fence sixty yards out, instantly turning the wire mesh into an opaque sheet of white rime. 

If that jet caught a man at fifty paces, it wouldn't just knock him down; it would strip his flesh from the bone and freeze him to the ballast before his heart could register the shock.

"The bank line's hot," Lukas muttered, tapping Matteo’s shin with a wrench. "The locomotive cells in the shed took the full charge off the Pelton bypass. Four hundred volts DC on the trunk, floating steady."

"And the contactors?"

"Solenoids are frozen solid," Lukas spat out the splintered elderberry twig. "Ice in the housing. The spring won't trip on the remote."

Matteo didn't curse. Cursing wasted warmth, and at five below zero, every breath was an expenditure of fuel his fifty-eight-year-old ribcage couldn't spare. When he was nine, his grandmother had taught him how to skin frozen rabbits behind their stone hut in Bussoleno by warming the knife blade flat against his own belly skin beneath three layers of flannel. Mechanical things didn't care about anger; they only yielded to heat, pressure, or weight.

He swung his right leg over the catwalk rail. The knee—mended with two stainless pins after an uncoupled freight hopper had pinned him against a loading dock in the late nineties—groaned with a deep, grinding ache that traveled straight up into his hip.

"Hold the jumper," Matteo ordered.

"Matteo, if the arc jumps—"

"Hold the jumper, Lukas."

Matteo dropped his weight onto the service platform suspended directly above the switchyard feeder cables. His boots hit the diamond-plate with a sharp *clack*. Ten yards ahead, bolted to the gantry's central upright, hung a bank of four massive searchlight housings salvaged from an old E.656 electric engine that had spent twenty years hauling freight over the Frejus pass before being shunted to the dead-tracks. Each parabolic reflector was eighteen inches across, carrying heavy quartz-halogen bulbs originally built to cut through blizzards at sixty miles an hour across the high alpine tunnels. 

Below, the armored carrier picked up speed. The low rumble of its rail-bogies on the cold-welded steel track quickened: *clack-clack, clack-clack*. 

Matteo reached the solenoid casing. The galvanized iron was coated in a quarter-inch crust of frozen drizzle. He didn't have a torch, and an open flame would shatter the thermal glass of the lamps. He unfastened the top two buttons of his heavy canvas mackinaw, reached into the pocket of his wool vest, and pulled out an eight-inch steel tapered reamer—the blunt, heavy tool he had carried in his thigh pocket since his first apprentice shift at the Turin roundhouse.

He wedged the flat edge of the reamer between the iced armature and the contact plate.

"Lukas!" he barked into the wind. "On three!"

"Standing by!"

"One!"

Down on the tracks, the armored carrier’s turret whined as its electric traverse motor spun the twin nozzles upward, aligning directly with the gantry’s main platform. They had spotted the movement against the gray sky. A search beam flickered to life on the vehicle's roof—a narrow, hostile pencil of violet-tinted LED light that swept across the gantry’s underside, searching for faces.

"Two!"

Matteo threw his entire weight against the reamer. His knee screamed, the old scar tissue tearing internally like dry canvas, but he leaned into the steel with his shoulder, jamming the tool into the ice. The frozen crust gave way with a brittle *snap*. 

The pencil of LED light struck Matteo squarely in the chest. Through the glare, he heard the sharp, pneumatic hiss of the carrier's high-pressure water pump engaging its second-stage impeller. A deep, mechanical throb surged through the rails under the gantry—thousands of gallons of freezing glacial discharge being drawn from the intake trench, pressurized to eighty atmospheres, ready to tear the platform out from under their feet.

"Three! Close the loop!" Matteo roared.

He drove the reamer home, bridging the frozen breaker gaps with the hardened chrome-vanadium steel of his tool.

Lukas slammed the heavy copper knife-switch shut inside the gantry box.

The darkness of the Susa Valley didn't simply lift; it vanished in an instantaneous, violent detonation of light.

Fed directly by the massive locomotive battery bank and backed by four megawatts of direct Pelton water-power humming through the valley substation, all four locomotive searchlights ignited simultaneously. Ten thousand lumens of raw, uncurtained halogen fire erupted from the parabolic reflectors, funneled down the narrow gut of the rail cutting like a solid wall of lightning.

The pencil-thin searchlight of the armored vehicle was instantly wiped out, swallowed completely in the blazing glare. 

From the gantry, Matteo couldn't see the ground anymore. There was only an infinite, blinding white corridor carved through the freezing night. The sleet falling through the beam turned into millions of microscopic prisms, scattering the light into an impenetrable, searing fog that eliminated every contour, every rail, every edge of the physical world.

Down in the cab of the riot carrier, the tactical driver shrieked. The night-vision optics mounted across the front windshield overloaded with an electric whine, their digital gain-stages blowing out into solid sheets of dead emerald green.

The carrier’s turret fired—blind.

A jet of high-pressure water shot into the sky, but with the crew blinded and the external telemetry cameras washed into white noise, the water column blasted harmlessly into the rock embankment fifty yards to the east. Thousands of gallons of freezing spray exploded against the granite wall, falling back onto the tracks as a harmless shower of slush.

The armored carrier lurched violently. Deprived of sight and disoriented by the terrifying brilliance reflecting off the snow-covered valley walls, the driver slammed on the pneumatic emergency brakes. The flanged guide-wheels locked instantly against the iron rails, sending a shower of orange sparks forty feet into the white glare, screaming in a high-pitched, metallic agony that echoed off the cliffs of the gorge.

The carrier shuddered to a dead halt twenty paces short of the turnout, its engine stalling out as the high-pressure water pumps choked on air.

Matteo stood on the narrow platform, his arm trembling where the heavy current had warmed the reamer until the tool was hot enough to scorch the leather of his mitt. The air around the searchlight housings hummed with heat, melting the ice on the catwalk and sending trickles of warm water dripping down through the steel mesh.

Beside him, Lukas slowly lowered his forearm from his eyes, blinking through the tears brought on by the peripheral flash.

"They're down," Lukas breathed, his voice barely audible over the crackle of cooling metal. "They stalled the pump. They can't see the hand in front of their faces."

In the cab below, an armored hatch cracked open three inches. A gloved hand emerged, waving a piece of white insulation foam toward the sky—not in ideological defeat, but in sheer, blinded biological panic, desperate to stop the light before the retinas of the crew were permanently seared.

Matteo didn't cheer. He didn't smile. He let out a long, shuddering breath that rose like a pillar of smoke into the blazing white corridor. His knee was throbbing with a dull, sickening heat, and the reamer was stuck to the palm of his wet mitten where the canvas had melted into the grip.

Then, from the darkness behind them—far above the switchyard, up on the high catenary bridge of the international line that led into the dark mouth of the Mont Cenis tunnel—a sharp, clear whistle sounded. 

It wasn't the diesel horn of a mercenary carrier. 

It was the deep, three-tone brass whistle of a heavy alpine snowplow locomotive, moving down the summit track in the blind dark without running lights, coasting entirely on gravity toward the illuminated yard.
