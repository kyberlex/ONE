The roar of collapsing suction tore through the turbine hall like a ripping sheet of boiler plate.

Matteo didn’t wait for the court receiver’s radio call. He lunged across the grated deck, his arthritic right knee screaming as his boot struck the wet diamond tread. Silicosis rattled in his ribs—a dry, sandpaper rasp that caught in his throat—but twenty-six years in the repair sheds had wired his reflexes deeper than his lungs. 

"Dump the auxiliary needle!" he bellowed. His voice was raw gravel against the shrill, rising pitch of the dual-runner Pelton. 

The spinning wheel was starving. With the high blow-off flume venting the penstock’s hydrostatic head into the night sky six hundred meters above, the columns of pressurized water striking the curved bronze buckets had broken into violent, frothing voids. Cavitation. Air bubbles forming and imploding against the metal at sonic speeds, biting gouges out of alloy worth forty years of valley labor. The massive drive shaft vibrated with a sickening, shuddering flutter that rattled the glass right out of the high clerestory windows.

Lukas was already at the secondary bypass, his frost-numbed fingers slipping on the grease-caked spigot. "The pilot valve is jammed with river slush! It won't seat!"

"Out of the way!" Matteo shoved him aside with a heavy shoulder, pressing his split thumb against the iron rim. Pain flared sharp through the greasy canvas tape bound tight around the knuckle, but he ignored it. He leaned his entire two-hundred-pound frame into the handwheel, throwing his hips into the turn.

Metal screeched against metal. A plug of mountain ice yielded with a dull, wet *clunk*. 

The mechanical deflector dropped into the path of the jet. The screaming whine flattened instantly into a heavy, suffocating thud as hundreds of gallons of diverted water smashed harmlessly down into the granite discharge tailrace beneath their boots. The floor ceased its violent bucking. The Pelton settled, dropping from its frantic runaway shriek back to a low, rhythmic baritone.

Matteo leaned his cheekbone directly against the bearing housing, closing his eyes. His calloused fingertips had lost fine nerve sensitivity decades ago on the drill presses, but the thin skin of his temple never lied. The cast iron was warm, trembling with a steady three-hundred-revolution thrum. The runner hadn't cracked.

He pushed off the casing and spat a brown streak of phlegm onto the wet concrete. "Head pressure is cut in half. We’re drawing barely a trickle until Favre gets up the ice-wall to clear that vent."

Behind him, the heavy oak doors of the turbine hall slammed open against the wall.

Franco Bera marched in, dragging the alpine night with him. A veteran switch-fitter from the Bussoleno classification yard, Franco wore three wool coats layered under an oilcloth vest, his beard crusted with crystalline breath. Behind him crowded three junior machinists, their faces dark with grease and cold fury.

"Cut the feeder to the cantonal clinic," Franco barked, pointing a thick, mittened finger straight at the slate distribution board. "Cut it now, Rossi."

Giulia Bellini, stepping through the doorway from the switchyard with snow clinging to her dark woolen collar, froze. Her left hand was tucked tight into her coat pocket to hide its violent tremor, but her posture went rigid. "The clinic in Bussoleno has two infants in warming cradles and forty villagers packed into the recovery ward with second-degree frostbite. We don't cut their heat."

"The mountain switch points are locking up!" Franco roared back, his breath pluming white against the warehouse rafters. "It’s fourteen below out there and dropping! If the rail heaters on the eastern throat freeze over, the switch frogs pack with hardpack ice. The supply freight from Susa won’t make the curve. The whole valley loop derails before sunrise, and then nobody gets coal, nobody gets flour, and nobody gets out!"

"And if the clinic drops below forty volts, the oxygen compressors seize," Giulia said, her voice dropping into a deadly, controlled calm that masked the exhaustion graying her temples. "You’d freeze sick children to keep steel rails warm."

"Don't you dare stand there with your Turin law books and call me a butcher, girl!" Franco took two stomping strides toward the low-voltage busbars. "My boy is on the switch crew chipping ice with a crowbar right now! If that freight jumps the rail, it takes out the catenary masts. We lose the whole grid! We need twenty-four hundred kilowatts on the track heaters, or the rail dies!"

"We don't have twenty-four hundred," Matteo cut in, his voice cutting through the rising shouting match like a cold chisel. 

He didn't yell. Yelling in an engine room was for men who didn't understand the inertia of spinning iron. He stepped between Franco and the knife switches, his heavy boots planting him firmly over the cable trench.

"Penstock flow is choked," Matteo said, wiping moisture from his cheek with the back of his coat sleeve. "With the bypass half-cracked to keep the pipe from pulling a vacuum, the Pelton is giving us eighteen hundred, total. That’s it. That’s the water we have in the mountain."

"Then the trains take it all," Franco snarled, his hand reaching for the black Bakelite breaker handle. "Bussoleno can burn wood stoves for six hours!"

"Touch that breaker," Matteo murmured, "and I’ll take your wrist off with a spud wrench, Franco."

The younger machinists bristled, shifting their weight forward. Lukas stepped up beside Matteo, holding an iron valve lever across his chest like a sentry’s pike. The tension in the cavernous stone hall stretched until the only sound was the damp hiss of the tailrace beneath the floor.

"Stage One," Giulia's voice rang out, sharp and metallic.

She pulled a heavy, brass-cased mechanical stopwatch from her pocket—not an antique heirloom, but a battered, dual-dial shop timer salvaged from the Fiat Mirafiori stamping line. She set it on the iron workbench between them with a solid *clink*.

"Founders' Covenant, Article Four," Giulia stated, her eyes locking onto Franco's bloodshot gaze. "When allocation threatens essential life-support or regional transit integrity, screaming stops. Five minutes. Timed silence. Franco speaks for two and a half without interruption. Then Dr. Leone’s clinic liaison speaks for two and a half. Nobody touches a switch."

Franco’s jaw worked silently. He looked at the heavy brass timer, then at Matteo, whose cold gray eyes hadn't blinked. 

"The points are icing while we watch a clock," Franco spat, but he crossed his arms over his chest, his breath steaming. "Fine. Start the damn spring."

Giulia thumbed the plunger. The heavy escapement began its rhythmic, deliberate chatter: *tock-tock-tock-tock.*

For one hundred and fifty seconds, Franco laid out the brute mechanics of the valley line. He didn't use theory. He spoke of forty-ton hopper cars carrying pea-gravel and grain, the dead-weight tonnage that would rip through the frozen wooden ties if the Bussoleno curve wasn't kept clear of black ice, and the three hundred families cut off up-valley if the morning train failed. 

When the hand clicked past the half-mark, Chiara Leone stepped into the ring of lantern light, her white medical smock visible beneath a stained parka. Her voice didn't shake. She gave names. Old man Baridon, whose bronchial pump was running on a dying car battery. The newborn twins from Meana whose core temperatures were dropping by half a degree every twenty minutes.

When the timer gave its final, hollow chime, neither side moved.

"The Covenant doesn't allow a coin flip on life," Matteo said quietly, looking at the two red-faced groups. "And we don't have time for a full valley assembly. Article Eight. Emergency sortition panel. Three arbiters, drawn from the floor register. Now."

Lukas brought the cylindrical brass tumbler from the corner desk. Inside were forty small wooden slugs, each stamped with the shop-mark of an active maintenance worker on shift. Lukas turned the crank three times. The dry wooden rattle sounded like dice in a cup.

He slid the hatch open and tipped three slugs onto the oily bench.

"Pietro Vanni, track maintenance," Lukas read. "Elio Maretti, substation apprentice. Matteo Rossi, turbine watch."

Franco grunted, staring at the floor. "Two rail hands and a machinist. Fair enough."

Matteo looked down at his own slug. He had spent forty years believing that iron was simple: give it grease, keep it cool, respect the tolerances, and it wouldn't kill you. But iron didn't have to choose between a sickroom and a supply line.

"We don't cut either," Matteo said, looking up at the switchboard.

Franco scoffed. "You deaf, Rossi? You just said we only have eighteen hundred kilowatts. The track heaters take twenty-four hundred by themselves!"

"They take twenty-four hundred if you cook the rails continuously from here to Salbertrand like an idiot," Matteo countered, stepping toward the high-voltage panel. He tapped the copper bus ties. "The switches don't need continuous boiling heat. They need thermal cycling to break the bond of the ice before it glazes."

He pointed to the secondary bank of knife switches. "Eight hundred kilowatts to the Bussoleno line, hard-locked. No shedding, no drops. That keeps the hospital's central air loop running and the incubators warm."

"That leaves a thousand for the tracks," Franco muttered darkly. "It won't keep four switches clear, let alone twelve."

"It keeps three clear at a time," Matteo said. "We run a staggered duty cycle. Phase A gets power for eight minutes, breaks the crust, then we trip the interlock to Phase B, then Phase C. Lukas, you and Elio man the manual transfer board. Every eight minutes on the bell, you throw the ganged switches. We scrape the softened slush by hand with the track gang behind each cycle."

Franco stared at the knife switches, calculating the thermal inertia of seventy-pound rail steel. "Eight minutes is tight. If the wind picks up above forty knots, the steel will cool faster than the cycle."

"Then your boys swing shovels faster," Matteo said bluntly. "Do we have two votes to certify?"

Pietro, the trackman, nodded once, his face grime-streaked. "Aye."

"Aye," Matteo said.

Franco let out a long, shuddering exhale that looked like smoke. "Lukas, get your mittens on. If you miss an eight-minute bell, I’ll throw you in the tailrace myself."

Within three minutes, the powerhouse settled into a new, grinding cadence. The heavy knife switches dropped with loud, blue-white arcs that cast jagged shadows across the damp stone walls. Out in the frozen valley, the faint orange glow of the resistance heaters flickered along the track throats, while three miles away, the yellow incandescent lights of the Bussoleno clinic steadied, humming with clean alpine hydro.

Matteo walked back to the turbine pit, his knee throbbing with a dull, sickening ache. He leaned over the safety rail, watching the twin Pelton runners spin in their dark iron enclosure, slicing through the pressurized water with relentless, unforgiving precision. 

Then, high above on the mountain face, a sharp, yellow flare bloomed against the black crags of the Rocciamelone.

Matteo squinted through the frost-rimed glass of the upper door. 

It wasn't a distress rocket from Favre's climbing crew. It was a military-grade phosphorous illumination round, drifting slowly beneath a miniature silk parachute, casting long, skeletal shadows across the sheer rock wall of the upper reservoir.

Beneath its harsh, chemical glare, two dark, heavy-lift transport helicopters were descending out of the low cloud base, their skids skimming just yards above the frozen surface of the Cenisio intake flume.
